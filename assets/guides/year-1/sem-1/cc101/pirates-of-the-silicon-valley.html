CHAPTER 00
Overview & Study Priority Map

Pirates of Silicon Valley is a 1999 dramatized film about how Steve Jobs and Bill Gates each built Apple and Microsoft from the ground up in the 1970s-80s, both borrowing the graphical interface idea from Xerox, competing and even betraying each other along the way, until they eventually needed each other to survive decades later. It was assigned for CC101 as a fast, human way to understand the real origin story behind the personal computer industry — the people, decisions, and rivalries that shaped the tools you'll be studying all semester.

⚠️
Dramatization, Not a Documentary

The film compresses timelines and invents specific dialogue for drama. Treat the broad strokes — who did what, roughly when, and why it mattered — as accurate. Don't treat every line of dialogue or exact date as verified historical fact.

CHAPTER 01
Full Story Timeline

The events below follow the film roughly in the order they happen on screen. The film itself opens with the 1984 Super Bowl commercial shoot, then flashes back — this timeline follows the actual chronological order of events instead, which is usually easier to study from.

1971
Berkeley — Blue Boxes
Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak build and sell "blue boxes," illegal devices that hack the phone system for free long-distance calls. Both Jobs and his girlfriend are broke at this point.
1974
Harvard — Bill Gates & Paul Allen
Gates and Allen see a magazine ad for the Altair 8800, one of the first personal computer kits, and commit to writing a programming language for it before it's even finished. Gates forgets to prepare the loader program needed to actually run it on the real machine; Allen scrambles to fix it just before their demo.
1976
Homebrew Computer Club, Berkeley
Jobs and Wozniak meet fellow hobbyists building homemade computers. At this point, established companies like IBM don't know they exist. Investor Mike Markkula later offers to back them, pushing them from a hobby project into a real company: Apple.
1976
Albuquerque — First Microsoft Office
Gates and Allen relocate to Albuquerque, close to the Altair's manufacturer, and license their BASIC language to the company. This becomes Microsoft's very first office.
1977
San Francisco Computer Faire
A large crowd shows up specifically to see Jobs and Wozniak's machine. Gates and Allen also attend but are largely ignored, since Apple is the one drawing attention. Around this time, Jobs is also shown avoiding responsibility for his girlfriend's pregnancy.
~1980
Microsoft Grows, IBM Approaches
IBM decides to build its own personal computer to compete with Apple and needs an operating system. Microsoft offers to supply it, calling it DOS (Disk Operating System) — while Microsoft doesn't have a finished OS yet, they insist on keeping ownership rights so they can license it to other computer makers too, not just IBM.
1980
The Seattle Computer Products Deal
Allen buys an existing operating system from a small Seattle company for a relatively small amount. This purchase becomes the foundation of DOS, and ends up worth far more once IBM's deal goes through.
~1980
Jobs Meets His Daughter, Names Her Lisa
Jobs visits his newborn daughter, seemingly more concerned about how the situation affects his public image than about being a father. He eventually agrees to name her Lisa, then later names Apple's new computer project "Lisa" too, without publicly admitting the personal connection.
Early 1980s
The Xerox PARC Visit — the Turning Point
Jobs negotiates a visit to Xerox's Palo Alto Research Center and sees their graphical user interface (GUI) and computer mouse — technology Xerox itself never turned into a real product. He immediately grasps its potential and starts building it into what becomes the Macintosh.
Early 1980s
Microsoft Sees the Macintosh
Gates takes interest in what Apple is building and visits to see it firsthand. Jobs demonstrates the Macintosh's graphical interface to Microsoft's team, trusting them as a software partner — not realizing Gates already knows how useful the idea is, partly because he understands how much Jobs distrusts IBM and can use that to get closer to Apple.
Early-mid 1980s
Wozniak Leaves Apple
Increasingly unhappy with Jobs's abrasive, arrogant management style, Wozniak decides to quit Apple.
Mid 1980s
Windows Development & the Confrontation
While dating Ann, Gates continues developing Microsoft's own operating system, Windows. Jobs confronts him, accusing Microsoft of copying the Macintosh interface; Gates denies it and convinces Jobs it's different, even though it wasn't. Both eventually argue over the fact that they each got the underlying idea from the same place: Xerox.
Mid 1980s
Jobs's 30th Birthday, St. Francis Hotel, San Francisco
A large celebration marking Jobs's 30th birthday, held not long after the Macintosh launch and the growing internal tension at Apple.
1997
Microsoft Invests in Apple
Years later, Apple is struggling and Jobs is back at the company. Microsoft announces a surprise investment to keep Apple alive, with Gates appearing on a massive screen above a stunned, partly booing crowd — the two rivals who spent decades accusing each other of theft ending up needing one another to survive. Wozniak, by this point, has moved on to teaching computers to schoolchildren.
CHAPTER 02
Who's Who — Character Guide

All major characters are dramatized versions of real people. Knowing who's who is usually the easiest, highest-value thing to get right on an exam or recitation.

Steve Jobs
APPLE CO-FOUNDER

Intense, visionary, and difficult. Obsessed with design and "changing the world." Shown as brilliant but arrogant and harsh toward employees, and initially avoidant with his daughter.

Steve Wozniak ("Woz")
APPLE CO-FOUNDER

The actual engineer of the pair — built the first working Apple computer. More laid-back and people-focused than Jobs; eventually leaves Apple over Jobs's management style.

Bill Gates
MICROSOFT CO-FOUNDER

Calculating and business-minded. Plays the long game — most notably by keeping licensing rights to DOS instead of selling it outright to IBM.

Paul Allen
MICROSOFT CO-FOUNDER

Gates's childhood friend and early technical partner. Handles the last-minute fix that saves their first Altair demo, and later secures the OS purchase that becomes DOS.

Steve Ballmer
MICROSOFT EXECUTIVE

Gates's college friend, later Microsoft's aggressive second-in-command, pushing hard on shipping Windows.

John Sculley
APPLE CEO

Former Pepsi executive recruited by Jobs to run Apple. Eventually sides with Apple's board against Jobs, contributing to Jobs being pushed out of the company he co-founded.

CHAPTER 03
Key Concepts for CC101

These are the ideas from the film that connect most directly to the history of computing — the part CC101 actually cares about.

CONCEPT · 01
Xerox PARC & the GUI/Mouse

Xerox's research lab developed the graphical user interface and mouse first, but never commercialized it. Both the Macintosh and, indirectly, Windows trace back to that single demo — the film treats it as the shared origin point both companies borrowed from rather than invented.

CONCEPT · 02
The IBM–Microsoft DOS Deal

Microsoft's key business decision wasn't writing DOS — it was insisting on keeping the rights to license it to other computer makers, not just IBM. That single licensing decision is why Microsoft, not IBM, ended up dominating the industry long-term.

CONCEPT · 03
Hobbyist Origins

Both companies started from hobbyist, almost accidental beginnings — blue boxes and homemade computers for Apple, a magazine ad and an unfinished language for Microsoft — not from formal corporate research.

CONCEPT · 04
Rivalry & Interdependence

Jobs and Gates spent years competing and accusing each other of theft, yet by 1997 Microsoft's investment was what helped keep Apple alive — showing that competitors in an industry can still end up needing each other.

CHAPTER 04
Reflection Q&A

Common questions a professor might ask about this film, answered directly — useful for recitation or a written reflection paper.

1
What is the movie about?
It's about how Steve Jobs and Bill Gates each built Apple and Microsoft from nothing, and how their rivalry and even their mistakes shaped the entire personal computer industry we still use today.
2
What did you learn from the movie?
A lot of the technology we use now, like graphical interfaces and the mouse, didn't originally come from Apple or Microsoft but from Xerox — neither company invented it first, they just knew how to use it before Xerox did.
3
What values or traits helped Jobs and Gates succeed?
Both showed persistence, risk-taking, and the ability to convince other people to believe in their vision even before they had anything finished or proven.
4
What negative traits did the movie show in both men?
Jobs was shown as arrogant, harsh toward employees, and avoidant of personal responsibility, especially with his daughter at first. Gates was shown as calculating and willing to bend the truth to protect his business interests.
5
Why is the Xerox visit an important part of the story?
It's the turning point where both Apple and Microsoft got the idea for the graphical interface that became the foundation of modern computers, even though Xerox, the company that actually invented it, never took advantage of it themselves.
6
Why did Wozniak eventually leave Apple?
He no longer agreed with how Jobs was treating people and running the company, and wanted to move on to a simpler life — later teaching computers to schoolchildren.
7
What is the significance of Microsoft investing in Apple at the end?
It shows the irony that after years of competing and accusing each other of stealing ideas, both companies still ended up depending on each other — business rivalry doesn't always mean permanent enemies.
8
What can IT/CS students learn from this movie?
The industry we study today was built through real human decisions, mistakes, rivalries, and personal flaws, not just clean technical breakthroughs — and that innovation often comes from borrowing and improving on other people's ideas rather than pure original invention.
✅
Key Takeaways

Apple and Microsoft both started from hobbyist, almost accidental beginnings · The graphical interface both companies built on came from Xerox, not either of them · Microsoft's real advantage was the DOS licensing deal, not the software itself · Jobs and Gates were rivals who still ended up needing each other by the late 1990s.
