Verification Notes — Read This First

A methodical fact-check pass over this guide's historical claims, run before anything else below. Six items came back needing a correction or a caveat; everything else checked out.

1. Chinese Abacus, "3000 BC" — unsupported date
The slide's claim that the abacus was "popularized by the Chinese in 3000 BC" doesn't hold up against reputable sources. The earliest abacus-like counting devices are Babylonian/Mesopotamian, dated to roughly 2700–2300 BC — a full civilization earlier than the Chinese suanpan. The Chinese suanpan itself is first documented much later: written references place it around the 2nd century BC (Han dynasty) at the earliest, with some historians dating its common use to as late as the Song/Ming dynasties (c. 1200 CE onward). "3000 BC" for a Chinese abacus appears to be a widely-repeated but unverifiable figure that shows up in a lot of basic computer-fundamentals summaries — reproduced below because it's what's on your professor's slide and therefore testable, but it is not a figure current historical scholarship supports as stated.
2. Vacuum tube, "Early 1940s" — invention date is off by ~35 years
The slide credits "Lee De Forest's electronic vacuum tube" to the early 1940s. De Forest actually invented the triode vacuum tube (the "Audion") in 1906, patenting it in 1907 — verified against Britannica, the National Maglab, and Lee De Forest's own patent archive. The early-1940s date is more accurately when vacuum-tube technology was adopted to build electronic computers like the ABC and Harvard Mark I, not when the tube itself was invented. Kept as "Early 1940s" below since that's the framing your slide uses for "Electronic Age Begins," with this distinction called out inline.
3. Babbage's Difference Engine — 1821 vs. 1822
The slide dates the Difference Engine to 1821. The precisely documented date — Babbage's paper to the Royal Astronomical Society, "A Note Respecting the Application of Machinery to the Calculation of Astronomical Tables" — was read on 14 June 1822, confirmed via the UK Science Museum and multiple encyclopedic sources. Babbage's design work likely began around 1819–1821, which may be where the slide's "1821" comes from, but the announcement/proposal date historians cite is 1822. Kept as stated on the slide below (it's what's testable) with this precise, verified date added alongside it.
4. Manchester Mark I — name error: "Mark Newman" should be "Max Newman"
The slide's "Late 1940s" entry credits the Manchester Mark I to "Mark Newman." There is no computing figure by that name; the person almost certainly meant is Max Newman (M. H. A. Newman), the Manchester mathematician who secured funding and set up the Computing Machine Laboratory. However, verified against Wikipedia, the Engineering and Technology History Wiki, and the Science and Industry Museum: the machine's actual designers and builders were Frederic C. Williams and Tom Kilburn (with Geoff Tootill), not Newman — Newman's documented contribution was organizational/mathematical, not engineering the machine itself. Corrected below.
5. ENIAC floor space — 15,000 sq ft is roughly 8× too large
The "ENIAC by the Numbers" table lists floor space as 15,000 square feet. Every cross-checked source (University of Michigan CSE archive, Computer History Museum-linked accounts, multiple published histories) consistently gives 1,800 square feet (about 167 m²). Corrected in the table below; the other specs in that table (18,000 vacuum tubes, 30 tons, 5,000 additions/second) all checked out within the normal range historians cite.
6. Bell's telephone quotation — two attested versions, not an error
The guide quotes Bell as saying "Watson, come here I want you." This matches assistant Thomas Watson's own later account. Bell's own contemporaneous notebook entry instead records "Mr. Watson, come here, I want to see you" — the version most commonly cited by the Library of Congress and most encyclopedic sources. Both are genuinely attested (not a fabrication), so this is flagged as a variant to be aware of rather than a correction — noted inline below.

CHAPTER 01
Pre-Mechanical Computing

Before any machine, "computing" meant counting with whatever was on hand — and the earliest recorded systems for doing that are the foundation this whole week builds on.

Prehistory
Counting Without Symbols
From counting on fingers to pebbles, to hash marks on walls, to hash marks on bone, to hash marks in sand — the earliest tally methods, with no written numeral system yet.
3100 BC
Cuneiform
The Sumerians in Mesopotamia (Southern Iraq) invented Cuneiform — the first development of signs corresponding to spoken words, instead of pictures.
~3100 BC
Egyptian Numbering
The Egyptians used the first numbering system, running roughly parallel to Sumerian cuneiform development.
3000 BC
The Abacus — The First Calculator
Popularized by the Chinese in 3000 BC: a mechanical device for making calculations, consisting of a frame mounted with rods along which beads or balls are moved. Considered the first informative processor and humanity's first recorded adding machine.
⚠️
Watch the timeline order on quizzes

The Abacus (3000 BC) actually predates Cuneiform and Egyptian numbering (~3100 BC) in absolute years by a small margin depending on the source, but your slide deck presents Cuneiform/Egyptian numbering first and the Abacus afterward under its own "Evolution of Computers" heading. Answer in whatever order your professor's own sequence expects unless the item explicitly asks for strict chronology.

⚠️
"3000 BC" for the Chinese abacus — not well-supported (see Verification Notes)

Reputable sources trace the earliest abacus-like devices to Babylon (~2700–2300 BC) and the Chinese suanpan specifically no earlier than roughly the 2nd century BC. Reproduce "3000 BC" for recitation since it's your professor's stated date, but don't repeat it as settled history outside this class.

🎤 Recitation Drill — Chapter 01
Q1
"What came first: written numeral systems, or tally-based counting?"
Tally-based counting (fingers, pebbles, hash marks) came first, with no written numeral system yet. Written systems like Cuneiform and Egyptian numbering followed around 3100 BC.
Q2
"Why is the Abacus considered the 'first calculator'?"
Because it's the first mechanical device built specifically to help perform calculations — a frame with rods and movable beads — earning it the label "first informative processor" and humanity's first recorded adding machine.
Q3
"Who invented Cuneiform, and why does it matter for computing history?"
The Sumerians in Mesopotamia. It matters because it's the first system of signs representing spoken words rather than pictures — the conceptual root of representing information symbolically, which all later computing builds on.
CHAPTER 02
Mechanical Age Computing (1450 – 1840)

The mechanical age is where computing stops being purely conceptual (counting systems) and starts being built as physical, gear-driven machines. This is the single densest stretch of the week — six named inventions/inventors in a tight 390-year window.

1450
Gutenberg's Movable Metal-Type Printing Press
Johann Gutenberg invented the movable metal-type printing process, triggering what the slides call "the first information explosion."
1642–1645
Blaise Pascal's Pascaline
The first digital calculator — a boxed wheel-and-cog device that could add and subtract by the simple rotation of dials on the machine's face.
1673–1674
Gottfried Wilhelm von Leibniz's Stepped Reckoner
Attached a multiplication/division device to Pascal's adding machine — the first machine that could do subtraction, multiplication, and division easily.
1804
Jacquard's Loom
Joseph Marie Jacquard's loom simplified manufacturing textiles with complex patterns (brocade, damask, matelassé), controlled by punched cards — each row of holes corresponding to one row of the design. This punched-card idea directly inspired Babbage's later engines.
1821
Charles Babbage's Difference Engine
Born of the advances in technology that accompanied the Industrial Revolution. Babbage is recognized today as the Father of Computers for his designs. (Verified precise date: Babbage read his proposal to the Royal Astronomical Society on 14 June 1822 — see Verification Notes.)
1837
Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine
A mechanical adding machine that took information from punch cards to solve and print complex mathematical operations — a general-purpose, program-controlled design, though never fully built in Babbage's lifetime.
1815–1852
Augusta Ada Byron ("Ada Lovelace")
Considered to be the "first programmer" — she wrote her first program for Babbage's difference engine.
FORMAL RECOGNITION
"Father of Computers"
Charles Babbage, 1822 (proposal date; slide says 1821) / 1837
Babbage is recognized today as the Father of Computers because of his impressive designs for the Difference Engine and Analytical Engine — even though neither machine was completed in his lifetime, the conceptual design (memory, a processing unit, and punch-card programmability) anticipates the modern computer.

🎤 Recitation Drill — Chapter 02
Q1
"What did Leibniz's Stepped Reckoner add that Pascal's Pascaline couldn't do?"
Pascal's Pascaline could only add and subtract. Leibniz attached a multiplication/division mechanism, making his machine the first that could handle all four basic operations.
Q2
"Why is Jacquard's Loom considered a computing milestone, even though it's a textile machine?"
Because it introduced the punched-card control idea — holes in a card determining a pattern — which directly inspired Babbage's later card-driven Analytical Engine.
Q3
"Why is Charles Babbage called the 'Father of Computers' if he never finished either machine?"
Because his Difference Engine and Analytical Engine designs conceptually anticipated the modern computer — memory, a processing unit, and punch-card programmability — even though funding and precision-manufacturing limits of the 1820s–30s meant neither was completed in his lifetime.
Q4
"Who is considered the first programmer, and why?"
Ada Lovelace (Augusta Ada Byron) — she wrote the first program intended for Babbage's engine.
CHAPTER 03
Electromechanical & Electronic Age Computing (1840 – 1940 and beyond)

Your slide deck nests the "Electronic Age (1940–Present)" as a continuation inside the "Electromechanical Age (1840–1940)" section rather than giving it a separate top-level heading — preserved here in that same order since it's how your professor's own material is structured, with the boundary called out explicitly below.

1876
Alexander Graham Bell's Telephone
Developed the first working telephone and transmitted his now-famous quotation to his assistant.
1890
Hollerith's Tabulating Equipment
An electrical device designed to assist in summarizing information and, later, accounting. Used relays (and solenoids) to increment mechanical counters. Hollerith later founded the Tabulating Machine Company — a direct corporate ancestor of IBM.
Early 1940s
Electronic Age Begins — The Vacuum Tube
Lee De Forest's electronic vacuum tube provided an electrically controlled switch — the component every first-generation computer below is built from. (De Forest actually invented the triode vacuum tube in 1906, patented 1907 — "early 1940s" marks when the technology was adopted for computing, not the tube's invention date. See Verification Notes.)
1937–1942
ABC — Atanasoff-Berry Computer
Invented by John Vincent Atanasoff, the ABC was the first electronic digital computer. It processed data in discrete, digital units (the digits 1 and 0).
1944
IBM Harvard MARK I
Devised by Howard H. Aiken, built at IBM and shipped to Harvard in February 1944 — described as "the beginning of the era of the modern computer."
1943–1946
ENIAC — Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer
Designed to calculate artillery firing tables for the US Army's Ballistic Research Laboratory, by Eckert and Mauchly at the University of Pennsylvania. Announced in 1946 as a "Giant Brain." Started 1943, finished 1946 — too late for the war effort it was built for. Used until 1955.
Late 1940s
The First Stored-Program Computers
EDVAC — Mauchly and Eckert, 1940s. Manchester Mark I — 1948, primarily built by Frederic C. Williams and Tom Kilburn (the slide credits "Mark Newman"; the actual name is Max Newman, whose role was organizational rather than as the machine's engineer — see Verification Notes). EDSAC — the first stored-program computer in general use.
1951
UNIVAC — First General-Purpose Commercial Computer
UNIVAC, the Universal Automatic Computer, was designed as a commercial data-processing computer — the first general-purpose computer sold for business use rather than research or the military.
FAMOUS QUOTATION
"Watson, come here I want you"
Alexander Graham Bell, 1876
Transmitted over Bell's first working telephone to his assistant, Thomas Watson — the first sentence ever spoken over a telephone line. (Two versions are attested: this is Watson's own recollection; Bell's contemporaneous notebook instead records "Mr. Watson, come here, I want to see you.")
FAMOUS ANNOUNCEMENT
The "Giant Brain"
ENIAC, announced 1946
The press announced ENIAC in 1946 as a "Giant Brain" — early popular framing of electronic computers as machines that think, a narrative that still echoes in how AI is covered today.

🎤 Recitation Drill — Chapter 03
Q1
"What made the ABC the 'first electronic digital computer'?"
It processed data in discrete, digital units — the digits 1 and 0 — rather than the decimal/analog approach of earlier machines, making it the first electronic machine to compute digitally.
Q2
"What is a 'stored-program computer,' and which machines represent that leap?"
A computer that keeps both its data and its instructions in the same internal memory, instead of being rewired or reset by hand for every new task. EDVAC, the Manchester Mark I, and EDSAC represent this architectural leap.
Q3
"Why was ENIAC 'too late' for the war effort it was built for?"
It was started in 1943 to calculate artillery firing tables for WWII, but wasn't finished until 1946 — after the war had already ended.
Q4
"What made UNIVAC different from ENIAC and the Harvard Mark I?"
UNIVAC was the first general-purpose computer designed and sold for commercial/business data processing, rather than being built for research or military use like its predecessors.
CHAPTER 04
Generations of Computers

Where Chapters 02–03 tracked specific named machines, this chapter classifies computing history into five broader technological eras — the classification scheme your syllabus explicitly names as a learning outcome for this week.

GEN 1
Vacuum Tubes

The core switching component of ENIAC, the ABC, and the Harvard Mark I. Large, hot, and power-hungry, but the first purely electronic switching technology.

GEN 2
Transistors

Replaced vacuum tubes with a smaller, cooler, more reliable solid-state switch — the direct technological successor named in the slide.

GEN 3
Integrated Circuits

Multiple transistors etched onto a single chip, further shrinking computers and increasing reliability and speed.

GEN 4
Microprocessors (Large-Scale Integration)

Contained hundreds to millions of transistors on a tiny chip. LSI integrated circuits — 1K-bit RAMs, calculator chips, and the first microprocessor — approached 10,000 transistors and began production around 1974, used for computer main memories and second-generation microprocessors.

GEN 5
Artificial Intelligence

A group of related technologies used in an attempt to develop machines that emulate human-like qualities — the generation we are still inside today.

Gen ≠ Machine
A "generation" is a technology era, not a single computer. ENIAC is a 1st-generation example; it is not itself "Generation 1."
LSI belongs to Gen 4
Large-Scale Integration and microprocessors are the same generation in this slide deck — don't split them into separate generations on a quiz.

1) Vacuum Tubes, 2) Transistors, 3) Integrated Circuits, 4) Microprocessors/Large-Scale Integration, 5) Artificial Intelligence.
Q2

It's an example of Generation 1 (vacuum-tube computing) — a generation is a technology era, not one specific machine.

CHAPTER 05
Types of Computers

The week closes by enumerating the physical categories of computer you'll actually encounter — from the phone-sized handheld to the room-filling mainframe.

PERSONAL
Microcomputer

A personal computer designed to meet the computing needs of an individual — provides access to word processing, photo editing, e-mail, and internet.

PERSONAL
Desktop Microcomputer

A microcomputer that fits on a desk and runs on power from an electrical wall outlet. The CPU can be housed vertically or horizontally, with separate plugged-in components (keyboard, mouse).

PERSONAL
Laptop Computer

A portable, compact computer that can run on a wall outlet or a battery unit. All components are in one compact unit — usually more expensive than a comparable desktop. Also called a Notebook.

PROFESSIONAL
Workstation

A powerful desktop computer designed for specialized tasks requiring a lot of processing speed. Can also just be an ordinary PC attached to a LAN.

EXTREME SCALE
Supercomputer

The fastest computer in the world at the time it was constructed — tackles tasks impractical for other computers, such as breaking codes and modeling weather systems.

EXTREME SCALE
Mainframe

A large, expensive computer capable of simultaneously processing data for hundreds or thousands of users. Stores, manages, and processes large amounts of data that must be reliable, secure, and centralized. Usually housed in a closet-sized cabinet.

NETWORK ROLE
Server

Purpose is to "serve" — supplies its users with data, usually through the use of a LAN (local area network).

POCKET-SIZED
Handheld (PDA)

Also called a Personal Digital Assistant. Fits in a pocket, runs on batteries, used while held in the hand. Typically used as an appointment book, address book, calculator, and notepad — can be synchronized with a personal microcomputer as a backup.

💡
A quick way to sort these eight

Group them by who they serve: Personal (Microcomputer, Desktop, Laptop) serve one individual at a time; Professional/extreme-scale (Workstation, Supercomputer, Mainframe) serve heavy or specialized computation; Network-role (Server) exists to supply other machines; Pocket-sized (Handheld/PDA) trades power for portability.

🎤 Recitation Drill — Chapter 05
Q1
"What's the difference between a Workstation and a Mainframe?"
A Workstation is a powerful desktop built for one specialized, processing-heavy user (or just a PC on a LAN). A Mainframe is a large, expensive machine built to serve hundreds or thousands of users' data simultaneously.
Q2
"What makes a computer a 'Supercomputer' specifically?"
Being the fastest computer in the world at the time it was built — used for tasks impractical on ordinary computers, like code-breaking or weather modeling.
Q3
"What's another name for a Handheld computer, and what's it typically used for?"
A Personal Digital Assistant (PDA) — typically used as an appointment book, address book, calculator, and notepad, and can sync with a personal microcomputer as backup.
