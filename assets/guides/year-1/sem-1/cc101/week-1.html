CHAPTER 01
Job vs. Profession & IT Roles

Everything else in this lesson depends on this one distinction. If you get called on for nothing else this week, get called on for this.

Job vs. Profession

NARROWER CONCEPT
Job

A job is just a specific task or position you're paid to do. It's narrow — it's the actual seat you sit in. A profession creates the space for a job to exist, not the other way around.

BROADER FIELD
Profession

A profession is the broad field itself — something you had to study for, with real educational qualifications and training behind it, like "Software Engineering" or "Nursing."

🎤
Recitation-Ready Answer

If asked "what's the difference between a job and a profession?" — say it like this: "A job is narrower — it's the specific position you hold. A profession is the broader field that had to be studied for and requires training. The profession creates the room for the job to exist." Example: "Software Developer" at a specific company is your job; "Software Engineering" as a whole — the degree, the skillset, the standards — is the profession behind it.

Traditional Professions in the IT Industry

These four are the "classic" roles the industry was already built around before newer specializations branched out.

Network Engineer
TRADITIONAL
The person who builds and keeps the network alive
Sets up computer networks, maintains them, and gives technical support when things break. Example: the IT staff who configured QCU's campus WiFi and the lab network in IK503/IL602.
Software Engineer
TRADITIONAL
Builds the software itself, start to finish
Develops the core concepts and code that exist across the entire software life cycle — not just writing code, but designing how it's built. Example: the engineers behind an app like GCash.
Quality Assurance Engineer
TRADITIONAL
Makes sure the software actually works before release
Monitors every phase of development to confirm the design and software match company standards. Example: the tester who clicks through a food-delivery app for bugs before it goes live.
IT Project Manager
TRADITIONAL
Leads the project, not the code
Responsible for leading a project from its inception all the way through execution — timelines, people, and deliverables. Example: the person coordinating a bank's new mobile-app rollout.

Emerging & Developing IT Careers

These are newer specializations the industry has introduced as technology and user expectations evolved.

Electronic Engineer
EMERGING
Designs the physical device
Creates, designs, and develops everyday devices — mobile phones, portable music devices, computers. Example: the engineers who designed the circuitry inside your Infinix phone.
UI Designer
EMERGING
Concerned with how you navigate the product
Primarily focused on how a user moves through a digital product — the buttons, layout, and visuals. Example: whoever decided where the "Checkout" button sits in the Shopee app.
UX Designer
EMERGING
Concerned with how the product feels to use
Responsible for creating an optimal overall experience when a user interacts with a digital or physical product. Example: the person who tested whether Shopee's checkout flow is actually easy or frustrating.
Business Analyst
EMERGING
Bridges IT and the business side
Bridges the gap between IT and the business using data to assess processes, determine requirements, and deliver data-driven recommendations to executives. Example: the analyst who figures out why a bank's loan-approval app is losing customers.
Software/QA Architect
EMERGING
Senior-level lead over the whole QA team
A senior position leading the QA team — driving test methods, risk analysis, resource planning, and estimating for client projects. Example: the senior tester who decides how an entire company tests every product it ships.
💡
UI vs. UX — the pairing that trips people up

Recitation-ready line: "UI is about how it looks and how you navigate it — the interface. UX is about how it feels to use — the overall experience." Same app, two different jobs: UI picked the colors and button placement; UX made sure the whole journey (browsing → cart → payment) doesn't frustrate you.

Top Fastest-Growing IT Positions

Per the lecture, these are among the fastest-growing positions industry-wide:

  • Computer software developers — build the applications people use daily
  • Computer support specialists — the frontline help-desk / troubleshooting role
  • Network and computer systems administrators — keep an organization's systems running
  • Network systems and data communications analysts — study and optimize how data moves
  • Database administrators — manage and protect an organization's stored data
🎤
Recitation Drill — Chapter 01
Q1
"Differentiate a job from a profession."
A job is narrower — a specific paid position. A profession is the broader studied field that creates room for that job to exist. Example: "Web Developer" (job) sits inside "Software Engineering" (profession).
Q2
"Give one traditional and one emerging IT role."
Traditional: Network Engineer — sets up and maintains networks. Emerging: UX Designer — makes sure the whole product experience feels good to use.
Q3
"What's the difference between UI and UX?"
UI is the interface — how the user navigates it. UX is the overall experience — how using it actually feels, end to end.
CHAPTER 02
The 4 Categories of IT Careers

Instead of memorizing dozens of unrelated job titles, the lecture groups every IT career into four categories based on the main focus of the job. Learn the category first — the specific roles fall into place underneath it.

CATEGORY 1
Sales and Support Careers
People-facing roles: installing, fixing, explaining, teaching, and documenting technology for other people.
CATEGORY 2
Software Development Careers
Building the software itself — from low-level system code to full commercial products.
CATEGORY 3
Application Development Careers
Adapting and running software for a specific organization — the "make it work for this company" roles.
CATEGORY 4
Hardware Design Careers
Designing the physical components — CPUs, video cards, memory — usually by electrical or computer engineers.

1. Sales and Support Careers

The common thread: every role here exists to serve a person, not just a machine.

RoleSimple Definition (say it like this)Example
Technical servicingInstalling and maintaining hardware and software.A technician fixing a printer or setting up a new office PC.
Technical supportResponding to phone/email requests for help from customers.A Globe or PLDT hotline agent walking you through a router reset.
Technical salesEducating potential customers about a product so they want to buy it.A sales rep at PC Express explaining laptop specs to a buyer.
Technical trainingTeaching individuals how to use a system.An in-house trainer teaching office staff how to use MS Excel or a new POS system.
Technical writingTurning an engineer's technical knowledge into written documentation.Whoever wrote the user manual that came with your router.

2. Software Development Careers

The common thread: creating the actual software product, at varying levels of depth.

RoleSimple Definition (say it like this)Example
Software developmentCreating new software products meant for commercial sale.A team building a mobile banking app from scratch.
Systems programmingHighly specialized coding — writing low-level instructions (e.g. in C++) to accomplish a task.Writing the driver software that lets Windows talk to your printer.
Application programmingNeeds similar skills to systems programming, but focused on end-user apps.Building the grading system a school uses internally.
Multimedia developmentUsing computers to create images, sounds, and movies.The animation/graphics team behind a mobile game's cutscenes.
Quality assurance (QA)Testing new products before they're released to the public.Testers running a food-delivery app through dozens of scenarios before launch.

3. Application Development Careers

The common thread: taking commercial software and making it work for one specific organization.

RoleSimple Definition (say it like this)Example
Application developmentUses commercial software to build a system for a specific organization.Customizing an ERP platform for a local retail chain.
Systems analysisGathering requirements and designing a system before it's built.Interviewing a hospital's staff to figure out what a new records system needs to do.
Database administrationManaging the database — the core of a company's information systems.Managing a bank's customer-account database.
Network administrationOperating and maintaining a company's network.Keeping an office's internal LAN and WiFi running day to day.
Web administrationKeeping a website working and up to date.Making sure an online shop's site stays online and updated.

4. Hardware Design Careers

The common thread: the physical side of computing, closer to engineering than programming.

RoleSimple Definition (say it like this)Example
Hardware designSpecifying computer hardware — CPUs, video cards, memory chips. Many hardware designers are electrical engineers or computer engineers, not IT graduates.The engineers who design a new graphics card's chipset.

Recitation Drill — Chapter 02
Q1
"Name the four categories of IT careers."
Sales and Support, Software Development, Application Development, and Hardware Design — grouped by the main focus of the job.
Q2
"What's the difference between software development and application development?"
Software development builds a commercial product from scratch to sell. Application development uses existing commercial software and customizes it to fit one specific organization.
Q3
"Where does database administration belong, and why?"
Under Application Development Careers — because the database sits at the core of a company's information systems, and admins keep that specific organization's data running.
CHAPTER 03
Computers Across Industries

The last part of the lesson zooms out from "IT jobs" to "where computers show up in every other industry" — proof that IT isn't a silo, it's the backbone underneath almost everything else.

Education
INDUSTRY
Computers aid teaching and learning
Used for lectures, research, and computer-aided instruction. Example: a QCU computer lab session or an online LMS.
Health & Medicine
INDUSTRY
Computers support diagnosis and treatment
Used in imaging, monitoring, and hospital records. Example: the CT-scan monitors and displays used during a scan.
Financial Institutions
INDUSTRY
Computers move and track money
Used for online banking, transfers, and account management. Example: checking your balance and paying bills through a bank's app.
Pastime / Entertainment
INDUSTRY
Computers power how we relax
Used for gaming, streaming, and leisure. Example: playing an online multiplayer game with a headset on.
Transport System
INDUSTRY
Computers run the vehicles and the routes
Used in train control systems and vehicle dashboards. Example: the onboard computer/control panel of an MRT/LRT train.
Business & Corporate
INDUSTRY
Computers are now unavoidable in business
Used for meetings, reporting, and decision-making. Example: a team reviewing sales numbers on a laptop in a client meeting.
E-Commerce
INDUSTRY
Computers make buying and selling online possible
Used for online ordering and delivery. Example: ordering food delivery from your phone in bed.
Defense
INDUSTRY
Computers support monitoring and control systems
Used in control rooms with real-time monitoring displays. Example: a defense control room tracking a launch on multiple screens.
Design
INDUSTRY
Computers are today's design tool
Used to create and edit visual work digitally. Example: a designer editing fashion illustrations in Photoshop.
🎤
Recitation Drill — Chapter 03
Q1
"Give three industries that rely on computers and one example each."
Health & Medicine — CT-scan imaging systems. Financial Institutions — online banking apps. E-Commerce — online food delivery ordering.
Q2
"Why does the lecture cover industries outside of 'IT jobs'?"
To show that computing isn't just its own industry — it's embedded inside nearly every other industry, which is exactly why IT graduates have career options far beyond software companies.

Glossary & Abbreviations

Every abbreviation used across this week's material, spelled out in full.

ACRONYM
IT
Information Technology — the industry and degree this entire lesson is mapping out.
ACRONYM
UI
User Interface — how a user navigates a product; the buttons, layout, and visuals a UI Designer owns.
ACRONYM
UX
User Experience — how a product feels to use end-to-end; the overall journey a UX Designer owns.
ACRONYM
QA
Quality Assurance — testing new products before public release; also a full career track (QA Engineer, Software/QA Architect).
ACRONYM
ERP
Enterprise Resource Planning — the kind of commercial software platform an Application Development role might customize for a specific organization (e.g. a retail chain).
ACRONYM
LAN
Local Area Network — the network a Workstation or office's internal systems run on; kept alive and maintained by network administration roles.
ACRONYM
POS
Point of Sale — the kind of system referenced in the Technical Training example (an in-house trainer teaching staff a new POS system).
ACRONYM
MRT / LRT
Metro Rail Transit / Light Rail Transit — the Manila rail systems used as the Transport System industry example (onboard computers/control panels).
ACRONYM
CT (scan)
Computed Tomography — the imaging technology behind the CT-scan monitors used as the Health & Medicine industry example.
