Overview & Study Priority Map

This reviewer consolidates HUM2 Modules 1–5 into one midterm guide. The recurring question across all five modules is: what makes an action right or wrong, who can be held accountable for it, and how do we judge it?

CHAPTER 01 · MODULE 1
Basic Concepts of Ethics

Vocabulary and classification: what ethics is, how it differs from morals, etiquette, aesthetics and technique, and the four areas it divides into.

Etymology and Definitions

Greek
Ethos

"Characteristic way of acting." Root of the word ethics.

Latin
Mos / Mores

Tradition or custom — the Latin equivalent of ethos. Root of the word moral.

Greek
Philosophy

philo (love) + sophia (wisdom) = "love of wisdom." Study of the most basic and profound matters of human existence. "It begins with wondering" — Plato.

Definitions
Ethics

Professor's one-liner: "the wrongness and correctness of our actions." Fuller form: the branch of philosophy that studies principles of right and wrong conduct.

🌳
Philosophy vs. Ethics

Philosophy is the whole tree; ethics is one branch (alongside metaphysics, epistemology, and so on). "Does free will exist?" is general philosophy; "Is it right to lie to protect someone?" is ethics.

Ethics vs. Morals

Morals / MoralityEthics
NatureSpecific beliefs or attitudes people hold; acts people performThe discipline of studying and understanding ideal human behavior and ideal ways of thinking
LevelPersonal conduct — the lived thingSystematic study — the study of the thing
Example"I personally believe cheating is wrong."Philosophers compare why cheating is wrong under different theories.
✅
Three phrasings of "morality" you may meet

M1: specific beliefs/attitudes of people. M2 quiz: "principles and standards used to determine what is right or wrong and what people ought to do." Long Quiz: "standards of right and wrong behavior." They are compatible — pick the one that matches the question's wording.

The Four Main Areas of Ethics

AREA 1
Descriptive Ethics
Empirical research into the attitudes of individuals or groups. Uncovers what people actually believe about values, right/wrong actions, and virtuous characteristics.
Example: surveying employees on whether they feel pressure to compromise principles.
AREA 2
Normative Ethics
Concerned with criteria of what is morally right and wrong. Central question: how are basic moral standards arrived at and justified? Two broad categories: deontological and teleological / consequentialist.
Example: "Pain is intrinsically bad — we ought not cause it without good reason."
AREA 3
Metaethics
Seeks to understand the nature of ethical properties, principles, judgments and attitudes. Asks: What is morality? What is goodness? How do we identify good or bad?
Sits "one level above the game."
AREA 4
Applied Ethics
Ethical questions within a professional, disciplinary or practical field: medical ethics, bioethics, business ethics, legal ethics, environmental ethics, and others.
Example: "Is it permissible to eat meat?" (Animal Ethics)
🔗
Is vs. ought

Descriptive = is (what people believe). Normative = ought (what should be believed or done). Normative gives rules ("don't lie"); metaethics asks what "right" even means. The deontological/teleological split foreshadows Part 2 theories (Deontology; Utilitarianism).

Issue, Judgment, Decision, Dilemma

TermDefinitionExamples
IssueControversial, and people keep on debating about itCigarette smoking; anti-terror bill
JudgmentAssessment of the actions or behavior of someone"Cigarette smoking is dangerous to your health"; "Humility is a virtue"; a friend steals and I judge it wrong
DecisionChoice of what to perform or doI choose not to take something I did not pay for; I decided to take a vacation
DilemmaA difficult choice between two or more alternatives, especially equally undesirable onesShould Michael reveal to Roger that Phyllis is married? Do the dishes or clean the bathroom?
Issue vs. dilemma
An issue is an ongoing public debate. A dilemma is one person's hard choice, right now.
Judgment vs. decision
Judgment evaluates (often someone else's) action. Decision is choosing your own course.

Ethics and Reasoning (bridge to Part 2)

The reasons we give for the things we do: rewards and punishment, principles, and ethical frameworks or theories. Rewards/punishment foreshadows consequence-based reasoning; principles foreshadow duty-based reasoning; frameworks lead into Part 2.

CHAPTER 02 · MODULE 2
Morality and Other Normative Systems

What makes something moral as opposed to legal, religiously required, culturally normal, personally preferred, or self-serving?

🧭
Mental map: five candidates, five problems

The module tests whether morality can be reduced to something else. Each candidate is eliminated as a complete account, then Socrates gives the positive answer.

1
Law
Morality vs. Law
Law only says what to avoid; what is legal is not necessarily moral.
2
Religion
Euthyphro
Is it holy because the gods love it, or loved because it is holy?
3
Culture
Cultural Relativism
No universal truth — but then no culture can be criticized.
4
Individual
Moral Subjectivism
"It is good if I say so" — but what is my basis?
5
Self-interest
Egoism
Psychological (is) vs. Ethical (ought); counterexamples: generosity, charity, hospitality.
✅
Socrates' answer

Being ethical = doing justice to oneself: an internally ordered, reason-led soul (Republic, Book IV).

Morality and Culture: Cultural Relativism

RACHELS, JAMES (1941–2003) · ELEMENTS OF MORAL PHILOSOPHY
Cultural Relativism
7th ed., 2012
There is "no such thing as universal truth in ethics; there are only the various cultural codes, and nothing more." It challenges our belief in the objectivity and universality of moral truth.
Darius & the Callatians
Darius c.550–486 BCE
Persian king
Callatians ate the bodies of their deceased fathers out of respect; the Greeks cremated theirs. Shows opposite customs for the same value, with no outside standard to rank them.
Eskimo infanticide
Reported practice
Female infanticide
Males preferred as future providers in a hunting economy; nursing a girl would delay the mother's next chance at a son.
FGM (Excision)
29 African countries*
Case study
Partial/total removal of external female genitalia for non-medical reasons. Rationalizations: less promiscuity, fewer unwanted pregnancies, wives less likely to cheat; unexcised women seen as unclean/immature. *Figure per the slide; later UN data is higher.
APPEAL
Why relativism attracts
  1. Conforms to experience
  2. Provides a basis for valuation
  3. Promotes tolerance of other cultures
  4. Denies universal truth in ethics
CRITICISMS
Why it fails
  1. Cannot criticize other cultures (even FGM)
  2. Different moral codes — no right answers?
  3. What exactly is "my culture"?

Moral Subjectivism

The individual thinking person (the subject) is at the heart of all valuations.

Subjectivist claimCounter-argument
"No one can tell me what is right and wrong."We can be mistaken and corrected by others.
"No one knows my situation better than myself."Many human experiences are common; others may suggest something useful.
"I am entitled to my own opinion."Closed-mindedness rightly invites censure from critical thinkers.
"It is good if I say that it is good."What is my basis for saying it is good?
Relativism vs. subjectivism
Relativism makes the culture the standard; subjectivism makes the individual the standard.

Psychological Egoism vs. Ethical Egoism

DESCRIPTIVE · "IS"
Psychological Egoism
The ego or self has desires and interests, and all our actions are geared toward satisfying them. A claim about what people actually do.
NORMATIVE · "OUGHT"
Ethical Egoism
We should make our own ends and interests the single overriding concern. A claim about what people ought to do.

Counterexamples to pure self-interest: generosity (a child shares ice cream with a dog), charity (giving food to the needy), hospitality (a Papua New Guinea community welcoming a visitor).

✚
Essay 2 key idea (added)

Feeling good as a side effect of helping is different from feeling good being the only reason for helping. That gap shows the act is not automatically morally selfish, and the "is" claim does not prove the "ought" claim.

Plato's Ring of Gyges (Republic, Book II, 359c–360d)

GLAUCON'S THOUGHT EXPERIMENT
The Myth of Gyges
Plato, Republic, Book II
A shepherd, Gyges, finds a ring that makes him invisible. He seduces the queen, kills the king and takes the kingdom. The ring — invisibility and anonymity — is the only barrier between a just and an unjust person. People are just only because they lack the power to be unjust.

Glaucon's challenge: if we are honest, what we really care for is self-interest rather than justice or moral goodness.

Socrates' reply — Republic, Book IV

1
Reason
top
Highest part; in charge. Proper order = justice.
2
Will (spirit)
middle
Dutifully follows reason.
3
Appetite
base
Desires; kept below reason. If appetites rule, the result is injustice (no harmony).
✅
Does it make sense to be ethical? Yes.

Because to be ethical is to do justice to oneself — justice is an internal ordering one consciously strives for, independent of anyone watching. Name three parts of the soul (reason, will, appetite), even though the triangle diagrams label only two.

CHAPTER 03 · MODULE 3
Ethical Levels of Human Existence

Ethical responsibility radiates outward: Personal → Societal → Environmental. Sanctions (external) and conscience (internal) keep a person accountable at the Personal Level.

Personal Level — the Ethos of Man

Man is revealed through three traits:

1
Distinguishes good from evil
Able to tell good from evil, right from wrong, moral from immoral.
2
Feels obligated to do good
Feels within himself/herself an obligation to do what is good and avoid what is evil.
3
Feels accountable
Feels accountable for actions, expecting reward or punishment.
AGAPAY · ETHICS AND THE FILIPINO, P. 1
On man's moral sense
Cited in the lesson
Man is endowed by nature with a moral sense, is self-conscious of his dignity, and submits to the duty of doing good and avoiding wrong, conducting himself according to the "dictates of reason."

Sanctions and Penalties (external accountability)

  1. The penal laws of our country
  2. Death as punishment for sin (Bible, OT)
  3. Physical and mental diseases
  4. Human suffering
Sanction vs. conscience
A sanction is external (imposed on you). Conscience is internal (a judgment you make). Jail for theft vs. private guilt over the same theft.

Conscience

ETYMOLOGY · TILLICH (1886–1965)
Latin conscientia
"Trial of oneself," both in accusation and in defense.
FUNCTIONAL · PANIZO
Practical judgment of reason
An act of the practical judgment of reason deciding upon an individual action as good and to be performed, or evil and to be avoided.

Six Kinds of Conscience

KindMeaningExample / memory hook
Correct / TrueJudges what is good as good and what is evil as evilKnows taking another's property without consent is stealing
Erroneous / FalseJudges incorrectly: good as evil, evil as goodLesson's case: conscience that tells a husband to have a mistress
CertainSubjective assurance of the lawfulness/unlawfulness of an act; sure of the decisionFully sure cheating is wrong
DoubtfulUnable to form a definite judgment; must settle its doubts before actingUnsure whether to keep money found on the street
ScrupulousRigorous; extremely afraid of committing evil; wants incontrovertible proofsAgonizes over whether a joke was a lie
LaxRefuses to be bothered about good vs. evil; rushes on and is quick to justify itself"Everyone cheats anyway."

Causes of Erroneous Conscience (4)

  1. Mistake in inferential thinking (wrong conclusion from given principles)
  2. Ignorance of the law
  3. Ignorance of the fact and other circumstances modifying human actions
  4. Ignorance of future consequences, especially those dependent on the free will of others

Pasted reviewers list only three causes

They omit the fourth. The module guide lists four; use four. See Chapter 09.

CLOSING PRINCIPLE
Conscience and truth
Unattributed on the slide

Erroneous has made a (wrong) decision. Doubtful has not decided yet.

CHAPTER 04 · MODULE 4
The Moral Agent

Module 3 asked how accountability works. Module 4 asks who can be held accountable, what shapes them outside their choosing, and how their moral reasoning develops.

Moral Agency

DEFINITION · FROM THE LESSON
Moral Agency
Evangelista & Mabaquiao Jr., 2020, pp. 24–38
Moral agency is "the capacity to make moral decision based on the perception of right and wrong." A moral agent is a "being who is conscious of the concepts of right and wrong." There is no moral society without its agents — they are the implementors of moral concepts.
UMBRELLA TERM
Moral Person
Any human person morality applies to; a receiver and/or source of morally evaluable action. All moral persons have moral rights.
RECIPIENT ONLY
Moral Patient
Can be helped or harmed (normal human adults and infants are patients) but is not a source of moral action. Rights, no duties.
Example: an infant.
RECIPIENT + SOURCE
Moral Agent
Only human adults, or humans capable of informed decisions. Has rights AND duties; the only one who can be responsible and accountable.
Example: a student who submits original work and does not copy.
✅
Core principle

"To be a person is to be a bearer of rights." Moral rights are higher than legal rights and identical to human rights. Rights correlate with duties, so only moral agents can be held responsible and accountable.

Dr. Ramon C. Reyes — the Four Crosspoints

One's identity is a product of many forces and events that happened outside of one's choosing.

PHYSICAL
Physical events or material factors

Physical attributes, genes, nationality. Place of birth and living conditions. Example: a child born into poverty vs. wealth.

INTERPERSONAL
People around you

Parents, relatives, peers, classmates, workmates; upbringing and family values. Example: strict religious household vs. secular one.

SOCIETAL
Society's impact

Cultural beliefs, customs, societal practices; legal and social justice systems. Example: living under strict laws on personal freedoms.

HISTORICAL
Your past

History shapes who one is now and is beyond one's control. Example: a young soldier drafted into a war.

The Heinz Dilemma

Heinz's wife is dying. A druggist charges $2,000 for a drug that cost him $200 to make; Heinz can raise only about $1,000. The druggist refuses to lower the price or accept later payment, so Heinz breaks into the lab and steals it. Kohlberg scores the reasoning, not the choice.

StageShould steal because…Should NOT steal because…
1 ObedienceDrug only worth $200; Heinz offered to payHe'll go to prison, which makes him a bad person
2 Self-interestHappier if his wife is saved, even with a sentencePrison is an awful place
3 ConformityHis wife expects it; he wants to be a good husbandStealing is bad; he is not a criminal and tried everything legal
4 Law & orderWife benefits, but he should accept the punishment and repay the druggistThe law prohibits stealing; actions have consequences
5 Social contractEveryone has a right to choose life, regardless of the lawThe scientist has a right to fair compensation
6 Universal ethicsSaving a life outranks property rightsOthers may need the medicine just as much

Man as Moral Agent: Reason, Freedom, Responsibility

  • Accountability is a natural product of rationality = reason (intelligence) + free will (freedom).
  • Will (Ocampo, 2018): the power of the soul by which a human being is in control of his actions. To have control means it is possible to act otherwise or not act at all.
  • "Sapagkat ako'y tao lamang" ("because I am only human") names our limitations, but being human also carries the power and moral capacity to choose.
  • We should live moral lives because we are social beings.

CHAPTER 05 · MODULE 5
Utilitarianism, Part I

The first Part 2 theory: judge an action by its consequences. Two ideas anchor the module — the Principle of Utility (what to aim for) and the Hedonistic Calculus (how to measure it).

Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832)

Born
London, England
Child prodigy
Studied Latin at age three (per the lesson).
Education
Queen's College, Oxford
Law
Did not practice law; spent his life writing about how existing laws could be improved.
Goal
Quantify ethics
Like Newton
Make ethics quantitative as Sir Isaac Newton made science; happiness and the good must meet the scientific method.

Historical background

  • Industrial Revolution in England (second half of the 18th to the 19th century); squalid conditions as people moved from the self-sufficient countryside to industrial towns
  • Scientific advancement and the Enlightenment; American and French revolutions; exploration and colonization; social and religious reform; industrialization; new transportation
  • Contention: it is wrong for the masses to live in unhappiness while a minority is well off
🏛️
Roots

Hedonism of the ancient Cyrenaics (4th century BCE) and the Epicureans — Epicurus (341–270 BCE).

Utilitarianism and the Principle of Utility

BENTHAM · AN INTRODUCTION TO THE PRINCIPLES OF MORALS AND LEGISLATION (1789)
"Two sovereign masters"
Quoted in the lesson
Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. They alone point out what we ought to do and determine what we shall do.
THEORY
Utilitarianism
An ethical theory arguing for the goodness of pleasure and judging right behavior by the usefulness of an action's consequences. Actions are good insofar as they are directed toward the experience of the greatest number of people.
DECISION RULE
Principle of Utility
Rightness or wrongness is judged by an action's utility in producing pleasure (used interchangeably with happiness). The action producing the most happiness is the most moral: greatest happiness for the greatest number.

The Hedonistic Calculus

A way to calculate happiness by adding up the happiness and subtracting the pain, using seven criteria.

1
Intensity
how strong?
How strong is the pleasure/pain? Deep or superficial?
2
Duration
how long?
How long will it last? Temporary or permanent?
3
Certainty
how likely?
How likely is it to occur?
4
Propinquity
how soon?
How soon will it occur?
5
Fecundity
more pleasure?
How likely is the action to produce more pleasure?
6
Purity
mixed with pain?
Will the pleasure be mixed with pain?
7
Extent
how many?
How many people will be affected?
🧠
Memory hook

Order: Intensity, Duration, Certainty, Propinquity, Fecundity, Purity, Extent (I-D-C-P-F-P-E). Group them as strong, long, sure, soon (I-D-C-P), then multiplies, stays clean, reaches many (F-P-E).

⚠️
Extent is defined late in the slides

The deck says "7 criteria" but defines only six at first; Extent appears as a bare row label and is defined in the Group Activity. Know all seven.

UNIT OF PLEASURE
Hedons (+)
Positive; a standard unit of pleasure/happiness.
UNIT OF PAIN
Dolors (−)
Negative; a standard unit of pain.
1
Count who is affected
Identify how many people are affected negatively (dolors) and positively (hedons).
2
Assess intensity
How intensely each group is affected.
3
Repeat for alternatives
Do the same calculation for every available alternative.
4
Choose
Pick the action with the greatest overall utility = hedons minus dolors.

Limits and Criticisms

Quantification
Pleasure and preference are easier to quantify than happiness or ideals. Love and family resist counting while productivity and material goods get overemphasized. Danger: "If it can't be counted, it doesn't count."
Measuring happiness
People think money brings happiness, but it does so only in the short term and unevenly. If we do not know what makes us happy, how can we base moral decisions on it?
Intent vs. consequence
Fiery Cushman (Harvard): Jon intends to kill but misses; Matt intends only to scare but kills. Matt is usually punished more severely. Are we satisfied with this assessment of responsibility?
Human rights
Emphasis on consequences can disregard human rights, treating the results as greater than the life sacrificed. No moral justification for killing someone other than self-defense.

Two reductio arguments (same pattern)

BIG MAN IN THE CAVE
Dynamite argument
  1. If utilitarianism is true, the people in the cave should use dynamite to blow the Big Man out.
  2. They should not do that.
  3. Therefore utilitarianism is not true.
ORGAN HARVEST
Doctor and janitor
  1. If utilitarianism is true, the doctor's act of killing the janitor for his organs was morally right.
  2. It was not morally right.
  3. Therefore utilitarianism is not true.

Reusable Templates

FOR DILEMMAS
Apply Kohlberg
  1. State the choice the person makes.
  2. Identify the reason they give (punishment? reward? approval? law? rights? principle?).
  3. Name the stage and level using the keyword table.
  4. Say why it is not the neighbouring stage.
FOR CHOICES
Apply the Hedonistic Calculus
  1. List the alternatives and who is affected.
  2. Score each alternative on all seven criteria (intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent).
  3. Hedons minus dolors for each option.
  4. Choose the greatest net happiness for the greatest number; then state one objection (rights, quantification).

Module 1 Essays

M1-1 · A friend cheated and asks you to keep it secret because failing may cost their scholarship.
Thesis: cheating is wrong; keeping silent shares responsibility, but the compassion motive matters. Right/wrong: cheating violates honesty and fairness to classmates. Moral responsibility: the friend is the moral agent responsible; you become responsible for what you choose now. Decision-making: options — urge the friend to confess, report, stay silent — compare consequences for fairness, trust and the friend. Close with your choice and the principle behind it.
M1-2 · "An action can be legal but still morally wrong."
Agree. Law only tells us what to avoid; morality tells us what to do (M2). Example: overpricing basic needs where no price control applies, or refusing to help someone in danger when no law requires help. Add: the reverse can also hold (what is moral may be illegal under unjust laws — Stage 5/6). Tie to the Module 1 conclusion: guides exist, but reasoning is ours.
M1-3 · Student leader discovers fund misuse.
Principles: honesty, accountability, justice to members who contributed. Options: confront privately, report to the adviser/proper authority, ignore. Consequences: restored trust and correction vs. continued abuse and complicity. Sanction vs. conscience: external penalties exist, but your conscience must act first. Choose reporting through proper channels and justify it.
M1-4 · Two students return a wallet — one because it is right, one from fear. Equally moral?
Not equally moral. Same outward act, different motivation. First student: principle/intention (moral conviction, moral integrity — acts even if unseen; Stage 5–6 reasoning at best). Second: fear of punishment (Stage 1). Gyges link: remove the fear (invisibility) and the second might keep it. Counterpoint: a consequence-only view sees equal outcomes — concede, then argue intention matters (Cushman's brothers).
M1-5 · Social media and conflicting opinions about right and wrong.
Philosophy begins with wondering — ask why before sharing. Ethics separates moral questions from etiquette, aesthetics and technique. Popularity is descriptive ("what people believe"), not normative ("what is right"). Example 1: verify before reposting a harmful rumor (judgment → decision). Example 2: refusing to join online shaming even when the crowd approves (Stage 3 conformity vs. Stage 5–6 principle). Subjectivism ("my opinion") fails because we can be mistaken and corrected.

Module 2 Essays

M2-1 · Cultural Relativism: should we never criticize a cultural practice?
Position: difference between cultures does not mean criticism is never justified. Value: conforms to experience, gives a basis for valuation, promotes tolerance (Darius: Callatians vs. Greeks). Limits: cannot criticize other cultures even for harmful practices such as FGM; leaves no right answers when codes conflict; "my culture" is unclear. Resolve: respect differences but judge practices by standards that go beyond one culture, such as harm and human rights.
M2-2 · A student helps a classmate because it also feels good. Is that morally selfish?
Define both egoisms: psychological = people actually act from self-interest (descriptive); ethical = people ought to (normative). Feeling good is a side effect, not necessarily the sole motive; generosity, charity and hospitality are counterexamples to pure self-interest. Even if feeling good motivates the act, that does not prove ethical egoism (is does not entail ought), and the act is not "selfish" in the everyday moral sense. Distinguish claiming people do act from self-interest vs. claiming they ought to.

Module 4 Essays

M4-1 · What is a moral agent? How does individual responsibility build a just society?
Define moral agent/agency; contrast with moral patient. No moral society without its agents. Rights correlate with duties, so agents are accountable (reason + free will). A student who submits original work models honesty; many such choices build fairness, respect and responsibility.
M4-2 · How do family, education and government shape moral agents?
Map to crosspoints: family and teachers → Interpersonal; laws, customs, schools, government → Societal. These are forces outside one's choosing (Reyes), so they carry responsibility to model honesty and integrity, teach reasoning, and apply fair rules.
M4-3 · Can a society thrive without a moral foundation?
No moral society without its agents. If agents stop exercising rights and duties or reasoned freedom, trust and justice decay. Moral agents prevent decay by acting from principle (post-conventional) rather than mere fear or approval (Stages 1–3).
M4-4 · Moral agents in a globalized, digital world.
New dilemmas (online harm, misinformation) still call for reason + free will. Mature agents reason from principle and human dignity (Stages 5–6), not only from "what is allowed" (Stage 4) or "what is popular" (Stage 3).
M4-5 · Compare virtue ethics, utilitarianism and deontology.
Only utilitarianism is taught in these modules: right acts maximize happiness for the greatest number; agents calculate consequences. Virtue ethics (Aristotle) focuses on character; deontology (Kant, Categorical Imperative) focuses on duty regardless of outcome. Use these one-liners only; consult your professor's Part 2 lectures for depth. Implication for community: consequences vs. character vs. duty each protect different goods.

Module 5 Short Essays

M5-1 · Main principle of utilitarianism per Bentham?
The Principle of Utility: the action producing the greatest happiness for the greatest number is the most moral; pain and pleasure are the two sovereign masters.
M5-2 · How does the Hedonistic Calculus help moral decisions?
It converts happiness into countable hedons and dolors across seven criteria, compares all alternatives, and picks the option with the greatest net utility.
M5-3 · Why did Bentham want ethics quantitative like science?
Reason and science were explaining human behavior; he wanted ethics to meet the scientific method as Newton had done for physics, and to improve laws so the masses are not unhappy while a minority prospers.
M5-4 · Major criticisms?
Quantification limits (love, family); we do not know what makes us happy (money is short-term); disregard for human rights; counterexamples (Big Man in the Cave, Organ Harvest); intent vs. consequence (Cushman's brothers).
M5-5 · How does Organ Harvest challenge utilitarianism?
If utilitarianism were true, killing one healthy janitor to save five would be right. We judge that act wrong, so the theory must be false; it shows consequences alone can justify sacrificing an innocent person.
