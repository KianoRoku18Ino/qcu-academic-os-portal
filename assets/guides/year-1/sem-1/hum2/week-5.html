CHAPTER 00
Course Overview & Study Priority Map

This module introduces Utilitarianism through its founder, Jeremy Bentham: what the theory says, why Bentham built it the way he did, the tool he designed for applying it (the Hedonistic Calculus), and the classic objections raised against it.

🧭
Recurring Framework — The Principle of Utility + the Hedonistic Calculus (7 Criteria)

Two ideas anchor this entire module, and the lesson keeps coming back to both by name: (1) the Principle of Utility — an action's rightness is judged by whether it produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number; and (2) the Hedonistic (Felicific) Calculus — Bentham's proposed method for actually measuring that happiness, using seven criteria (Intensity, Duration, Certainty, Propinquity, Fecundity, Purity, Extent) to add up "hedons" and subtract "dolors." Chapters 02 through 05 all build directly on these two ideas — the Stakeholders example, the school-lunch example, the Group Activity, and the Rubric all explicitly apply the same 7-criteria calculus. Keep it in mind chapter to chapter rather than treating each one as a fresh start.

CHAPTER 01
Jeremy Bentham — Life and Historical Context

Brief Biography From the Lesson

  • Born in London, England — February 15, 1748 (precise date verified — see Ch. 00, Note 1); died June 6, 1832.
  • A child prodigy: read as a young toddler and studied Latin at age three (see Ch. 00, Note 2).
  • Studied law at Queen's College, Oxford, England.
  • Instead of practicing law, he spent his life looking for and writing about ways in which existing laws could be improved.

Historical Background

The lesson frames Bentham's era as shaped by three overlapping forces:

1
Economic, Political, and Ideological change
2
The Industrial Revolution in England
In the second half of the 18th to the 19th century.
3
A Revolution in Ethics

The lesson further situates this period through a broader list of concurrent developments: scientific advancement; revolutions (the American and French); exploration and colonization; social and religious reform; industrialization; and new modes of transportation.

Impacts to Bentham

  • Reason and science were used to explain human behavior.
  • His ethical system was an attempt to quantify happiness and the good so they would meet the conditions of the scientific method.

How Did the Theory Come About?

  • Utilitarianism has its origins in the hedonism of the ancient Cyrenaics (4th century BCE), and the Epicureans — Epicurus (341–270 BCE) (verified — see Ch. 00, Note 3).
  • Bentham lived in a time where many people lived in squalid conditions as a result of moving from the self-sufficient country into the new industrial towns, looking for a better life.

Bentham's Contention and Goal

  • He believed that it was wrong for the masses to live in unhappiness while the minority were well off.
  • He also wanted to make ethics quantitative, as Sir Isaac Newton had made science.

vs. Epicurus: Epicurus is an ancient source of the pleasure/pain-focused idea (hedonism) Bentham draws on; Bentham is the one who turned it into a formal ethical system with a "calculus."
Example from the lesson: trained in law at Oxford, but spent his life writing about how existing laws could be improved instead of practicing law.
KEY MOTIVATION
Quantifying Ethics "Like Newton Made Science"
Plain English: Bentham wanted a math-like method for measuring right and wrong, the same way Newton gave physics precise, measurable laws.
vs. the Hedonistic Calculus (Ch. 03): this goal is Bentham's motivation; the Hedonistic Calculus is the actual tool he built to try to achieve it.
Example from the lesson: his ethical system was "an attempt to quantify happiness and the good."

CHAPTER 02
Defining Utilitarianism
JEREMY BENTHAM · AN INTRODUCTION TO THE PRINCIPLES OF MORALS AND LEGISLATION (1789)
"Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters..."
Cited in the lesson as "Principles of Morals and Legislation" — full title and 1789 publication date verified, see Ch. 00, Note 4
"Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do. On the one hand, the standard of right and wrong, on the other, the chain of causes and effects, are fastened to their throne. They govern us in all we do, in all we say, in all we think: Every effort we can make to throw off our subjection, will serve but to demonstrate and confirm it. In words a man may pretend to abjure their empire: But, in reality, he will remain subject to it all the while."

Defining Utilitarianism

⚖️
Definition (as given in the lesson)

An ethical theory that argues for the goodness of pleasure and the determination of right behavior based on the usefulness of the action's consequences.

One's actions and behavior are good in as much as they are directed toward the experience of the greatest number of people.

The Fundamentals of Utilitarianism

All humans by nature seek to attain pleasure and avoid pain.

The Principle of Utility

  • The rightness or wrongness of an action is judged by its utility or usefulness to produce pleasure.
  • Because pleasure produces a feeling of happiness, it is used interchangeably.
  • The action that produces the most happiness is the most moral.
🦇
Illustration given in the lesson — "The best action is the one that leads to the greatest amount of happiness for the greatest number of people."

Shouldn't Batman kill the Joker? He doesn't kill. He's killed many people and will kill many more. He has no friends. He's hated." CHAPTER 03 · Applying the Recurring Framework
The Hedonistic Calculus

This is the idea of how to calculate happiness by adding up the happiness and subtracting the pain. This is done by using 7 different criteria (see Ch. 00, Note 6 — only 6 are defined on the very next slide; the 7th, Extent, isn't defined until the Group Activity later in this chapter).

The 7 Criteria From the Lesson

CRITERION 1
Intensity

How strong is the pleasure/pain? How deep or superficial is it?

CRITERION 2
Duration

How long will it last? Is it temporary or permanent?

CRITERION 3
Certainty

How likely is it to occur?

CRITERION 4
Propinquity

How soon will it occur?

CRITERION 5
Fecundity

How likely is the action to produce more pleasure?

CRITERION 6
Purity

Will the pleasure be mixed with pain?

7️⃣
Extent — the 7th criterion (not defined until the Group Activity, Ch. 05)

"Extent" appears as a bare row label in the Stakeholders table below with no definition attached, and is only spelled out later, in the Group Activity instructions: "Extent – How many people will be affected?" Included here for completeness since it belongs conceptually with the other six.

What Do We Calculate?

Hedons (positive) / dolors (negative) may be defined in terms of:

  • Pleasure
  • Happiness
  • Ideals
  • Preferences

How Do We Calculate?

  • How many people will be affected, negatively (dolors — a standard unit of pain) as well as positively (hedons — a standard unit of pleasure).
  • How intensely they will be affected.
  • Similar calculations for all available alternatives.
  • Choose the action that produces the greatest overall amount of utility (hedons minus dolors).

KEY TERM
Hedonistic Calculus
Plain English: Bentham's proposed method for scoring how much pleasure vs. pain an action produces, using 7 measurable criteria.
vs. Principle of Utility: the Calculus is the practical "how to calculate" tool for applying the Principle of Utility's general rule.
Example from the lesson: the Rural Life vs. Urban Life table — Rural scores 22, Urban scores 19, so Rural Life comes out as the "more moral" choice under this calculation.
KEY TERM
Hedons vs. Dolors
Plain English: hedons are your "points" for pleasure; dolors are your "points" for pain.
vs. each other: you add hedons and subtract dolors to get the net utility of an action.
Example from the lesson: "choose the action that produces the greatest overall amount of utility (hedons minus dolors)."

CHAPTER 04 · Testing the Recurring Framework
Limits and Criticisms of Utilitarianism

How Much Can We Quantify?

Pleasure and preference satisfaction are easier to quantify than happiness or ideals.

Can Everything Be Quantified?

  • Some would maintain that some of the most important things in life (love, family, etc.) cannot easily be quantified.
  • Other things (productivity, material goods) may get emphasized precisely because they are quantifiable.
⚠️
Danger

"If it can't be counted, it doesn't count."

Measuring Happiness

Most people think that earning lots of money will make them happy, so the best utilitarian choice is to ensure that everyone has a good job and prosperity. However, scientific studies show that money only brings happiness in the short term, and that it works better for some people than others. As human beings, then, we actually don't know how to make ourselves happy? So how can we trust ourselves to make moral decisions on this basis?

Objection 1 — The Cushman Case

🧠
Hypothetical story told by Harvard psychologist Fiery Cushman

When a man offends two volatile brothers with an insult, Jon wants to kill him; he shoots but misses. Matt, who intends only to scare the man but kills him by accident, will suffer a more severe penalty than his brother in most countries. Are you satisfied with this assessment of responsibility? Why or [cut off in the source — see Ch. 00, Note 9]?

Objection 2 — The Big Man in the Cave

1
If utilitarianism is true, the people in the cave should use the dynamite to blow the Big Man out of the cave opening.
2
The people in the cave should not use the dynamite to blow the Big Man out of the cave.
3
Therefore, utilitarianism is not true.

Illustrated in the lesson with the cover of David Edmonds's Would You Kill the Fat Man? (Princeton University Press, verified publication date October 23, 2013) — see Ch. 00, Note 5.

Objection 3 — The Organ Harvest

1
If utilitarianism is true, then the doctor's act of killing the janitor for his organs was morally right.
2
The doctor's act of killing the janitor for his organs was not morally right.
3
Therefore, utilitarianism is not true.

Objection 4 — Human Rights

With the consequences being the emphasis of utilitarianism, the problem that arises is the disregard for human rights, contending that the results are far greater than the life sacrificed. There is no moral justification for the killing of someone other than self defense.

Recitation-Ready Answer Added — Study Aid

KEY ARGUMENT FORM
The Big Man in the Cave / The Organ Harvest
Plain English: both arguments follow the same pattern — show that utilitarianism implies something clearly wrong, then conclude utilitarianism must be false.
vs. each other: the Cave case targets sacrificing one person to save a group; the Organ Harvest case targets killing one person to save several others via transplants — different scenarios, identical argument structure.
Example from the lesson: "If utilitarianism is true, then the doctor's act... was morally right. [It wasn't.] Therefore, utilitarianism is not true."
KEY OBJECTION
The Human Rights Objection
Plain English: utilitarianism can justify sacrificing one innocent person's rights if it produces enough total happiness — critics say that's exactly the problem.
vs. the Organ Harvest objection: Organ Harvest is a specific illustrative case; the Human Rights objection is the general principle behind why cases like it feel wrong.
Example from the lesson: "There is no moral justification for the killing of someone other than self defense."

Group Activity Instructions From the Lesson (headed "GROIP ACTIVITY" in the source)

Each group will act as a moral decision-making council.

1
Read the Scenario
Your group will receive or choose one moral dilemma related to utilitarian decision-making. Examples given: (a) a doctor can save five patients by using the organs of one healthy person; (b) a government considers cutting a forest to provide jobs for many families; (c) a student cheats on an exam but argues that no one gets hurt.
2
Analyze the Situation
Discuss the possible actions and their consequences. Apply Bentham's Hedonistic Calculus using all seven criteria (this is the slide where Extent — "how many people will be affected?" — is finally defined in the source; see Ch. 03 / Ch. 00 Note 6).
3
Decision-Making
Compute or qualitatively assess the hedons (pleasures) and dolors (pains). Choose the action that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. Justify your decision using utilitarian reasoning.
4
Prepare a Short Presentation
Present your moral dilemma, decision, and justification in 2–3 minutes. Each member must contribute to the explanation. Use creative formats (e.g., skit, role-play, or panel discussion) if desired.
