CHAPTER 00
Course Overview & Study Priority Map

This module (source file: "Moral Agent: The Role in Building a Moral Society") picks up where Module 3 left off. Module 3 mapped ethics onto three expanding levels (Personal → Societal → Environmental) and explained the Personal Level's two accountability mechanisms, Sanctions and Conscience. Module 4 asks the prior question those mechanisms assume an answer to: who, exactly, can be held morally accountable at all? It defines the moral agent, distinguishes agents from mere moral patients, introduces Dr. Ramon C. Reyes's four "crosspoints" that shape a person's identity outside their own choosing, walks through Lawrence Kohlberg's six stages of moral development (tested against the classic Heinz Dilemma), and closes by grounding accountability in rights, duties, reason, and freedom.

🧭
Recurring Framework — The Four Crosspoints

The lesson names one structure and returns to it across Chapter 02: identity is shaped by four "crosspoints" outside a person's choosing — Physical, Interpersonal, Societal, and Historical. Essay Question 2 in Chapter 07 ("how do family, education, and government shape individuals into moral agents") is really asking about this same framework from a different angle — keep the four names and their order in mind.

🧭
Recurring Framework — Kohlberg's Three Levels, Six Stages

The module's largest section (Chapter 03) organizes moral reasoning into three levels of two stages each: Preconventional (Stages 1–2) → Conventional (Stages 3–4) → Postconventional (Stages 5–6). Chapter 04's Heinz Dilemma then re-applies this exact same three-level structure to one concrete case — the two chapters are meant to be studied together, not separately.

CHAPTER 01
What Is a Moral Agent?

The lesson opens by anchoring moral agency in an everyday example before defining it formally (lesson opening slides).

Moral Agents in Practice

A moral society depends on individuals acting as moral agents. By making ethical decisions, influencing others, and upholding justice, moral agents shape a community where fairness, respect, and responsibility thrive — everyone has the potential to be a moral agent, contributing to a better and more just world.

🏫
Example — Students as Moral Agents

In education, teachers, students, and school administrators act as moral agents by promoting ethical behavior, integrity, and social responsibility, shaping a moral society by influencing future generations. Practicing Honesty: a student submits original work and does not copy from classmates during exams.

Defining Moral Agency

There will be no moral society without its agents — moral agents are the implementors of moral concepts.

DEFINITION — FROM THE LESSON
Moral Agency
as given in the lesson
Moral agency means "the capacity to make moral decision based on the perception of right and wrong." Therefore, a moral agent is "being who is conscious of the concepts of right and wrong."

Moral Agents vs. Moral Patients

While all moral persons are moral patients (recipients), only some are or can be moral agents. All human persons can be receivers of morally evaluable actions; but only some of them can be sources of such actions. Normal human adults and infants, being moral persons, are moral patients; but only human adults, or humans already capable of making informed decisions, can be moral agents.

EVANGELISTA & MABAQUIAO JR., 2020 — PP. 24–38
Rights Without Duties
Ethics: Theories and Applications. Mandaluyong City: Anvil Publishing, Inc.
Another way of saying this is that all moral persons have moral rights, but not all have moral duties as well.

Recitation-Ready Answers Added — Study Aid

KEY TERM
Moral Agent
Plain English: someone capable enough (informed, rational) to be a source of right/wrong actions, not just a receiver of them.
vs. Moral Patient: a patient can only be helped or harmed; an agent can also do the helping or harming, and answer for it.
Example: the honest student who chooses not to copy during an exam — a decision only a moral agent can be credited (or blamed) for making.
KEY TERM
Moral Patient
Plain English: a moral person who can be affected by moral actions but isn't (yet, or ever) a source of them.
vs. Moral Agent: has rights but not duties — a one-way relationship to morality instead of a two-way one.
Example: an infant — it would be wrong to harm one, but an infant cannot itself be held morally responsible for anything.
💬
Recitation Drill — Chapter 01
What does moral agency mean, in the lesson's own words?
"The capacity to make moral decision based on the perception of right and wrong" — a moral agent is "being who is conscious of the concepts of right and wrong."
Why is an infant a moral patient but not a moral agent?
An infant can be helped or harmed (so it has moral rights), but it isn't yet capable of making informed decisions, so it can't be held responsible for actions — it lacks moral duties.

CHAPTER 02 · Recurring Framework: The Four Crosspoints
Moral Agent and Its Context

Having defined the moral agent, the lesson turns to what shapes one's identity before any moral choice is ever made.

DR. RAMON C. REYES — CITED IN THE LESSON
Identity as a Product of Crosspoints
Dr. Ramon Castillo Reyes, Ateneo de Manila University philosophy professor ("Doc Reyes"), d. January 17, 2014 — biographical detail added, not in the original slide; see Chapter 09
One's identity, who one is or who I am, is a product of many forces and events that happened outside of one's choosing.

The Four Crosspoints

1
Physical
Physical events or material factors such as our physical attributes, genes, or nationality.
2
Interpersonal
How our parents, relatives, peers, classmates, or workmates made an impact on our personality and characteristics.
3
Societal
The impact of our society on us, including cultural beliefs, customs, and societal practices that we do not choose.
4
Historical
Your history shapes "who one is" right now — your history is your past, and it is beyond your control.

Crosspoint Examples From the Lesson From the Lesson

🌍
Physical — Place of Birth and Living Conditions

A person does not choose the country, city, or socioeconomic status they are born into. Example: a child born into poverty faces different ethical struggles than someone born into wealth, such as access to education and healthcare.

👨‍👩‍👧
Interpersonal — Birth and Upbringing (Family and Environment)

A person has no control over where, when, or in what circumstances they are born. Family values, cultural traditions, and early experiences influence moral perspectives. Example: a child raised in a strict religious household may develop different ethical views than someone raised in a more secular or liberal environment.

⚖️
Societal — Legal and Social Justice Systems

Individuals must follow the laws and justice systems of the society they are born into, even if they disagree with them. Example: someone living in a country with strict laws on personal freedoms may face ethical dilemmas about following or resisting those laws.

📜
Historical — Wars and Conflicts

People are born into or live through wars they did not start, yet they must navigate the moral challenges of survival, loyalty, and justice. Example: a young soldier drafted into a war must decide whether to follow orders or question the morality of war crimes.

Recitation-Ready Answers Added — Study Aid

CROSSPOINT 1
Physical
Plain English: the body and material circumstances you were born with or into.
vs. Interpersonal: Physical is about material/biological facts (genes, nationality); Interpersonal is about the people around you.
Example: being born into a poor household vs. a wealthy one.
CROSSPOINT 2
Interpersonal
Plain English: the people who raised and surrounded you shaped your values before you could choose your own.
vs. Societal: Interpersonal is your specific family/peers; Societal is the wider culture everyone in your country shares.
Example: a child's ethics shaped by a strict religious upbringing.
CROSSPOINT 3
Societal
Plain English: the laws, customs, and culture of the society you happen to live in.
vs. Historical: Societal is about the present-day rules around you; Historical is about the past events that came before you.
Example: having to live under a country's strict laws on personal freedom.
CROSSPOINT 4
Historical
Plain English: events from the past — including ones before you were born — that still shape who you are now.
vs. Physical: Physical is about your body/material facts; Historical is about your timeline and the events you were born into.
Example: a soldier drafted into a war he didn't start.

CHAPTER 03 · Recurring Framework: Three Levels, Six Stages
Kohlberg's Six Stages of Moral Development

The lesson's largest section reorganizes moral reasoning itself into a developmental sequence, grouped into three levels of two stages each.

PSYCHOLOGIST
Lawrence Kohlberg
American developmental psychologist. The lesson gives only "(1927–1987)." Verified: born October 25, 1927 (Bronxville, New York); died — most commonly cited as January 17, 1987, though a few otherwise-reliable sources give January 19, 1987. See Chapter 09.

The Six Stages, Level by Level From the Lesson

STAGE 1 · PRECONVENTIONAL
Obedience and Punishment Orientation

The child assumes powerful authorities hand down a fixed set of rules to be unquestioningly obeyed. "It's bad to steal" — because "you'll get punished" (Kohlberg, 1958b). Example: a child does not steal candy because they fear getting spanked.

STAGE 2 · PRECONVENTIONAL
Instrumental-Relativist

Children recognize there isn't just one right view; different people have different viewpoints, and each is free to pursue their own interests. Morality is based on personal rewards and benefits. Example: a child shares their toy because they expect a reward in return.

STAGE 3 · CONVENTIONAL
Good Interpersonal Relationships

Entering their teens, people see morality as more than deals — they should live up to family/community expectations and behave in "good" ways, with love, empathy, trust, and concern for others. Example: a teenager follows school rules to gain approval from teachers and friends.

STAGE 4 · CONVENTIONAL
Maintaining the Social Order

Concern broadens to society as a whole: obeying laws, respecting authority, and performing one's duties so the social order is maintained (Colby & Kohlberg, 1983, p. 27). Example: an employee doesn't steal from work because it's against the law, even if no one would find out.

STAGE 5 · POSTCONVENTIONAL
Social Contract and Individual Rights

People step back and ask "what makes for a good society?", evaluating existing societies against the rights and values a society ought to uphold — a "prior-to-society" perspective (Colby & Kohlberg, 1983, p. 22). Example: a citizen protests an unfair law because they believe it violates human rights.

STAGE 6 · POSTCONVENTIONAL
Universal Principles

Justice requires treating all parties impartially and respecting everyone's basic dignity — even against laws or society, guided by self-chosen ethical principles. Example: a person hides a refugee in their home, even though it's illegal, believing in the moral duty to protect human life.

Recitation-Ready Answers, Stage by Stage Added — Study Aid

STAGE 1
Obedience & Punishment
Plain English: right and wrong = whatever avoids getting punished.
vs. Stage 2: Stage 1 obeys out of fear; Stage 2 acts for personal gain, not just to dodge punishment.
Example: not stealing candy purely to avoid a spanking.
STAGE 2
Instrumental-Relativist / Self-Interest
Plain English: "what's in it for me?" morality — do good if it gets you something back.
vs. Stage 1: Stage 2 recognizes different viewpoints exist; Stage 1 still thinks there's one fixed rulebook.
Example: sharing a toy expecting praise or a toy in return.
STAGE 3
Good Interpersonal Relationships
Plain English: be "good" to keep relationships and win approval.
vs. Stage 4: Stage 3 cares about pleasing people close to you; Stage 4 cares about the rules of society at large.
Example: a teen follows school rules to stay in their teachers' and friends' good graces.
STAGE 4
Maintaining the Social Order
Plain English: follow the law and do your duty, because society needs order.
vs. Stage 5: Stage 4 asks "is it legal?"; Stage 5 asks "is the law itself just?"
Example: not stealing from work purely because it's illegal, not because anyone's watching.
STAGE 5
Social Contract & Individual Rights
Plain English: laws are only good if they actually serve a good, rights-respecting society — and can be changed if they don't.
vs. Stage 6: Stage 5 still works through democratic/legal processes; Stage 6 will act against them if justice demands it.
Example: protesting a law believed to violate human rights.
STAGE 6
Universal Principles
Plain English: self-chosen ethical principles come before any law or social approval.
vs. Stage 5: Stage 6 will break an unjust law directly, not just work to change it.
Example: hiding a refugee illegally, believing it's a moral duty to protect a human life.

CHAPTER 04 · Applying Kohlberg's Three Levels
The Heinz Dilemma

The classic case Kohlberg used to probe which stage of reasoning a person applies — the point isn't which choice someone makes, but the reasoning behind it.

💊
The Dilemma — From the Lesson

A woman was on her deathbed. There was one drug that doctors thought might save her — a form of radium a druggist in the same town had recently discovered. The drug was expensive to make, but the druggist charged ten times what it cost him: he paid $200 for the radium and charged $2,000 for a small dose. The woman's husband, Heinz, went to everyone he knew to borrow the money, but could only get about $1,000 — half of what it cost. He told the druggist his wife was dying and asked him to sell it cheaper or let him pay later. The druggist refused: "No, I discovered the drug and I'm going to make money from it." Desperate, Heinz broke into the man's laboratory to steal the drug for his wife.

Should Heinz have broken into the laboratory to steal the drug for his wife? Why or why not? (Source: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinz_dilemma, as linked in the lesson.)

💭
There's No Single "Correct" Answer

Kohlberg's method scores the reasoning behind a person's choice, not whether they said "steal" or "don't steal." The table below shows how each of the three levels can justify either choice.

Reasoning by Level and Stage From the Lesson

LevelStageHeinz should steal, because...Heinz should NOT steal, because...
Pre-ConventionalObedienceIt is only worth $200, not what the druggist wanted; Heinz had even offered to pay for it and wasn't stealing anything else.He will be put in prison, which will mean he is a bad person.
Pre-ConventionalSelf-interestHe will be much happier if he saves his wife, even if he has to serve a prison sentence.Prison is an awful place — he would more likely languish in a jail cell than mourn his wife's death.
ConventionalConformityHis wife expects it; he wants to be a good husband.Stealing is bad and he is not a criminal; he tried everything he could without breaking the law — you cannot blame him.
ConventionalLaw-and-orderHis wife will benefit, but he should also take the prescribed punishment for the crime, as well as paying the druggist what he is owed.Criminals cannot run around without regard for the law; actions have consequences, and the law prohibits stealing.
Post-ConventionalSocial contract orientationEveryone has a right to choose life, regardless of the law.The scientist has a right to fair compensation — even if his wife is sick, that doesn't make Heinz's actions right.
Post-ConventionalUniversal human ethicsSaving a human life is a more fundamental value than another person's property rights.Others may need the medicine just as badly, and their lives are equally significant.

Recitation-Ready Answer Added — Study Aid

CASE
The Heinz Dilemma
Plain English: a man must decide whether to steal an overpriced drug to save his dying wife.
Why it's used: the same "should he steal it?" question can be justified from every one of Kohlberg's six stages — it isolates how someone reasons, not what they decide.
Example use: comparing a Stage 1 answer ("he'll go to prison") against a Stage 6 answer ("a life outweighs property rights") for the same final choice.

CHAPTER 05
Rights, Duties, and Moral Agency

Heading from the lesson: "Moral Agent: Characteristics and Role in Building Moral Society."

EVANGELISTA & MABAQUIAO JR., 2020 — PP. 24–38
To Be a Person Is to Be a Bearer of Rights
Ethics: Theories and Applications. Mandaluyong City: Anvil Publishing, Inc.
"To be a person is to be a bearer of rights." When we speak of moral rights, we refer to the moral rights of humans that are over and above, or better yet, higher than their legal rights. Moral rights are identical to "human rights."

Rights correlate duties — therefore, to protect and promote another person's rights, there is a need for duties and responsibilities.

✅
Core Principle — From the Lesson

Every moral person has rights, but not all moral persons can do their duties. Moral agency means moral persons in duty — only moral agents have both rights and duties. Therefore, if a moral agent possesses duties, only a moral agent can be responsible and accountable for his/her actions.

Recitation-Ready Answers Added — Study Aid

KEY TERM
Moral Rights
Plain English: rights that sit above and beyond a person's legal rights — identical to "human rights."
vs. Legal Rights: legal rights come from a country's laws and can vary or be revoked; moral rights don't depend on any government granting them.
Example: the right to life is treated as a moral right regardless of what any single country's law says.
KEY TERM
Rights-Duties Correlation
Plain English: having a right implies someone else has a duty to respect it.
vs. Ch. 01's Moral Patient/Agent split: this is the same idea restated as a relationship — a moral patient's rights create duties for the moral agents around them.
Example: a citizen's right to a fair trial creates a duty on the state to actually provide one.
💬
Recitation Drill — Chapter 05
What does it mean to say moral rights are "identical to human rights"?
Moral rights aren't granted by any government — they belong to a person simply for being human, and rank higher than whatever a country's legal rights happen to say.
Why do only moral agents — not all moral persons — have duties?
Because duties require the capacity to be held responsible and accountable, which is exactly what separates a moral agent from a moral patient.
CHAPTER 06
Man the Moral Agent: Reason, Freedom, and Responsibility

Heading from the lesson, closing out the module's theory before its assessment section.

🧠
Accountability's Source — From the Lesson

Accountability is a natural product of our rationality, which consists of our reason (or intelligence) and free will (or freedom).

Being "Tao": Human Limitation and the Power of the Will

FROM THE LESSON — AS WRITTEN
"Sapagkat ako'y tao lamang"
reproduced exactly as given in the source slide
"Sapagkat ako'y tao lamang," may point out to human limitations but being human also possesses the power. Being "tao," does not simply point out our limitations, but it has also implications in the realm of morality.
🇵🇭
Added — Plain-English Reading

"Sapagkat ako'y tao lamang" is Filipino for "because I am only human." The source sentence's own English phrasing is a little garbled — the likely intended point: being merely "tao" (human) names our limitations, but it also names our power — the same limited condition that makes us fallible is what gives humans the moral capacity to choose at all.

The Will

OCAMPO, 2018 — P. 19
The Will as the Power of the Soul
Ethics Primer: A Young Person's Guide to Moral Reasoning. Quezon City: Vibal Group, Inc.
The will is the power of the soul that starts the dynamics of what it takes human beings to act…the will, broadly considered, may be understood simply as that power of the soul by which a human being is in control of his actions…To have control means that as a person acts, it is possible to act otherwise or not to act at all.

Recitation-Ready Answers Added — Study Aid

KEY TERM
Reason
Plain English: the intelligence that lets a person figure out right from wrong in the first place.
vs. Freedom: Reason is the "figuring out" half of rationality; Freedom (the will) is the "choosing to act on it" half.
Example: working out that lying to a friend is wrong, before ever deciding whether to actually lie.
KEY TERM
Freedom / The Will
Plain English: the power of the soul that lets a person control their own actions — including the power to act otherwise or not act at all.
vs. Reason: Freedom is what makes an action genuinely yours to be praised or blamed for, once Reason has judged it.
Example: choosing not to cheat, even when you could easily get away with it.
KEY TERM
Responsibility / Accountability
Plain English: being answerable for actions that came from your own reason and free will.
vs. Ch. 05's Rights-Duties Correlation: this is the "duties" half in practice — you can only be held accountable for what you were free and rational enough to choose.
Example: a moral agent facing consequences for a decision they made with full knowledge of right and wrong.
💬

CHAPTER 09
Source Verification & Discrepancy Notes

Everything below was checked against outside sources or against the lesson's own internal consistency. None of it changes what to recite — it's here so nothing catches you off guard.

1
Kohlberg's death date — two figures circulate

The lesson gives only "(1927 – 1987)." Verified birth date: October 25, 1927 (unanimous across sources). His death date is given as January 17, 1987 by Wikipedia and Encyclopaedia Britannica, but as January 19, 1987 by New World Encyclopedia and a peer-reviewed psychology profile (IJIP). Both fall within 1987, so the lesson's year-only claim isn't contradicted either way — just don't cite a specific day with more confidence than the sources themselves agree on.

2
The lesson's two Kohlberg age-bracket graphics disagree with each other

Slide with the "Kohlberg's Moral Stages" table gives Preconventional as "up to the Age of 9." The separate step-diagram slide gives Level One Preconventional as "younger than six," Level Two Conventional as "7 to 11 years," and Level Three Postconventional as "11 years on." These don't match. This exact pairing of two conflicting Kohlberg summary graphics circulates widely online — it isn't specific to this professor's material, but both are reproduced in Chapter 03 faithfully since both appear in the source. Treat the stage content as reliable; don't treat either age bracket as the single correct cutoff.

3
Stage 2 has two different names in the same lesson

Introduced as "Instrumental - Relativist" (Chapter 03's stage-by-stage section), but labeled "Self-Interest Orientation" in the CHOICES word bank and identification exercise (Chapter 07). Both refer to the same Kohlberg stage — this reads as the professor's own alternate shorthand for the identification exercise, not an error, but it's worth knowing so the two names aren't mistaken for two different stages.

4
A slide-footer citation has a one-word title mismatch

The footer on the "Four Crosspoints" slide reads "Ethics: Foundation of Moral Valuation: 102." The actual published book is titled "Ethics: Foundations of Moral Valuation" (plural "Foundations"), by Oscar G. Bulaong Jr., Mark Joseph T. Calano, Albert M. Lagliva, Michael Ner E. Mariano, and Jesus Deogracias Z. Principe (Quezon City: Rex Book Store, 2018). The book and its five authors are confirmed to exist; the specific page (102) couldn't be independently checked since only this slide excerpt was available.

5
Two citations verified as accurate, with no discrepancies

Both independently confirmed as real, correctly attributed published works: Evangelista, Francis Julius N. & Mabaquiao Jr., Napoleon M. (2020). Ethics: Theories and Applications. Mandaluyong City: Anvil Publishing, Inc. (ISBN 978-971-27-3575-2) — and Ocampo, Ma. Liza Ruth A. (2018). Ethics Primer: A Young Person's Guide to Moral Reasoning. Quezon City: Vibal Group, Inc.

Added biographical detail — Dr. Ramon C. Reyes

The lesson names him but gives no dates to check. Added for context: Ramon Castillo Reyes was a longtime Ateneo de Manila University philosophy professor known as "Doc Reyes," credited with introducing existential-phenomenological philosophy there; he died January 17, 2014, at age 79. This is supplementary identification, not a correction.
