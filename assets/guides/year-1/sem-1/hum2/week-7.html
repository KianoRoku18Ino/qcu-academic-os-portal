CHAPTER 02
What Is Deontology
⚖️
Definition

Deontology is an ethical theory that focuses on duty, rules, and moral obligations rather than consequences. It is often associated with Immanuel Kant, who emphasized that moral actions must be guided by universal principles.

Deontological ethics defines the moral rightness or wrongness of an act in terms of the intrinsic value of the act. According to this theory, the duty to perform an action (or refrain from doing it) is based on the nature of the act itself and not on its consequences.

The Term "Deontology"

From the Greek word deon, meaning "duty" or "obligation."

✅
Act vs. Consequence

act (good in itself) → consequence — the act itself is what carries moral approval; the consequence that follows is a separate matter.

CHAPTER 03
Basic Principles of Deontology
1
Duty-Based Ethics
Actions are morally required, permitted, or forbidden based on a set of rules or duties, regardless of the consequences.
2
Categorical Imperative
Kant's famous principle states that we should act only according to maxims that could be universal laws (i.e., rules that everyone could follow without contradiction).
3
Intrinsic Moral Value
People and actions have intrinsic worth; individuals should always be treated as ends in themselves, not merely as means to an end.
4
Moral Absolutism
Certain actions (like lying or killing) are inherently right or wrong, no matter the situation or outcomes.
5
Intentions Over Consequences
The morality of an action is determined by the intention behind it, rather than its outcomes.
6
Universalizability
If an action cannot be consistently applied to everyone, it is not morally acceptable.
7
Respect for Autonomy
Each individual has the right to make rational moral choices, and others should respect their autonomy.
CHAPTER 04
Immanuel Kant, Main Tenets & the Good Will

Immanuel Kant (1724 – 1804)

Main Tenets

  • The morality of an act is focused on the act itself regardless of the consequences.
  • There are actions which are good in themselves no matter what may follow.
  • Actions that by nature are good are to be the object of one's duty.

The Good Will

KANT, IMMANUEL
Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, trans. Mary Gregor, 1997. Cambridge, U.K.: Cambridge University Press, 3.
"A good will is not good because of what it effects or accomplishes, because of its fitness to attain some proposed end, but only because of its volition, that is, it is good in itself and, regarded for itself, is to be valued incomparably higher than all that could merely be brought about by it in favor of some inclination and indeed, if you will, of the sum of all inclinations."
CHAPTER 05
Duty

Duty — in Itself, Apart from Inclinations

Duty, in itself, stands apart from: preferences, emotions, likes, dislikes, bias/prejudice, and inclinations.

What Does Kant Mean?

  • Kant uses the term 'will' to refer to intention or motive.
  • For Kant, the only thing that is good in itself is what he calls a good will.
  • A person has a good will if he/she does something right or good in itself.

Duty — Related Terms

commitmentfaithfulnessdedicationfidelitydevotionself-givingallegiancedeterminationloyaltyconsistencyunconditionality

Pure Sense of Duty

  • One must perform a moral duty for its own sake.
  • Doing good should be constant. We can constantly do something if it is done under a pure sense of duty.

When Is an Act Morally Worthy?

✅
Answer

An action gains moral worth when it is done under a pure sense of duty.

CHAPTER 06
The Categorical Imperative

The Command to Do One's Duty

  • The command to do one's duty is otherwise known as the "categorical imperative."
  • It is categorical because it doesn't admit the conditions or qualifications.

Autonomy and Heteronomy

Autonomy

autos (self) and nomos (law) = self-law or self-legislating.

Heteronomy

heteros (other) and nomos (law) = other law.

Maxims

A maxim is the rule or principle on which one acts. For example, one might make it one's maxim to give at least as much to charity each year as one spends on eating out, or one might make it one's maxim only to do what will benefit some member of one's family.

Source cited in the lesson: https://www.csus.edu/indiv/g/gaskilld/ethics/kantian%20ethics.htm

Where Does the Command Come From?

It comes from oneself. As an attribute of our rational nature, there are things in life that we have to do and not do out of our own initiative under the light of our own reason. We do not depend on others' commands anymore, unlike during the times when we were still young. This is what Kant calls autonomy of the will.

KANT, IMMANUEL
What is autonomy of the will? — Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, trans. Mary Gregor, 1997. Cambridge, U.K.: Cambridge University Press, 47.
"Autonomy of the will is the property of the will by which it is a law to itself (independently of any property of the objects of volition)."

How Do We Evaluate Our Motivations for Action?

How do we know that what we are doing is in the right way? Given our weaknesses and limitations, is there a way to create a law that can be morally justified? Here, Kant offers his formula or moral criterion known as the categorical imperative.

What Is an Imperative?

An imperative is a command. So, "Pay your taxes!" is an imperative, as are "Stop kicking me!" and "Don't kill animals!"

Hypothetical Imperatives

These imperatives command conditionally on having a relevant desire. E.g., "If you want to go to medical school, study biology in college." If you don't want to go to medical school, this command doesn't apply to you. Another example: a father says, "If you are hungry, then go eat something!" — if you aren't hungry, then you are free to ignore the command.

Categorical Imperatives

These command unconditionally. E.g., "Don't cheat on your taxes." Even if you want to cheat and doing so would serve your interests, you may not cheat.

Morality and the Categorical Imperative

🔗
What is the connection between morality and categorical imperatives?

Morality must be based on the categorical imperative because morality is such that one is commanded by it, and is such that one cannot opt out of it or claim that it does not apply to oneself.

CHAPTER 08
Testing a Maxim's Universality

Two ways when a maxim won't be able to pass the universality qualification:

1. Contradiction in Conception Test

Maxim: Whenever anyone is in need, they will make a false promise in order to get some cash.

⚠️
Problem

It is impossible to imagine circumstances in which this is a universal law. If the false promises maxim were accepted by everyone, the whole practice of making promises would collapse.

2. Contradiction in Will Test

Maxim: Whenever anyone sees another person in urgent need of help, they will not help the needy in order to avoid sacrificing their own happiness.

⚠️
Problem

In the world in which no one helps others, one would still have some goals. Because one wants things that will require other people's help to secure, one can't consistently will the selfish maxim to be a universal law.

CHAPTER 09
Advantages
  • Kant's morality is very straightforward and based on reason, making it accessible to everyone.
  • Duty is part of human experience.
  • Morality doesn't depend on motives, consequences, or religious laws.
  • Categorical imperative gives rules that apply to everyone and command respect for human life.
  • It makes clear that morality is doing one's duty and not just following feelings. One cannot assume what is good for oneself is good for everyone else — Kant's equivalent of the Golden Rule. (the source slide also includes the single word "karma" alongside this point)
  • It aims to treat everyone fairly and justly, so it corrects the utilitarian idea that some can suffer as long as others are happy.
  • Kant sees humans as being of intrinsic worth as they are the rational high point of creation. This means they cannot be enslaved or exploited (basis of the Declaration of Human Rights).
  • Equal treatment of individuals gets rid of bias which sometimes influences decision-making.
  • Categorical imperative tells us exactly what is right and wrong, giving a clear sense of moral guidelines.
  • Kant draws a clear distinction between duty and preference. Moral value of an action comes from its intrinsic rightness, so issues of teleological ethics are avoided.
  • People generally do have the same ideas about morality.
  • Ethical practice should be based on reason, not subjective emotion.
  • Most people recognize the idea of duty — it is part of what it means to be human.
  • There is a difference between duty and inclination.
CHAPTER 10
Disadvantages
  • Kant's theory is abstract and not always easily applicable — it tells what type of actions are good but not the right thing to do in particular situations.
  • People rarely act purely out of duty, as they always have some expectation of what they'll get in return.
  • Some philosophers think putting duty above feeling is cold and inhuman — there is no place for love and personal relations in Kant's theory.
  • Kant tells us in general terms to respect others and not treat them as a means to an end, but doesn't tell us what to do in individual cases.
  • Philippa Foot, among others, has criticized that Kant's theory doesn't help with the situation of the double effect.
  • There are no exceptions in using people as means to ends, which severely restricts behavior.
  • People are different and don't necessarily have the same sense of 'good will.'
  • Not everyone is capable of making rational moral decisions.
  • Every action taken involves love and compassion, because human beings are involved.
  • It is human nature to consider the consequences.

CHAPTER 11
Concluding Remarks

Deontology or Kantian ethics is based on the view that human beings are rational. It is this rationality that enables a person to independently create a moral law for oneself, going beyond the grips of emotions and desires, which establishes one's autonomy and freedom.

CHAPTER 12
Group Activities

1. Case Study Analysis (Moral Dilemmas)

Objective: Apply deontological principles to real-world ethical dilemmas.

  • Present scenarios such as lying to save a life, stealing to feed a starving person, or breaking a promise for a greater good.
  • Analyze whether the action is morally acceptable according to deontology (duty-based ethics).
  • Discuss the Categorical Imperative and whether the action could be universalized.

2. Role-Playing: Autonomy vs. Heteronomy

Objective: Understand the difference between autonomy (self-governance) and heteronomy (external control) in moral decision-making.

  • Assign roles (e.g., a person deciding whether to follow a strict rule or obey an authority figure).
  • One student makes a decision autonomously (based on moral reasoning).
  • Another follows heteronomy (obeys authority or external rules without question).
  • Discuss the impact of autonomy and heteronomy on moral responsibility.

3. "Moral Laws" Debate

Objective: Explore moral absolutism in deontology.

  • Divide the class into two teams. One supports absolute moral rules (e.g., lying is always wrong), while the other argues for exceptions based on consequences.
  • Use examples like lying to protect someone from harm.
  • Debate whether certain moral rules should be followed in every situation or if exceptions should be allowed.

4. Universalization Test (Kant's Categorical Imperative)

Objective: Evaluate ethical principles using Kant's test.

  • Give different ethical statements (e.g., "It is okay to steal if you are poor.").
  • Apply the universalization test: "What if everyone did this?"
  • Discuss whether the rule can be logically applied to everyone without contradiction.

5. Deontology in Everyday Life — Reflection Journal

Objective: Encourage reflection on moral decision-making.

  • Keep a journal for a week, recording situations involving moral choices.
  • Analyze those decisions using deontological principles (e.g., duty, universal laws, intentions).
  • Reflect on whether the choice was made out of autonomy or heteronomy.

III. Essay

1.
Explain the difference between Deontology and Utilitarianism.
Suggested Answer: Deontology judges morality based on duty and intention, while Utilitarianism judges based on the consequences or outcomes of actions.
2.
What is the significance of the Categorical Imperative in moral decision-making?
Suggested Answer: It serves as a universal moral law requiring that actions be applicable to everyone without contradiction, guiding individuals to act out of moral duty.
3.
Why does Kant believe that 'Good Will' is the only thing good in itself?
Suggested Answer: Because it represents the pure intention to do what is morally right, regardless of the outcome or reward.
4.
Differentiate between Autonomy and Heteronomy.
Suggested Answer: Autonomy means acting according to one's own rational moral law, while heteronomy means acting under external influence or authority.
5.
How can Deontological ethics be applied in real-life situations?
Suggested Answer: By making decisions based on duty and principles rather than personal gain, such as telling the truth even when lying might be more convenient.
