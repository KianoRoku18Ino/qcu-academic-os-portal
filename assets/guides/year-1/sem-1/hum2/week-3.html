CHAPTER 00
Course Overview & Study Priority Map

This module (source file: "Ethical Levels of Human Existence") lays out ethics as operating on three expanding levels of moral responsibility — the Personal Level, the Societal Level, and the Environmental Level — and explains the two mechanisms that keep a person accountable at the Personal Level: Sanctions (external consequences) and Conscience (internal judgment). The lesson draws its Personal-Level material from Ramon B. Agapay's Ethics and the Filipino, and its Conscience definitions from Paul Tillich and Alfredo Panizo.

🧭
Recurring Framework — The Three Ethical Levels

The lesson keeps returning to one structure: ethical concern radiates outward from the individual. Personal → Societal → Environmental. Conscience and Sanctions are the accountability mechanisms that operate specifically at the Personal Level, before responsibility widens to community-level concerns (Societal) and then to the shared ecological world (Environmental). Chapters 01, 04, and 05 each open by naming which level of this framework they belong to — keep this structure in mind, since it's the most likely organizing question a professor would ask in recitation ("which level does X belong to, and why?").

The Three Levels at a Glance

1 · Personal Level
CH. 01
Individual
Can the person tell good from evil, and do they feel obligated and accountable for their own actions?
2 · Societal Level
CH. 04
Community
Ethical concerns that affect society at large — corruption, poverty, violence, and similar issues.
3 · Environmental Level
CH. 05
Ecosystem
Ethical concerns about humanity's impact on the natural world — pollution, deforestation, and similar issues.

CHAPTER 01 · Personal Level of the Three-Levels Framework
The Personal Level

The lesson opens with the Personal Level — the ethos (characteristic moral nature) of the individual person, revealed through three observable traits (lesson p. 2–4), then grounded in a broader claim about human moral sense (lesson p. 5).

The Ethos of Man — 3 Traits

1
Distinguishes good from evil
He/she is able to distinguish between good and evil, right and wrong, moral and immoral. (lesson p. 2, citing Agapay p. 1–60)
2
Feels obligated to do good
He/she feels within himself/herself an obligation to do what is good and to avoid what is evil. (lesson p. 3)
3
Feels accountable
He/she feels accountable for his/her actions, expecting reward or punishment for them. (lesson p. 4)
AGAPAY · ETHICS AND THE FILIPINO, P. 1
On Man's Moral Sense
Ramon B. Agapay — cited in the lesson, p. 5
"Man is endowed by nature with a moral sense. He/She is self-conscious of his dignity and submits to the duty of doing what is good and avoiding what is wrong. This much is expected of man: that he/she conducts himself/herself according to the 'dictates of reason'."

Recitation-Ready Answers Added — Study Aid

KEY TERM
Personal Level
Plain English: the starting point of ethics — whether you, as one person, can tell right from wrong and hold yourself accountable for it.
vs. Societal Level: Personal is about the individual's inner moral compass; Societal is about the same questions applied to a whole community.
Example: knowing on your own, without being told, that copying a classmate's exam answers is wrong.
KEY TERM
Moral Sense / "Dictates of Reason"
Plain English: the natural human capacity to sense right from wrong and to follow what reason says is the moral thing to do.
vs. Conscience: Moral sense is the general human capacity; Conscience (Ch. 03) is the specific act of judging a particular action using that capacity.
Example: feeling an instinctive discomfort before even reasoning it out, when asked to lie for a friend.
💬
Recitation Drill — Chapter 01
What ethical level is being described when a person recognizes good from evil?
The Personal Level.
Name the three traits that reveal the ethos of man.
(1) distinguishing good from evil, (2) feeling obligated to do good and avoid evil, (3) feeling accountable for one's actions.
According to Agapay, what should a person's conduct be based on?
The "dictates of reason" — man is endowed by nature with a moral sense and is expected to act according to reason.
CHAPTER 02
Sanctions and Penalties

Where the Personal Level established that a person feels accountable (trait 3, Chapter 01), this section lists the actual external consequences the lesson identifies for moral wrongdoing (lesson p. 7).

The Four Sanctions Listed in the Lesson

1
The penal laws of our country
Civil/criminal law as an external, legal sanction.
2
Death as punishment for sin
Cited in the lesson to the Bible, Old Testament (OT).
3
Physical and mental diseases
Listed as a consequence-type sanction in the lesson.
4
Human suffering
Listed as a general consequence-type sanction in the lesson.

Recitation-Ready Answer Added — Study Aid

KEY TERM
Sanction
Plain English: an outside consequence — legal, physical, or spiritual — that follows from doing wrong.
vs. Conscience: a Sanction is external (imposed on you from outside); Conscience (Ch. 03) is internal (a judgment you make within yourself).
Example: a person going to jail for theft (penal law) versus a person privately feeling guilty about the same theft (conscience).
💬
Recitation Drill — Chapter 02
List the four sanctions/penalties given in the lesson.
Penal laws of the country, death as punishment for sin (Bible OT), physical and mental diseases, and human suffering.
How does a Sanction differ from Conscience?
A Sanction is an external consequence imposed on a person; Conscience is the internal judgment the person makes about their own action.
CHAPTER 03 · Personal Level — the Internal Judge
Conscience: Nature, Definitions, and the Six Kinds

Conscience is the internal counterpart to the external Sanctions of Chapter 02 — both operate at the Personal Level of the three-levels framework. The lesson gives two definitions (an etymological one from Tillich, and a functional one from Panizo), then works through six kinds of conscience and four causes of an erroneous conscience.

Two Definitions of Conscience

ETYMOLOGY
Tillich's Definition
From the Latin "conscientia," meaning "trial of oneself," both in accusation and in defense.
FUNCTIONAL
Panizo's Definition
An act of the practical judgment of reason deciding upon an individual action as good and to be performed, or as evil and to be avoided.
TILLICH — ETYMOLOGY, LESSON P. 9
Conscience — from the Latin "Conscientia"
Paul Tillich, theologian (August 20, 1886 – October 22, 1965) — dates verified; lesson gives only "1886–1965"
"From the Latin 'conscientia' meaning 'trial of oneself' both in accusation and in defense."
PANIZO, P. 63 — LESSON P. 10
Conscience — the Practical Judgment of Reason
cited in the lesson
"An act of the practical judgment of reason deciding upon an individual action as good and to be performed or as evil and to be avoided."

The Six Kinds of Conscience From the Lesson (p. 11–18)

TYPE 1
Correct / True Conscience

It judges what is good as good and what is evil as evil. Example given in the lesson: it is correct when it tells that getting the property of another without consent is stealing.

TYPE 2
Erroneous / False Conscience

Judges incorrectly that what is good is evil and what is evil is good. Example given in the lesson: erroneous conscience is what tells the husband to have a mistress.

TYPE 3
Certain Conscience

A subjective assurance of the lawfulness or unlawfulness of a certain act — the person is sure of his/her decision.

TYPE 4
Doubtful Conscience

Unable to form a definite judgment on a certain action; it must first be allowed to settle its doubts before an action is performed.

TYPE 5
Scrupulous Conscience

Rigorous conscience, extremely afraid of committing evil. A scrupulous conscience is meticulous and wants incontrovertible proofs before it acts.

TYPE 6
Lax Conscience

Refuses to be bothered about the distinction of good and evil. It rushes on and is quick to justify itself.

Recitation-Ready Answers, Kind by Kind Added — Study Aid

TYPE 1
Correct Conscience
Plain English: gets the moral call right.
vs. Erroneous: Correct matches reality (good=good, evil=evil); Erroneous flips it.
Example: knowing that taking someone's phone without asking is stealing.
TYPE 2
Erroneous Conscience
Plain English: gets the moral call backwards.
vs. Correct: the opposite outcome from the same judging process.
Example: the lesson's own case — a conscience that tells a husband it's fine to have a mistress.
TYPE 3
Certain Conscience
Plain English: fully sure, one way or the other.
vs. Doubtful: Certain has resolved the question; Doubtful hasn't yet.
Example: being completely sure that cheating on an exam is wrong, no hesitation.
TYPE 4
Doubtful Conscience
Plain English: genuinely unresolved, not yet ready to act.
vs. Certain: lacks the assurance Certain conscience has.
Example: not knowing whether it's okay to keep money found on the street, and pausing before deciding.
TYPE 5
Scrupulous Conscience
Plain English: overly cautious — sees sin where there may be none, and needs excessive proof.
vs. Lax: Scrupulous overreacts to evil; Lax barely reacts at all.
Example: someone who agonizes over whether an innocent joke was secretly a lie.
TYPE 6
Lax Conscience
Plain English: too relaxed — brushes past the good/evil question entirely.
vs. Scrupulous: the opposite extreme from Scrupulous.
Example: someone who shrugs off cheating with "everyone does it anyway."

Why a Conscience Becomes Erroneous — 4 Causes

1
Mistake in inferential thinking
Deriving a wrong conclusion from given moral principles. (lesson p. 13)
2
Ignorance of the law
(lesson p. 13)
3
Ignorance of the fact and other circumstances
Ignorance of the fact and other circumstances modifying human actions. (lesson p. 13)
4
Ignorance of future consequences
Especially those dependent on the free will of others. (lesson p. 14)
✅
Closing Principle (lesson p. 19)

"Insofar as conscience operates within the realm of truth and sound reason, it is compulsory." — unattributed in the source slide; presented here exactly as given.

⚠️
Common Mix-Ups Added
Erroneous vs. Doubtful
Erroneous conscience has made a (wrong) decision; Doubtful conscience has not decided at all yet.
Scrupulous vs. Certain
Both feel strongly, but Certain is confidently right-sized; Scrupulous is excessively anxious and keeps demanding more proof.
Sanction vs. Conscience
Don't confuse Chapter 02's external Sanctions with this chapter's internal Conscience — a quiz item may ask you to tell "internal" from "external" accountability.

Recitation-Ready Answer Added — Study Aid

KEY TERM
Societal Level
Plain English: ethics at the scale of a whole community, not just one person.
vs. Personal Level: Personal asks "can I tell right from wrong?"; Societal asks "what happens when many people's wrongs pile up into a community-wide problem?"
Example: corruption — a societal-scale ethical failure, versus one official privately taking one bribe (which starts as a Personal-Level lapse).

Recitation-Ready Answer Added — Study Aid

KEY TERM
Environmental Level
Plain English: ethics applied to how human activity affects the natural world.
vs. Societal Level: Societal concerns are about people affecting other people; Environmental concerns are about people affecting the ecosystem they all depend on.
Example: deforestation for commercial logging — an ethical question about humanity's impact on nature, not directly about one person's or one community's conduct toward other people.
