CHAPTER 02 · PART 1
What Is Ethics?

From the lesson.

Etymology

Ethos (Greek) — "characteristic way of acting." Its Latin equivalent is mos, mores, meaning tradition or custom.

Ethics and Philosophy

Philosophy, derived from the Greek 'philo' (love) and 'sophia' (wisdom), is literally defined as "the love of wisdom." More broadly, it is the study of the most basic and profound matters of human existence — fundamental questions of human existence, the love of wisdom asking "Why?" It begins with wondering. — (Plato)

Ethics: the wrongness and correctness of our actions.

Ethos / Mores
Recitation-ready
Plain-English"Ethos" is the Greek word for a person's or group's characteristic way of acting — their habitual character. Its Latin cousin "mos/mores" gives us the word "moral" and means custom or tradition.
CompareSame root idea in two languages: ethos (Greek) → "ethics"; mos/mores (Latin) → "morals." That's why "ethics" and "morals" feel so close in everyday speech.
ExampleA community's mores (its established customs around honesty in trade) is the Latin-rooted way of describing what the Greek-rooted word "ethos" points at.
Philosophy vs. Ethics
Recitation-ready
Plain-EnglishPhilosophy is the broad "love of wisdom" — asking fundamental questions about existence in general. Ethics is one branch of that: specifically the study of what makes an action right or wrong.
ComparePhilosophy is the whole tree; ethics is one branch of it (alongside branches like metaphysics or epistemology).
Example"Does free will exist?" is a philosophy question in general; "Is it right to lie to protect someone?" is specifically an ethics question.

Recitation Drill

Q:What does "ethos" literally mean, and what is its Latin equivalent?
A:"Ethos" is Greek for "characteristic way of acting." Its Latin equivalent is mos/mores, meaning tradition or custom.
Q:How does the professor define ethics, in one line?
A:The wrongness and correctness of our actions.
Q:Who is quoted as saying philosophy "begins with wondering," and is that attribution accurate?
A:Plato — and yes, verified: the line traces to Plato's dialogue Theaetetus (155d).
CHAPTER 03 · PART 1
The Four Main Areas of Ethics

From the lesson.

AREA 1
Descriptive Ethics

Empirical research into the attitudes of individuals or groups of people. Aims to uncover people's beliefs about values, which actions are right and wrong, and which characteristics of moral agents are virtuous.
Focus: what people actually believe to be right/wrong, the moral values they hold, how they behave, what ethical rules guide their reasoning.
Example: surveys of ethical attitudes among employees and managers — e.g. whether individuals feel pressure to compromise moral principles to achieve organizational goals.

AREA 2
Normative Ethics

Concerned with criteria of what is morally right and wrong. Central question: how are basic moral standards arrived at and justified? Answers fall into two broad categories — deontological and teleological/consequentialist.
Examples: "It is wrong to kill people just because they make you angry." · "We should fight to free slaves when necessary, even when doing so is illegal." · "Pain is intrinsically bad — we ought not cause pain without a good reason to do so."

AREA 3
Metaethics

Seeks to understand the nature of ethical properties, principles, judgments, and attitudes. Attempts to answer questions like "What is morality?", "What is goodness?", "How to identify if something is good or bad?"
Sample questions: "What is goodness?" and "How can we tell what is good from what is bad?"

AREA 4
Applied Ethics

Deals with ethical questions specific to a professional, disciplinary, or practical field. Subsets include medical ethics, bioethics, business ethics, legal ethics, and others.

Applied Ethics — The Full Subset List

SubfieldSample Question
Animal EthicsIs it permissible to eat meat?
Biomedical EthicsOn whom are we allowed to perform medical tests?

Environmental EthicsOught we curb climate change for the sake of future generations?

Descriptive vs. Normative Ethics
Recitation-ready
Plain-EnglishDescriptive ethics reports what people do believe is right or wrong (like a survey). Normative ethics argues for what actually is right or wrong (like a standard).
CompareDescriptive = "is" (observing beliefs); Normative = "ought" (setting standards).
Example"Most Filipino employees say it's okay to accept small gifts from clients" is descriptive. "Employees ought not accept gifts that could bias their decisions" is normative.
Metaethics vs. Normative Ethics
Recitation-ready
Plain-EnglishNormative ethics gives the rules ("don't lie"). Metaethics steps back and asks what "right" and "wrong" even mean in the first place, and where moral knowledge comes from.
CompareNormative ethics is one level "in the game" (making moral claims); metaethics is one level "above the game" (analyzing what moral claims are and mean).
Example"Stealing is wrong" is a normative claim. "What does it even mean for something to be 'wrong,' and can moral claims be true or false?" is a metaethical question.

Recitation Drill

Q:Name the four main areas of ethics.
A:Descriptive Ethics, Normative Ethics, Metaethics, and Applied Ethics.
Q:Normative ethics answers fall into which two broad categories?
A:Deontological and teleological (or consequentialist).
Q:A company surveys employees about whether they'd report a coworker's minor policy violation. What area of ethics is this an example of?
A:Descriptive ethics — it's empirical research into people's actual attitudes, not a claim about what's right.
Q:"Is it permissible to eat meat?" belongs to which applied-ethics subfield?
A:Animal Ethics.
CHAPTER 04 · PART 1
Clarifications and Terminology: Ethics and Morals

From the lesson.

PERSONAL CONDUCT
Morals
On specific beliefs or attitudes that people have, or to describe acts that people perform — personal conduct.
DISCIPLINE / STUDY
Ethics
The discipline of studying and understanding ideal human behavior and ideal ways of thinking.
Ethics vs. Morals
Recitation-ready
Plain-EnglishMorals are the actual beliefs and conduct a specific person or group holds. Ethics is the organized, systematic study of what human behavior and thinking ideally should be.
CompareMorals = the lived thing (what someone actually believes/does). Ethics = the study of the thing (the discipline that examines it).
Example"I personally believe cheating is wrong" is a moral stance. "Philosophers debate what makes cheating wrong across different ethical theories" is doing ethics.

Recitation Drill

Q:Per the lesson, what is the difference between "morals" and "ethics"?
A:Morals refer to specific beliefs, attitudes, or acts of personal conduct. Ethics is the discipline of studying and understanding ideal human behavior and ideal ways of thinking.
CHAPTER 05 · PART 1
Kinds of Valuation

From the lesson. This section maps to the Course Contents' "Morality & Other Normative Systems" — it contrasts ethics against three other kinds of value judgment people commonly confuse it with.

VALUATION 1
Etiquette

Customary code of polite behavior in society or among members of a particular profession or group.
Examples (classroom etiquette): 1. Turn off your cell phone. 2. Don't pack up before class ends. 3. Do not have private conversations.

VALUATION 2
Aesthetics

Judgments of personal approval and disapproval referring to individual tastes and preferences.
Examples: 1. A sunset on the beach is highly beautiful, therefore its aesthetic value is also high for people. 2. A plate of food served with elegance. 3. It is "wrong" to wear a leather vest over a Barong Tagalog.

VALUATION 3
Technique / Technical

The proper way or right way of doing things.
Examples: 1. How to bake (it could be "wrong" when it is against the correct way of doing it). 2. How to play basketball. 3. How to shut down a computer.

Ethics vs. the Other Three Valuations
Recitation-ready
Plain-EnglishAll four (ethics, etiquette, aesthetics, technique) use words like "right/wrong" or "good/bad" — but they're judging completely different things.
CompareEthics judges moral rightness (harm, fairness, duty). Etiquette judges social politeness. Aesthetics judges beauty/taste. Technique judges procedural correctness.
ExampleInterrupting someone mid-sentence is rude (etiquette), an ugly painting is a bad aesthetic judgment, baking bread with the wrong ratio of flour to water is a technique error, and lying to a friend to protect yourself is an ethics problem — only the last one is a genuine moral issue.

Recitation Drill

Q:Give the four kinds of valuation covered in the lesson, including ethics itself.
A:Ethics, Etiquette, Aesthetics, and Technique/Technical.
Q:"It is 'wrong' to wear a leather vest over a Barong Tagalog" — which kind of valuation is this, and why isn't it an ethics example?
A:Aesthetics — it's a matter of taste/appropriateness in personal style, not a matter of moral right or wrong.
Q:Why is "turn off your cell phone in class" etiquette and not ethics?
A:It's a customary code of polite behavior specific to a group/setting (a classroom), not a question of moral harm or duty.

Ethical Levels of Human Existence
Added interpretation — not a slide caption
Plain-EnglishThe diagram places human ethical life between two poles: the animal level (instinct, survival, no moral reasoning) below, and the divine level (a higher, ideal standard of goodness) above. Humans occupy the middle — capable of both instinct-driven and morally-reasoned action.
CompareThis isn't the same as the Four Main Areas of Ethics (Ch. 03) — that's a classification of ethics as a field of study; this diagram is about where human moral capacity sits relative to other kinds of existence.
ExampleAn animal fighting for food acts on pure instinct (animal level); a person choosing to share food even when hungry, guided by a sense of duty or compassion, is acting at the human/moral level.

CHAPTER 07 · PART 1
Issue, Judgment, Decision, and Dilemma

From the lesson.

TERM 1
Issue

Controversial, and people keep on debating about it.
Examples: a. cigarette smoking · b. anti-terror bill.

TERM 2
Judgment

Assessment of the actions or behavior of someone.
Examples: a. A friend chooses to steal from a store, and I make an assessment that it is wrong. b. "Cigarette smoking is dangerous to your health." c. Humility is a virtue.

TERM 3
Decision

Choice of what to perform or do.
Examples: a. I choose not to take something I did not pay for. b. I decided to look for both local and remote employees. c. I decided to take a vacation in the province.

TERM 4
Dilemma

A situation in which a difficult choice has to be made between two or more alternatives, especially equally undesirable ones.
Examples: a. Should Michael reveal to Roger that Phyllis is married? b. My mother gave me two choices: do the dishes or clean the bathroom. c. Say nothing and just wait to see what happens?

Issue vs. Dilemma
Recitation-ready
Plain-EnglishAn issue is a topic society keeps arguing about in general. A dilemma is a specific, personal moment where you must pick between hard options.
CompareIssue = ongoing public debate (no single decision-maker). Dilemma = one person's hard choice, right now.
Example"Should the death penalty be legal?" is an issue debated nationally. "Should I turn in my brother, who I know committed a crime?" is a personal dilemma.
Judgment vs. Decision
Recitation-ready
Plain-EnglishJudgment evaluates someone's (often another person's) actions as right or wrong. Decision is choosing your own course of action.
CompareJudgment looks backward/outward (assessing an act that happened). Decision looks forward/inward (choosing what you will do).
Example"What she did was dishonest" is a judgment. "I will not do the same thing she did" is a decision.

Recitation Drill

Q:Define "dilemma" as given in the lesson.
A:A situation in which a difficult choice has to be made between two or more alternatives, especially equally undesirable ones.
Q:"Cigarette smoking is dangerous to your health" — is this an issue, judgment, decision, or dilemma?
A:Judgment — it's an assessment/claim about smoking, given directly as a judgment example in the lesson.

Group Performance Activities

From the lesson. These are in-class group activities graded by the rubric below — not written-exam material, so they're summarized rather than expanded into recitation drills.

  • 1. Ethical Dilemma Role Play — groups act out an assigned ethical dilemma (e.g. whistleblowing, plagiarism) and debrief as a class.
