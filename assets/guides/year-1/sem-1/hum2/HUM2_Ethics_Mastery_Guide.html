CHAPTER 01 · WEEKS 1–2 · FROM MODULE 1
What Is Ethics?

This chapter now follows Dr. Jackelyn L. Legaspi's actual first module directly — her own framing, examples, and practice quiz, not general reference material. Her module's own three-part roadmap for the whole semester: Part 1 (Basic Concepts, Ch. 01–04), Part 2 (Ethical Theories, Ch. 05–09), Part 3 (Making Informed Decisions, Ch. 10–11) — which matches this guide's structure exactly.

Why Rules Matter to Social Beings

Week 1's activity asks what rules you found difficult to follow during the COVID-19 pandemic, and why. The mini-lecture question — "why do rules matter to social beings?" — is the course's opening thesis: humans are inherently social, and social life is impossible without shared norms that constrain individual behavior for collective benefit.

Ethics and Philosophy

Philosophy, derived from the Greek philo (love) and sophia (wisdom), is literally "the love of wisdom" — the study of the most basic and profound matters of human existence, the fundamental questions of "why." As Plato put it, philosophy begins with wondering. Ethics, within that larger discipline, is specifically concerned with the wrongness and correctness of our actions.

The Meaning of Ethics

"Ethics" derives from the Greek ethos — "characteristic way of acting." Its Latin equivalent is mos, mores, meaning tradition or custom.

Where Ethics Sits — Animal, Human, Divine

Your module frames ethics within a three-level hierarchy. Human beings occupy a unique middle position: capable of reason and moral responsibility in a way animals aren't, yet distinct from the divine.

Animal
Governed by instinct; no moral responsibility.
Human
Capable of reason and moral choice — the domain where ethics actually operates.
Divine
The ultimate reference point several ethical theories orient toward — recall this when Natural Law (Ch. 06) grounds morality in a God-given human purpose.

The Four Main Areas of Ethics

Four branches, not three — Descriptive Ethics is the addition worth knowing on top of the usual Normative/Meta/Applied split.

1. Descriptive Ethics
WHAT PEOPLE ACTUALLY BELIEVE
Empirical research into people's actual attitudes — not what's right, but what people believe is right, what values they hold, how they behave, and what rules guide their reasoning. Example: surveying employees on whether they feel pressure to compromise principles for organizational goals.
2. Normative Ethics
WHAT STANDARD DETERMINES RIGHT/WRONG
Concerned with the criteria of what is morally right and wrong, and how such standards are justified — falling into deontological or teleological/consequentialist camps. Example: "It is wrong to kill people just because they make you angry."
3. Metaethics
WHAT MORALITY EVEN IS
Seeks to understand the nature of ethical properties, principles, and judgments themselves — "What is goodness?" and "How can we tell what is good from what is bad?"
4. Applied Ethics
ETHICS APPLIED TO A FIELD
Deals with ethical questions specific to a professional, disciplinary, or practical field — medical ethics, bioethics, business ethics, legal ethics, and more.

Clarifications and Terminology — Ethics vs. Morals

PERSONAL CONDUCT
Morals
Specific beliefs or attitudes that people have, or the acts that people perform.
THE DISCIPLINE
Ethics
The discipline of studying and understanding ideal human behavior and ideal ways of thinking.

Kinds of Valuation

Not everything that sounds like a judgment is actually a moral one. Your module draws a sharp line between three non-moral kinds of valuation and genuine ethical/moral valuation.

SOCIAL CONDUCT
Etiquette
A customary code of polite behavior in society or within a profession/group. Example: turning off your phone in class; not packing up before class ends.
PERSONAL TASTE
Aesthetics
Judgments of personal approval/disapproval about individual tastes and preferences. Example: calling a sunset "beautiful," or saying it's "wrong" to wear a leather vest over a Barong Tagalog.
CORRECT METHOD
Technique/Technical
The proper or right way of doing something. Example: how to bake, how to play basketball, how to shut down a computer — "wrong" here means against correct procedure, not immoral.
📌
Exam-Critical Distinction

Calling something "wrong" doesn't automatically make it a moral claim. "You're doing that wrong" (technique), "that outfit is wrong" (aesthetics), and "cheating is wrong" (ethics) all use the same word for three genuinely different kinds of valuation — only the last is a moral judgment.

Issue, Judgment, Decision, and Dilemma

Issue
Something controversial that people keep debating. E.g., cigarette smoking; the anti-terror bill.
Judgment
An assessment of someone's actions or behavior. E.g., "Cigarette smoking is dangerous to your health"; "Humility is a virtue."
Decision
A choice of what to perform or do. E.g., choosing not to take something you didn't pay for.
Dilemma
A situation requiring a difficult choice between two or more alternatives, especially equally undesirable ones. E.g., whether to reveal a friend's secret.

Ethics and Reasoning

Whatever we do, we give reasons for it. Your module names four sources those reasons typically draw on:

  • Reason that we give for the things we do
  • Rewards and punishment
  • Principles
  • Ethical frameworks or ethical theories — the five named theories this course builds toward (Ch. 05–09)

CHAPTER 02 · WEEKS 3–4
Morality & Other Normative Systems

Morality isn't the only system that tells people how to behave — law, culture, and religion all do too. This chapter draws the boundaries between them, then turns inward to ask what "the self" even is when it comes to moral motivation.

Sources of Authority — Comparing Normative Systems

CODIFIED & ENFORCED
Ethics and Law
Law is codified, state-enforced, and backed by coercive penalties. Ethics is broader and can judge the law itself — an act can be legal but unethical (e.g., historical slavery statutes), or ethical but illegal (e.g., civil disobedience).
SHARED & INHERITED
Ethics and Culture
Culture transmits norms through custom, tradition, and socialization rather than formal codification. Ethics asks whether a cultural practice is actually justifiable, not just customary — cultural relativism is a live debate here.
REVEALED & SACRED
Ethics and Religion
Religion grounds morality in divine command or sacred teaching. Ethics asks whether religious grounding is necessary for morality at all — this exact question resurfaces as the Euthyphro Dilemma in Chapter 06.

Senses of the Self — Why Do We Act Morally At All?

Subjectivism
METAETHICAL VIEW
Moral judgments are just expressions of personal feeling or opinion — "murder is wrong" really means "I disapprove of murder." No objective moral facts exist.
Psychological Egoism
DESCRIPTIVE CLAIM
A factual claim about human psychology: people always act from self-interest, even when it looks selfless (e.g., helping others because it makes you feel good).
Ethical Egoism
NORMATIVE CLAIM
Not a description but a prescription: people ought to act in their own self-interest — that this is what morality actually requires.

Terminology Glossary

Normative System
Any organized set of rules or standards guiding behavior — morality, law, culture, and religion are each one.
Cultural Relativism
The view that moral standards are only valid relative to a given culture, with no culture-independent standard to judge between them.
Subjectivism
The metaethical view that moral claims express personal feeling rather than objective fact.
Psychological Egoism
The descriptive theory that all human action is ultimately self-interested.
Ethical Egoism
The normative theory that self-interest is what morality itself requires.

Exam-Critical Distinction — Descriptive vs. Normative

Psychological Egoism describes how people actually behave (a claim that could be empirically false). Ethical Egoism prescribes how people should behave (a value claim). Confusing these two is one of the single most common mistakes on this exact quiz topic.

CHAPTER 03 · WEEK 5
The Ethical Levels of Human Existence

Not every ethical failure carries the same weight. This short but exam-relevant chapter gives you a way to grade the seriousness of moral valuations, then connects that directly to social responsibility during crisis — using the same COVID-19 framing the syllabus leans on throughout.

Ethical Levels of Moral Valuations

Moral philosophers commonly distinguish levels of ethical seriousness rather than treating all "wrongs" as equivalent:

Level 1 — Technical/Efficiency
Judgments about whether an action works well or poorly — not yet moral at all (e.g., "that's an inefficient way to cook rice").
Level 2 — Social/Prudential
Judgments about social acceptability or personal advantage — etiquette, custom, practical wisdom, still short of full moral weight.
Level 3 — Moral
Judgments about whether an action is right or wrong in the deepest sense — whether it's truly permissible, obligatory, or forbidden.

This progression — Mabuti bang gawain o di kaya'y masama? ("is this act good, or is it bad?") — is the throughline Filipino ethics textbooks use to show how a simple practical judgment gradually deepens into genuine moral judgment.

Moral Reasoning and Social Responsibility

Moral reasoning is the process of working from general ethical principles to a specific judgment about a particular case — and it doesn't happen in a vacuum. Every moral agent operates inside a web of social responsibility: your choices affect others, and "social responsibility" names the moral weight of that fact. The pandemic-era framing question the syllabus uses directly — "How can I be moral in the midst of the COVID-19 Pandemic?" — is a genuine applied-ethics exercise: it forces you to weigh personal liberty against collective health outcomes in real time.

Terminology Glossary

Moral Valuation
The act of judging whether something has moral worth — right/wrong, good/bad.
Moral Reasoning
The process of deriving a specific moral judgment from general ethical principles.
Social Responsibility
The moral obligation an individual has toward the wellbeing of the community they belong to.

CHAPTER 04 · WEEK 6
The Moral Agent

Before judging actions, ethics needs to say something about who's doing the judging. This chapter is built around Filipino philosopher Ramon C. Reyes's account of moral identity — genuinely useful material, and a direct source for your "Who am I as a Filipino Moral Agent?" reflection paper.

Moral Agent: Context and Moral Development

A moral agent is a person capable of distinguishing right from wrong and being held accountable for their actions. Ramon C. Reyes, writing in his essay "Man and Historical Action," argued that who one is is itself a "cross-point" — identity is the product of forces largely outside your own choosing.

Physical Cross-point
Genetic and environmental factors — your body, health, and material circumstances of birth.
Interpersonal Cross-point
Relationships with parents, family, and significant others who shaped your early moral formation.
Social Cross-point
The culture and values of the society you were raised in.
Historical Cross-point
Major events — a war, a revolution, a pandemic — that shape an entire generation's outlook at once.
RESEARCH NOTE — "FOUR," NOT "FIVE"
A Correction Worth Knowing
Verified against multiple academic sources on Reyes's work
Your syllabus refers to "Ramon C. Reyes' Five Cross-points." Every available academic source on Reyes's actual essay — including Ateneo's own philosophy archive — describes exactly four cross-points: physical, interpersonal, social, and historical. Reyes's further point, that a person is not merely determined by these but also possesses genuine freedom and self-determination to shape their own identity as a "project of the self," may be the intended "fifth" element your syllabus is counting — worth clarifying directly with your professor, since it changes how you'd answer an identification question.

Moral Agent: Characteristics

Despite being shaped by the four cross-points, Reyes insists a moral agent retains genuine freedom and self-determination — the capacity to become "a project of the self" rather than a mere product of circumstance. This is what makes moral accountability coherent at all: if you were purely determined by forces outside your control, holding you responsible would make no sense.

Moral Agent: Moral Accountability

Moral accountability is the flip side of moral agency: because you have the capacity for free, reasoned choice, you can legitimately be praised or blamed for what you choose. Accountability requires three things together — the capacity to understand the situation, the freedom to have chosen otherwise, and a genuine causal link between your choice and the outcome.

Terminology Glossary

Moral Agent
A person capable of distinguishing right from wrong and being held accountable for their actions.
Cross-point
Ramon Reyes's term for a force outside one's control that shapes identity — physical, interpersonal, social, or historical.
Self-Determination
The capacity to freely shape one's own identity and choices despite being conditioned by the cross-points.
Moral Accountability
Being legitimately praiseworthy or blameworthy for a choice, given genuine freedom and understanding.

CHAPTER 05 · WEEKS 7–8
Utilitarianism

The first of the five named ethical theories, and the one most students find intuitive at first — until Mill's refinement complicates it. Judge actions purely by their consequences.

The Meaning of Utilitarianism & the Principle of Utility

Utilitarianism is a consequentialist theory: an action's moral worth is determined entirely by its outcomes, specifically its effect on overall happiness. Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) founded the theory in his 1789 Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation.

PRIMARY SOURCE
Bentham's Principle of Utility
An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, Ch. 1 (1789)
Bentham defined the principle of utility as that which "approves or disapproves of every action whatsoever, according to the tendency which it appears to have to augment or diminish the happiness of the party whose interest is in question." He grounded this in a blunt claim about human nature: that pain and pleasure are our two "sovereign masters," governing everything we do.

The Principle of the Greatest Number

Bentham's fullest formulation — "the greatest happiness of the greatest number" — extends the calculation from one person's pleasure/pain to society's aggregate happiness. To make this measurable, Bentham proposed the felicific (hedonic) calculus: a method for quantitatively weighing an action's pleasure and pain by factors like intensity, duration, certainty, and proximity.

Bentham vs. Mill — Lower vs. Higher Pleasures

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) accepted Bentham's core framework but rejected his purely quantitative approach. For Bentham, a contented pig and a discontented philosopher could, in principle, score the same. Mill found this absurd.

BENTHAM · QUANTITATIVE
Lower-Pleasure Model
All pleasures count equally; only the amount (intensity × duration × certainty, etc.) matters. A simple physical pleasure and an intellectual one are compared purely by quantity.
MILL · QUALITATIVE
Higher-Pleasure Model
Some pleasures are intrinsically superior in kind, not just amount — intellectual, moral, and aesthetic pleasures outrank purely bodily ones, regardless of quantity.
PRIMARY SOURCE
Mill's Famous Line
Utilitarianism, Ch. 2 (1861)
"It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." Mill's point: a being capable of higher pleasures will never trade them away for any quantity of lower ones, even if the higher life feels less perfectly content.

Justice and Moral Rights

A standard objection to utilitarianism: if only aggregate happiness matters, could sacrificing an innocent minority ever be justified if it maximizes total welfare? Mill responded that genuine long-term utility actually requires respecting individual rights and justice as stable rules — violating them tends to undermine social trust and total happiness in the long run, even if a single case looks locally beneficial.

Terminology Glossary

Utilitarianism
The consequentialist theory that an action is right if it produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number.
Consequentialism
The broader family of theories judging actions solely by their outcomes.
Hedonic/Felicific Calculus
Bentham's method for quantitatively measuring pleasure and pain to compare actions.
Greatest Happiness Principle
Bentham's fundamental axiom: the measure of right and wrong is the greatest happiness of the greatest number.
Higher/Lower Pleasures
Mill's distinction between intellectual/moral pleasures (higher) and bodily pleasures (lower), differing in kind not just amount.

CHAPTER 06 · WEEKS 10–11
Natural Law

The second named theory, and the oldest — grounding morality not in outcomes but in human nature's built-in purpose. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) is the central figure.

Introduction to Aquinas

Thomas Aquinas, a Dominican friar and theologian, synthesized Aristotelian philosophy with Christian theology in his massive work the Summa Theologica (1265–1273). His treatise on law remains the foundational text of natural law theory in Western ethics.

Motivating Natural Law — The Euthyphro Dilemma & Divine Command Theory

Before Aquinas's own theory, it helps to see the problem it's solving. Plato's Euthyphro dialogue (c. 400 BCE) poses a dilemma still cited today: is an act good because God commands it, or does God command it because it's already good?

HORN 1
"Good because commanded"
Makes morality entirely dependent on God's will — but then morality looks arbitrary. If God had commanded cruelty, would cruelty be good?
HORN 2
"Commanded because good"
Preserves morality as non-arbitrary — but now goodness exists independently of God, which many find theologically uncomfortable (God becomes a messenger, not the source).

Aquinas takes the second horn — but instead of leaving God "superfluous," he grounds goodness in God's own unchanging rational nature, then builds Natural Law Theory to explain how humans access that goodness through reason rather than direct revelation alone.

Natural Law Theory — The Four Kinds of Law

PRIMARY SOURCE
Aquinas's Definition of Law
Summa Theologica, Prima Secundae, Q. 90
Aquinas defines law generally as "an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the community, and promulgated." Everything that follows — all four kinds of law — has to satisfy this basic definition.
1. Eternal Law
God's rational plan governing the entire universe — timeless, and the source from which all other law derives.
2. Natural Law
Humanity's rational participation in the Eternal Law, discoverable through reason alone, without needing revelation. Its first precept: "do good and avoid evil."
3. Human Law
Laws made by civil governments, derived from and constrained by Natural Law — a human law that contradicts Natural Law is, for Aquinas, not truly a law at all.
4. Divine Law
Law revealed directly through Scripture — the Old Law (Old Testament) and New Law (New Testament) — guiding humans toward their supernatural end.

Summary of Aquinas's Natural Law Theory

The four laws form a hierarchy, all ultimately grounded in Eternal Law. Human reason gives everyone access to basic moral truth through Natural Law — you don't need to be religious to grasp that murder is wrong, because that truth is written into human nature itself and accessible to reason. This is precisely what makes Natural Law Theory usable in secular ethical debates (like the same-sex marriage case analysis your syllabus assigns) even outside explicitly religious contexts.

Terminology Glossary

Natural Law
The theory that morality is grounded in human nature's inherent, rationally-discoverable purpose.
Eternal Law
God's timeless rational plan governing the whole universe; the source of all other law.
Divine Law
Law revealed through sacred scripture, guiding humans to their supernatural end.
Human Law
Civil laws made by governments, valid only insofar as they align with Natural Law.
Divine Command Theory
The view that an act's morality is determined entirely by God's command.
Euthyphro Dilemma
Plato's challenge: is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it's good?

CHAPTER 07 · WEEKS 12–13
Deontology

The third named theory — Immanuel Kant's (1724–1804) duty-based ethics, arguably the most rigorously logical of the five, and the most commonly misunderstood.

Good Will, Duty, and Agency

Kant opens his 1785 Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals with a striking claim: nothing in the world — not even talent or happiness — is good without qualification except a good will. An action has moral worth only when done from duty, not merely in accordance with duty for some other motive (like self-interest or inclination).

Autonomy vs. Heteronomy

SELF-LEGISLATED
Autonomy
Acting according to a moral law you rationally give to yourself. For Kant, this is the only genuine source of moral worth — you follow the law because reason itself demands it.
EXTERNALLY IMPOSED
Heteronomy
Acting according to a law imposed from outside yourself — by desire, social pressure, or even divine command. For Kant, this can never generate genuine moral obligation.

Universalizability — Categorical vs. Hypothetical Imperative

TypeStructureExample
Hypothetical Imperative"If you want X, do Y" — conditional on a desire"If you want to lose weight, exercise."
Categorical Imperative"Do Y" — unconditional, binding regardless of desire"Do not lie." Full stop, no "if."
PRIMARY SOURCE — FORMULA OF UNIVERSAL LAW
Kant's First Formulation
Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, 4:421 (1785)
"Act only according to that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law." The universalizability test: could everyone, everywhere, act on your same underlying rule without contradiction? If not, the act is impermissible.
PRIMARY SOURCE — FORMULA OF HUMANITY
Kant's Second Formulation
Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, 4:429 (1785)
"So act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means." People have inherent dignity, not a price — using someone purely as a tool violates this formulation even if the Formula of Universal Law is satisfied.

Terminology Glossary

Deontology
The theory that morality consists in following duty/rules, regardless of consequences.
Good Will
Kant's term for the only thing unconditionally good — willing to act from duty itself.
Maxim
The underlying personal rule or principle behind a specific action.
Autonomy
Acting according to a self-given, rational moral law.
Heteronomy
Acting according to a law imposed by something external to reason (desire, authority).
Categorical Imperative
An unconditional moral command binding regardless of personal desire.
Hypothetical Imperative
A conditional command dependent on wanting some particular outcome.
Universalizability
The test of whether a maxim could be willed as a rule for all rational agents without contradiction.

Common Misconception

Deontology is NOT simply "follow the rules of your society" or "follow the law." Kant's categorical imperative is a rational test you apply to any proposed maxim — it can just as easily condemn an unjust law as endorse a just custom. Confusing Kantian duty with mere rule-following or social conformity is a common exam trap.

CHAPTER 08 · WEEK 14
Virtue Ethics

The fourth named theory, and the oldest of the five in origin — Aristotle (384–322 BCE) asks not "what rule should I follow?" but "what kind of person should I become?"

Happiness and Ultimate Purpose

Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics opens by noting that every action aims at some good, and asks what the single highest good — the one thing pursued for its own sake and never as a means to something else — actually is. His answer: eudaimonia, usually translated "happiness" but better understood as human flourishing — not a fleeting feeling, but the active, excellent exercise of one's rational capacities over a complete life.

PRIMARY SOURCE
Aristotle on Happiness as Final End
Nicomachean Ethics, Book I
"Happiness, then, is something final and self-sufficient, and is the end of action." Unlike wealth or honor — which we pursue for the sake of something further — eudaimonia is chosen purely for its own sake.

Virtue as Excellence (Arete)

Virtue (arete, "excellence") is a stable character trait enabling a person to perform the right action, in the right way, for the right reasons, consistently. Crucially, Aristotle insists virtues are not innate — they're acquired through habituation: you become brave by repeatedly acting bravely, just as you become a builder by building.

Moral Virtue and Mesotes (The Doctrine of the Mean)

Aristotle's most distinctive claim: every moral virtue is a mean between two vices — one of excess, one of deficiency.

Vice of DeficiencyVirtue (the Mean)Vice of Excess
CowardiceCourageRecklessness
StinginessGenerosityWastefulness
Self-deprecationProper prideVanity
ApathyGood temperIrascibility
PRIMARY SOURCE
Aristotle's Definition of Virtue
Nicomachean Ethics, Book II
"Virtue, then, is a state that decides, consisting in a mean, the mean relative to us, which is defined by reference to reason, that is to say, to the reason by reference to which the prudent person would define it." The "mean" isn't a rigid mathematical midpoint — it's relative to the individual and situation, judged by phronesis (practical wisdom).

Terminology Glossary

Virtue Ethics
The theory centering moral evaluation on character rather than rules or consequences.
Eudaimonia
Human flourishing/happiness as the highest good — a life of excellent rational activity, not a fleeting feeling.
Arete
Greek for "excellence" or virtue — an excellent, stable character trait.
Mesotes
The "doctrine of the mean" — moral virtue as a balance point between excess and deficiency.
Phronesis
Practical wisdom — the capacity to judge what the virtuous mean actually is in a specific situation.
Habituation
Aristotle's account of how virtue is acquired — through repeated virtuous action, not instruction alone.

CHAPTER 09 · WEEK 15
Justice as Fairness

The fifth and final named theory — John Rawls (1921–2002) asks not what's virtuous or dutiful, but what rules a rational person would agree to if they didn't know their own place in society.

The Original Position

Rawls's central thought experiment, from A Theory of Justice (1971): imagine a group of rational people tasked with designing society's basic rules — but placed behind a veil of ignorance. They know general facts about economics and psychology, but nothing about their own race, class, gender, talents, or even their personal conception of the good life.

THE MECHANISM
Why the Veil of Ignorance Works
A Theory of Justice (1971), summarized
Not knowing where you'll land in society, the rational strategy is to protect the worst-off position — because that position might turn out to be yours once the veil lifts. This converts pure self-interest into genuine impartiality without requiring anyone to be altruistic.

Principles of Justice

FIRST PRINCIPLE · PRIORITY
Equal Liberty
Each person has an equal right to the most extensive basic liberties compatible with the same liberties for everyone else — free speech, conscience, the right to vote, etc. This principle cannot be traded away for economic gain, even if doing so would supposedly benefit everyone.
SECOND PRINCIPLE
The Difference Principle
Social and economic inequalities are only justified if they (a) work to the greatest benefit of the least-advantaged members of society, and (b) are attached to positions genuinely open to all under fair equality of opportunity.
📌
Exam-Critical Fact — Lexical Priority

The two principles aren't weighed against each other freely — Rawls orders them strictly: the Liberty Principle must be fully satisfied before the Difference Principle even applies. A society cannot justify restricting basic liberties by claiming it produces better economic outcomes for the poor.

Terminology Glossary

Justice as Fairness
Rawls's theory that just principles are those rational people would agree to from an impartial starting position.
Original Position
Rawls's thought experiment: a hypothetical starting point for choosing society's basic rules impartially.
Veil of Ignorance
The device stripping parties in the Original Position of knowledge of their own race, class, gender, and talents.
Liberty Principle
Rawls's first principle: equal basic liberties for all, non-negotiable.
Difference Principle
Rawls's second principle: inequalities are just only if they benefit the least advantaged.

CHAPTER 10 · WEEK 16
Moral Deliberation

With all five theories now covered, this chapter turns to the psychology of moral choice itself — how people actually develop the capacity for it, and what role emotion plays alongside reason.

Six Stages of Moral Development

Psychologist Lawrence Kohlberg's influential model (built on Piaget) describes moral reasoning developing through six stages across three levels.

LevelStageReasoning Focus
Preconventional1 — Obedience/PunishmentAvoid punishment
2 — Self-InterestPersonal reward/benefit
Conventional3 — Good Boy/Nice GirlGain others' approval
4 — Law and OrderFollow rules, maintain social order
Postconventional5 — Social ContractLaws as revisable agreements serving rights
6 — Universal PrinciplesSelf-chosen, abstract ethical principles (justice, dignity) that may override law
📌
Exam-Critical Fact — Most People Don't Reach Stage 6

Kohlberg's own research suggests only 10–15% of adults reason consistently at the postconventional level (stages 5–6) at all — most moral reasoning, even among adults, operates at the conventional level (stages 3–4). This is why "law and order" reasoning ("it's illegal, so it's wrong") is a stage 4 answer, not automatically a sophisticated one.

Feelings in Moral Deliberation

Moral reasoning is not purely cold calculation — emotions like guilt, empathy, disgust, and compassion play a genuine role in how people actually deliberate, sometimes tracking moral truth faster than explicit reasoning can, and sometimes distorting it. Recognizing your own emotional reaction to a dilemma is itself a data point worth examining, not something to suppress entirely in favor of "pure" logic.

Moral Problems: Self, Society, and Environment

Moral dilemmas rarely stay contained to one sphere — a personal choice (whether to travel during a pandemic) ripples into society (public health outcomes) and sometimes the environment (resource use, pollution). The syllabus's own "Travel Prohibition in this Time of Pandemic" case analysis is designed precisely to force you to trace a single personal decision across all three spheres at once, using Ramon Reyes's cross-points from Chapter 04 as the analytical tool.

Terminology Glossary

Preconventional Level
Kohlberg's earliest level — morality driven by avoiding punishment or gaining reward.
Conventional Level
Morality driven by social approval and maintaining rules/order.
Postconventional Level
Morality driven by self-chosen, abstract ethical principles that may transcend existing law.
Moral Deliberation
The reasoning-and-feeling process of working through a moral problem toward a decision.

CHAPTER 11 · WEEK 17
The Seven-Step Ethical Decision Model

The course's capstone — a practical procedure for applying everything from Chapters 00–10 to a real, messy, multi-stakeholder problem. This is also your final project's required structure, so treat this chapter as a template, not just theory.

1
The Facts
Establish immediate and long-term prospects, the demography of those involved (age, sex, economic standing, education), and the concrete human impact — fear, lost income, eroded trust — before any ethical analysis begins.
2
The Ethical Issues
Name the specific value conflicts at stake: what type of solution is appropriate? What approach (force, diplomacy, dialogue) earns genuine cooperation rather than mere compliance? Who else should have a voice in the decision?
3
The Alternatives
List every realistic option on the table — education, justice-system reform, economic reform, social ID systems, community watch, capital punishment, martial law — without prematurely ruling any out.
4
The Stakeholders
Identify everyone affected by each alternative, then rank their claims. Whose interests carry the most weight, and why — not just who has the loudest voice.
5
The Ethics of the Alternatives
Run each remaining alternative through the five theories from Chapters 05–09 — this is where the whole semester's theory actually gets used — then choose the best-supported alternative.
6
The Practical Constraints
Can the ethically best alternative actually be implemented? Distinguish constraints that are merely practical (cost, logistics) from ones that are genuinely ethical (the option itself is wrong) — the two are easy to conflate.
7
Actions to Take
Enact the alternative that survives both the ethical and practical tests — including the concrete steps needed to carry it out effectively, not just the decision in the abstract.

Terminology Glossary

Stakeholder
Any person or group affected by a decision's outcome, whose interests deserve consideration.
Practical Constraint
A real-world limitation (cost, logistics, feasibility) on implementing an otherwise ethical choice.
Ethical Decision Model
A structured, repeatable procedure for working through a moral problem toward a justified action.

Closing Synthesis — Connecting All Eleven Chapters

Trace the full arc: ethics begins with a question about rules and social life (Ch. 01), gets distinguished from law, culture, and religion (Ch. 02), gains a way to grade moral seriousness (Ch. 03), and locates a genuine moral agent — shaped but not fully determined by four cross-points (Ch. 04). That agent then needs actual standards to reason with — five of them, each asking a genuinely different question: does it maximize happiness (Ch. 05, Utilitarianism), align with human nature's purpose (Ch. 06, Natural Law), pass the test of universal law (Ch. 07, Deontology), reflect virtuous character (Ch. 08, Virtue Ethics), or survive impartial agreement behind a veil of ignorance (Ch. 09, Justice as Fairness). Understanding how that agent actually develops the capacity to reason this way at all (Ch. 10) sets up the final, practical payoff: a seven-step procedure (Ch. 11) that forces you to actually use all five theories together on one real problem, rather than reciting them in isolation. That synthesis — not memorized definitions — is what both your final exam and your final project are actually testing.
