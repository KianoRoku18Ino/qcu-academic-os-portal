CHAPTER 00
Overview & Study Priority Map

This module asks a single organizing question: what actually makes something moral — and how is that different from what makes something legal, religiously required, culturally normal, or personally preferred? The lesson works through five candidate answers in sequence — law, religion, culture (cultural relativism), personal opinion (subjectivism), and self-interest (psychological/ethical egoism) — showing a problem with each, before closing with Plato's Myth of Gyges and Socrates' own answer in Republic Book IV: that being ethical is a matter of inner harmony, not outer reward.

🧭
Added — Organizing Framework for This Guide

The professor's slides don't name this structure explicitly, but the deck's own ordering forms a clear "five candidates, five problems" progression: Law → Religion → Culture → Subjectivism → Egoism, each eliminated as a complete account of morality, followed by Socrates' positive answer. This guide's chapters follow that same order and refer back to it — use it as your mental map of the whole module, not just chapter titles in isolation.

CHAPTER 01
Morality, Law & Religion

The module opens by separating morality from two systems people often confuse it with: law and religion. Both regulate behavior, but neither is identical to morality.

Morality vs. Law

MoralityLaw
About what to do in a certain situation.Does not tell us what to do, only what to avoid.
What is moral could be legal.What is legal is not necessarily moral.

Morality vs. Religion — the Euthyphro Exchange

PRIMARY SOURCE
Socrates vs. Religion, on the source of "holy"
Plato, Euthyphro
Socrates: "Is it holy in itself and that is why it is loved by the gods?"
Religion's answer (Euthyphro): "What is holy is loved by the gods."
✚
ADDED

"Euthyphro" here is both the name of the person Socrates is questioning and the title of the Platonic dialogue this exchange comes from. This exact contrast — is something good because a god commands it, or does a god command it because it's already good? — is famous enough in philosophy to have its own name: the Euthyphro Dilemma. The slide doesn't use that term, but it's worth knowing if a professor asks "what is this argument usually called?"

A second slide pairs two visuals with no accompanying text: a "Concrete vs. Abstract Nouns" diagram (apple/tree/book vs. love/peace/idea) next to a "Top-Down" diagram (God → King → People). No claim sentence connects them in the source file.

❓
Under-Narrated Slide — Ask Your Professor

Because this slide has visuals only and no supporting text, this guide is not guessing at the intended point (per your instructions, nothing is being invented here). If this pairing shows up in recitation, ask your professor directly what conclusion the two images are meant to support — likely something about morality dealing in abstract, self-justifying concepts versus religion's top-down, authority-derived structure, but that's a reasonable guess, not something stated in the material.

Recitation Drill — Chapter 01

1
Q: Give one sentence that separates morality from law.
A: Law only tells you what to avoid (prohibitions with penalties); morality tells you what to do — and something can be legal without being moral, or moral without being codified into law.
2
Q: What is the Euthyphro Dilemma asking?
A: Whether something is good because God commands it (making morality dependent on divine will), or whether God commands it because it is already good (making morality independent of, and prior to, religious authority).
CHAPTER 02
Morality and Culture: Cultural Relativism

This is the most heavily assessed chapter in the module — it is tested directly in the matching quiz and is the subject of Quiz B's first essay question.

Definition

DEFINITION · SOURCE
Cultural Relativism
Rachels, James (2012). Elements of Moral Philosophy. 7th Edition. Boston, McGraw-Hill, 16. (citation flagged — see Ch. 09)
"Cultural Relativism says, in effect, that there is no such thing as universal truth in ethics; there are only the various cultural codes, and nothing more. Cultural Relativism challenges our belief in the objectivity and universality of moral truth."

Case Study 1 — Darius and the Callatians

Darius (c. 550 BCE – d. October 486 BCE — precise date verified, see Ch. 09), a king of ancient Persia, was intrigued by the variety of cultures he encountered in his travels. The Callatians, a tribe of Indians, traditionally ate the bodies of their deceased fathers, out of respect and a desire for their father's spirit to live on in them — whereas the Greeks cremated their dead.

PRIMARY SOURCE
Rachels on "correct" vs. "incorrect" customs
Illustrated with the Hadzabe Tribe, East Africa
"These customs cannot be said to be 'correct' or 'incorrect,' for that implies that we have an independent standard of right and wrong by which they may be judged."

Case Study 2 — Infanticide Among Eskimos

Until recently, certain Eskimo groups were reported to practice female infanticide in the belief that the time spent nursing a girl would delay the mother's next opportunity to bear a son — males being preferred for their future role as providers in a hunting economy.

Case Study 3 — Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

FGM (or Excision) involves the partial or total removal of external female genitalia, or other injury to the female genital organs, for non-medical reasons. It is known to be practiced among certain communities in 29 African countries (figure verified — see Ch. 09 for an important update note): Benin, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Central African Republic, Chad, Côte d'Ivoire, DR Congo, Djibouti, Egypt, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Kenya, Liberia, Mali, Mauritania, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, Sierra Leone, Somalia, Sudan, Tanzania, Togo, Uganda, and Zambia.

Rationalizations offered within some practicing communities, per the lesson material:

  • Women incapable of sexual pleasure are seen as less likely to be promiscuous — fewer unwanted pregnancies among unmarried women.
  • Wives for whom sex is only a duty are seen as less likely to cheat, and more attentive to husbands and children.
  • Unexcised women are, in the view described, considered unclean and immature by the men in these communities.

The lesson also documents organized resistance to the practice — including the "Alale Alternative Rite of Passage" campaign (World Vision, Alale community, 2022) promoting an end to FGM and child marriage.

The Appeal of Cultural Relativism

01
Conforms to Experience
Different societies visibly do have different moral codes — relativism matches what we actually observe.
02
Gives a Basis for Valuation
As a standard, it provides a working basis for making value judgments at all.
03
Promotes Tolerance
Tolerance of other cultures follows naturally if no single code can claim superiority.
04
Denies "Universal Truth"
There are no moral truths that hold for all peoples at all times — a strong, clean philosophical claim.

Criticisms Against Cultural Relativism

01
Can't Criticize Other Cultures
If relativism is true, no one can ever call another culture's practice wrong anymore — including practices like FGM above.
02
No Right Answers
Different moral codes conflict directly — relativism offers no way to say which, if either, is correct.
03
"My Culture" Is Unclear
What exactly counts as "my culture" for someone who belongs to more than one, or none cleanly?

Recitation Drill — Chapter 02

1
Q: In one sentence, define Cultural Relativism.
A: The view that there is no universal moral truth — only the various cultural codes, so a practice's morality must be judged relative to the culture it occurs in.
2
Q: Give the Darius/Callatian example in one sentence and say what it's meant to show.
A: Callatians ate their dead fathers out of respect while Greeks cremated theirs — used to show that even something as basic as respect for the dead is expressed through opposite customs, seemingly with no external standard to rank one as more correct.
3
Q: What is the strongest criticism of Cultural Relativism, and why does it matter for cases like FGM?
A: That it removes our ability to call any cultural practice wrong — which becomes uncomfortable fast once the practice in question causes serious, documented harm, as with FGM.
CHAPTER 03
Morality and Subjectivism

Subjectivism relocates the standard of morality from culture (an external group) to the individual: "The individual thinking person (the subject) is at the heart of all valuations." The lesson presents four subjectivist claims, each immediately paired with a counter-argument.

CLAIM 1
"No one can tell me what is right and wrong."

Counter: We can be mistaken, and we can be corrected by others.

CLAIM 2
"No one knows my situation better than myself."

Counter: Many human experiences are common, and others may have something useful to suggest.

CLAIM 3
"I am entitled to my own opinion."

Counter: Closed-mindedness rightly invites censure from someone trying to think more critically about values.

CLAIM 4
"It is good if I say that it is good."

Counter: What is my basis for saying that it is good?

Recitation Drill — Chapter 03

1
Q: Define Moral Subjectivism.
A: The belief that moral judgments depend entirely on the individual's own feelings, opinions, or attitudes — the person is the final standard.
2
Q: Give the strongest single counter-argument to subjectivism as a whole.
A: If morality is purely a matter of personal opinion with no outside standard, we could never say anyone is mistaken — but we clearly do correct people's moral views and expect them to reconsider, which subjectivism can't easily account for.
CHAPTER 04
Psychological Egoism vs. Ethical Egoism

This chapter is the second essay topic in Quiz B. The core distinction the essay tests is descriptive vs. normative — what people do versus what people ought to do.

DESCRIPTIVE — AN "IS" CLAIM
Psychological Egoism
"The ego or self has its desires and interests, and all our actions are geared toward satisfying these interests." A claim about what people actually, factually do.
NORMATIVE — AN "OUGHT" CLAIM
Ethical Egoism
"We should make our own ends, our own interests, as the single overriding concern." A claim about what people should do, regardless of what they currently do.

Counterexamples the Lesson Offers

Against pure self-interest, the material presents three examples of apparently other-directed action: Generosity ("we do things that are directed toward the other" — illustrated with a child sharing ice cream with a dog), Charity (giving food to needy people), and Hospitality (a Papua New Guinea community welcoming a visitor).

✚
ADDED — USEFUL FOR ESSAY 2

A committed psychological egoist has a standard reply the slides don't spell out: that even generosity secretly serves the giver's self-interest (it feels good, builds reputation, relieves guilt). This is exactly the tension Quiz B's Essay 2 asks you to untangle — a student helping a classmate because it makes them feel good does not automatically prove either that psychological egoism is true, or that the act is "morally selfish" in the everyday sense. Feeling good as a side effect of helping is different from feeling good being the only reason for helping — that distinction is the heart of a strong Essay 2 answer.

Recitation Drill — Chapter 04

1
Q: In one line, what's the difference between psychological and ethical egoism?
A: Psychological egoism describes what people actually do (act from self-interest); ethical egoism prescribes what people ought to do (act from self-interest) — descriptive vs. normative.
2
Q: Name the three "other-directed" counterexamples from the lesson.
A: Generosity, Charity, and Hospitality.
CHAPTER 05
The Myth of Gyges

Plato's Republic Bk. II, 359c–360d — Plato's friend Glaucon tells a story about a magical ring that grants invisibility, arguing this thought experiment reveals people's true nature once the fear of getting caught is removed.

THE STORY
Gyges the Shepherd
Plato, Republic, Book II
Given a ring, a shepherd named Gyges becomes invisible and anonymous. Through his invisibility he seduces a queen, kills her king, and takes over the kingdom. Plato argues that the Ring of Gyges — invisibility and anonymity — is the only barrier between a just and an unjust person.

Three Reading Quotes to Know

QUOTE 1
On why people practice justice
"...those who practice justice do so involuntarily and because they have not the power to be unjust..."
QUOTE 2
On what people believe in their hearts
"For all men believe in their hearts that injustice is far more profitable to the individual than justice..."
QUOTE 3
On unchecked power
"No man would keep his hands off what was not his own when he could safely take what he liked out of the market or go into houses and lie with any one at his pleasure, or kill or release from prison whom he would, and in all respects be like a God among men."

Discussion Prompts From the Lesson

  • Are you a decent person? What if you suddenly gained incredible power?
  • Are humans inherently selfish? Are we inherently compassionate?
  • If you had the ring of Gyges, what would you like to do?
  • What "immoral" acts might you commit if you knew you would not be caught?
  • Are there any things you still would not do even if you would get away with it? What are they, and why not?

Recitation Drill — Chapter 05

1
Q: What does the ring of Gyges do, and what does Glaucon use it to argue?
A: It makes the wearer invisible. Glaucon argues that if anyone had it and could act with total impunity, they would behave unjustly — meaning people are just only because they lack the power to get away with injustice, not because justice is good in itself.
2
Q: Where in Plato's Republic does this story appear?
A: Book II, around Stephanus sections 359–360 (cited on the slide as 359c–360d; see Ch. 09 for a note on citation variation).
CHAPTER 06
Does It Make Sense to Be Ethical?

This is Socrates' direct reply to Glaucon's challenge from Chapter 05, drawn from Book IV of Plato's Republic.

SOCRATES' ANSWER
Harmony of the Soul
Socrates c. 470 BCE – 399 BCE · Plato's Republic, Book IV
The good human life stems from a proper harmony of the parts of the soul. Harmony requires a certain ordering — a hierarchical system in which reason, as the "highest" part, is in charge, dutifully followed by the "lower" parts of the soul: will and appetite.
PROPER ORDER = JUSTICE
Reason on top, appetites at the base
"The presence of such an internal ordering that one consciously strives to accomplish is what it means for justice to be present in the individual."
REVERSED ORDER = INJUSTICE
Appetites on top, reason at the base
"The absence of order or the lack of harmony, with desires and appetites running rampant, results in acts of injustice."
✚
ADDED — WORTH KNOWING FOR FULL MARKS

The text on this slide correctly names three parts of the soul — reason, will, and appetite — which matches the standard Platonic "tripartite soul" (reason, spirit/thymos, and appetite). The two triangle diagrams, however, only visually label two of the three ("reason" and "appetites") — the middle "will/spirit" part isn't shown in the diagram itself. This is a visual simplification, not a factual error in the lesson; if asked to name all the parts of the soul, give three, not two.

The Module's Final Answer

Does it make sense to be ethical? Yes. Why? Because to be ethical means doing justice to oneself — an internally ordered, reason-led soul is what justice in the individual actually is, independent of whether anyone is watching (directly answering Glaucon's ring-of-Gyges challenge from Chapter 05).

Recitation Drill — Chapter 06

1
Q: What is Socrates' answer to "does it make sense to be ethical"?
A: Yes — because being ethical means doing justice to oneself: keeping reason in charge over one's own will and appetites, producing an internally harmonious soul, regardless of external reward or punishment.
2
Q: How does this directly answer Glaucon's Ring of Gyges challenge?
A: Glaucon argued justice is only kept up out of fear of consequences; Socrates counters that justice is a state of one's own soul, valuable in itself — so it still "makes sense" even for someone who could act unjustly and get away with it.

CHAPTER 09
Source Verification & Discrepancy Notes

Every date, statistic, and citation above was checked against independent sources. Nothing in the earlier chapters was altered to match these findings — the lesson content stays exactly as your professor presented it. This chapter is where any discrepancy is flagged separately, as requested.

Rachels citation
The slide cites the publisher location as "Boston". The actual copyright page of the 7th edition lists McGraw-Hill, New York, NY as the publisher's address (copyright dated 2012, matching the slide's year exactly). The year is correct; the city is the only discrepancy, and it's a minor citation detail that doesn't affect the content.
Darius's dates
The slide's range "550–486 BCE" is correct. Independently verified: Darius I ("Darius the Great") was born c. 550 BCE (no more precise date exists in the historical record) and died specifically in October 486 BCE — a more precise death date than the slide gives, safe to cite if asked for exact figures.
James Rachels's dates
The slide's "(1941–2003)" is correct. Full precise dates, independently verified: May 30, 1941 – September 5, 2003 (Columbus, Georgia – Birmingham, Alabama).
FGM "29 countries" figure
This exact count and list is a verified, word-for-word match to an official UNFPA FAQ page as written. However, UNFPA's current published data (checked live) has since been revised upward — recent UNFPA/UN Women figures cite 33 African countries and up to 94–92 countries worldwide (including regions in Asia, the Middle East, and Europe not on the original slide). This is not an error in your professor's slide — it accurately reflects the source at the time it was written — but if a professor or exam asks for "current" figures, the numbers have moved.
Tripartite soul diagram
Covered in Chapter 06 — the accompanying text correctly names three parts of the soul (reason, will, appetite), but the triangle diagrams only visually label two. A simplification, not an error.
Myth of Gyges citation
The slide cites "Republic (359c–360d)." Independently verified: scholarly sources cite this passage across a range of very similar but not identical Stephanus spans (359b–360b, 359d–360c, 359d–360d, among others, depending on translation/edition). The slide's citation falls within normal variation and is not considered an error.
