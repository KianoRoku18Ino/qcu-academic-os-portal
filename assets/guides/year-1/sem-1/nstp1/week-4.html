THE ROLE OF FOLLOWER
The Role of Follower

Leaders and Followers

We are convinced that corporations succeed or fail, compete or crumble, on the basis of how well they are led. So we study great leaders of the past and present, and we spend vast quantities of time and money looking for leaders to hire and trying to cultivate leadership in the employees we already have.

But leadership is only half the equation for organizational success. The other half is followership — the flip side of the coin. Bosses are not necessarily good leaders; subordinates are not necessarily effective followers. Many bosses couldn't lead a horse to water. Many subordinates couldn't follow a parade.

Effective vs. Ineffective Followers

What distinguishes an effective follower from an ineffective one is enthusiastic, intelligent, and self-reliant participation — without star billing — in the pursuit of an organizational goal. Effective followers differ from ineffective ones in their level of active, independent, critical thinking, and in their degree of active engagement, and are also distinguished by whether or not they are dependably or passively obedient.

Ineffective followers are often described as sheep — placid, passive, tractable, and lacking in initiative — or as "yes people" — deferential, submissive, and unimaginative. Neither type takes an active role, or thinks for themselves, or challenges their bosses' judgment.

Two Behavioral Dimensions

Kelley's model plots followers along two dimensions:

  • Independent, critical thinking vs. dependent, uncritical thinking — the degree to which a follower thinks for themselves rather than looking to the leader to do the thinking for them.
  • Active vs. passive — the degree to which a follower actively engages and takes initiative, rather than waiting to be told what to do.

Kelley's Model of Follower Behavior (as captioned in the lesson)
PATTERN
Alienated Followers

Independent, critical thinkers, but passive in a group. Often a once-effective follower who became disillusioned by something/someone in the group.

PATTERN
Effective Followers

Independent, critical thinkers who are also active in the group. Have the courage to initiate change and put themselves at risk/conflict with other members, including their leaders, to serve the group's best interests.

PATTERN
Sheep

Passive and uncritical thinkers who lack initiative and a sense of responsibility. They perform the tasks given to them and stop.

PATTERN
Yes People

Active but dependent, uncritical thinkers. Positively reinforce and support the opinions of the leader without much of an original thought of their own.

🎯
Survivors

Sit at the center of the model. They are the "middle-of-the-roaders" who are constantly, sometimes randomly, sampling the actions of the other four extremes and are adept at surviving change.

Followership
Plain English: the "other half" of leadership — how well a subordinate performs their role, independent of how good their boss is.
vs. Leadership: Leadership is about how well someone directs others; Followership is about how well someone performs as a subordinate, and the two skill sets don't automatically come together in the same person.
Example from the lesson: "Many bosses couldn't lead a horse to water. Many subordinates couldn't follow a parade" — both roles can fail independently.
KEY CONCEPT
Effective Follower
Plain English: a follower who thinks for themselves and actively participates, rather than just doing as told.
vs. Yes People: both are active/engaged, but Yes People don't think critically for themselves, while Effective Followers do.
Example from the lesson: "enthusiastic, intelligent, and self-reliant participation... without star billing."

THE QUALITIES OF FOLLOWERS
The Qualities of Followers

According to the lesson, effective followers share four essential qualities:

QUALITY 1
Self-Management

The key to being an effective follower is the ability to think for oneself — to exercise control and independence and to work without close supervision. Effective followers are people to whom a leader can safely delegate responsibility, people who anticipate needs at their own initiative.

QUALITY 2
Commitment

Effective followers are committed to something — a cause, a product, an organization, an idea — in addition to the care of their own lives and careers. They build their competence and focus their efforts for maximum impact, and they are perceived as bringing energy, dedication, and a high standard of excellence to their work.

QUALITY 3
Competence and Focus

Effective followers master skills useful to their organization, hold higher standards than required, and their competence and focus often exceed job requirements.

QUALITY 4
Courage

Effective followers establish themselves as independent, critical thinkers whose knowledge and judgment can be trusted, and they hold to their own set of values, maintaining their honesty and credibility even when it means going against the group or their leader.

Recitation-Ready Answer Added — Study Aid

KEY CONCEPT
Self-Management
Plain English: being able to work well without someone constantly checking on you.
vs. Courage: Self-Management is about how you handle your own work independently; Courage is specifically about standing your ground when your honest judgment conflicts with the group or leader.
Example from the lesson: being "people to whom a leader can safely delegate responsibility."
KEY CONCEPT
Commitment
Plain English: caring about something bigger than just your own paycheck or career.
vs. Competence and Focus: Commitment is about what you care about (a cause, idea, organization); Competence and Focus is about the skill level you bring to executing on that commitment.
Example from the lesson: being committed "to something — a cause, a product, an organization, an idea."

CULTIVATING EFFECTIVE FOLLOWERS
Cultivating Effective Followers

The Same Qualities as Leaders

Notice that the qualities that distinguish effective followers are the same ones needed to be a good leader — self-management, commitment, competence, courage. This challenges our conventional stereotype of followers as passive order-takers.

Redefining Followership and Leadership

If we agree that a leader's job is to transform followers, then it must be a follower's job — see Chapter 00, Note 3 for the unclear original wording of this sentence. What is clear from the lesson's framing is that followership and leadership are meant to be understood as complementary, interacting roles rather than fixed, opposite personality types.

Honing Followership Skills

Most followership training efforts rest on three faulty premises:

  1. That leaders are different kinds of people from followers, "born" and not "made."
  2. That followers are, by nature, inferior to leaders in drive, intellect, or vision.
  3. That followership is a role people can be conditioned to accept passively, rather than an active skill to be developed.

Organizations wishing to develop effective followers should instead focus training on the same skills used to cultivate leaders — the qualities from Chapter 02 (self-management, commitment, competence and focus, courage) applied from the follower's own vantage point.

Performance Evaluation and Feedback

Just as leaders are evaluated and given feedback on their leadership performance, effective organizations extend the same evaluation and feedback practices to how well employees perform as followers, reinforcing that followership is a skill worth developing and recognizing in its own right.

Organizational Structures That Encourage Followership

The lesson names structures/practices organizations can use to encourage strong followership:

  • Leaderless groups — arrangements where no single person is designated leader, requiring members to exercise independent judgment and initiative.
  • Rotating leadership — leadership responsibility is passed among members, so everyone experiences both roles.
  • Delegation to the lowest level — decision-making authority is pushed down the organization rather than concentrated at the top.
  • Rewards — organizations reward good followership the same way they reward good leadership.

Leaderless Groups

vs. Rotating Leadership: Leaderless groups have no fixed leader at all; Rotating Leadership still has one leader at a time, but the role changes hands.
Example from the lesson: named as one of the organizational structures that encourages followership skills.

The Three Faulty Premises
Plain English: the wrong assumptions that make most followership training ineffective.
vs. the lesson's actual recommendation: instead of assuming followers are just naturally inferior or passive, train them in the same active skills (self-management, commitment, competence, courage) used to develop leaders.
Example from the lesson: assuming followers are "born," not "made."
