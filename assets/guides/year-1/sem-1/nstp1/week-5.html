🔍
Source Verification Notes — read before studying

1. Likely name error: the lesson states "General Jose Prim dethroned Queen Isabela II of Spain in 1868." Independent verification confirms the general who led the 1868 revolution that deposed Isabella II was Juan Prim, not "Jose Prim." Presented in Chapter 01 exactly as the lesson names him, flagged here as a likely name error.

2. Major date error: the lesson states the Japanese invasion forces "landed in Vigan and Aparri in Luzon on December 10, 1945." Independent verification confirms this landing took place on December 10, 1941 — the actual start of the Japanese invasion of the Philippines, not 1945 (by which point Japan had already surrendered, ending WWII). This is reproduced exactly as the lesson states it in Chapter 01, but is very likely a typo for "1941."

3. Unclear/unmatched date: the lesson states the Philippine Navy "was created 22 June 22, 1898 by the Revolutionary Army" (the source repeats "22" this way in the text itself). The Philippine Navy's founding is most commonly dated to May 20, 1898 in independent sources, with June 23, 1898 also appearing as the date Aguinaldo's government administratively organized naval affairs under a bureau. "June 22" does not clearly match either commonly-cited date — flagged as unverified rather than corrected.

4. Likely date error: the lesson states the Philippine Military Academy was established "on February 7, 1905." Independent verification gives the Philippine Constabulary's founding (PMA's institutional root) as February 17, 1905 — likely a dropped digit in the lesson.

5. Imprecise date: the lesson states "the war broke out between the United States and Spain on April 23, 1898." Independent verification finds no single authoritative source using April 23 specifically — Spain declared war on April 24, 1898; the U.S. Congress declared war on April 25, 1898, made retroactive to April 21, 1898. April 23 falls between these dates without matching any of them — flagged as imprecise rather than corrected.

6. Likely conflict/era error (IED deck): the lesson states the Explosively Formed Projectile (EFP) design was "used to great effect during the first Gulf War – Destroyed a US M1 Abrams MBT." Independent verification indicates Iranian-designed EFPs used against U.S. M1 Abrams tanks are a defining and extensively documented feature of the Iraq War (2003–2011), not the First Gulf War (1990–91, Operation Desert Storm) — flagged as a likely conflict mix-up.

7. Garbled text (IED deck, Instructor Profile slide): the line "DATE OF InIn Mil Srvc (CSC CL-221 on Jul 16 2009" is not legible as clean text in the source itself — reproduced as-is in Chapter 03's context box, not corrected or guessed at.

8. Scope of verification: the Philippine Military History deck contains many additional specific dates (particularly around UP's internal ROTC/DMST organizational history — field artillery units, specific dates of unit activation, etc.) that were not independently re-verified given the volume of the material; verification effort was concentrated on the most prominent, commonly-documented named events.

PART A · PHILIPPINE MILITARY HISTORY
Brief History of the AFP
27 Apr 1521
Pre-Spanish Occupation — Battle of Mactan
The first recorded organized resistance against a foreign aggressor took place in the Visayas during the Battle of Mactan, when the native chieftain Lapu-Lapu — the acknowledged father of the AFP — fought against the Spaniards led by Magellan in the latter's effort to subdue him. Lapu-Lapu refused to pay homage to the King of Spain. Underestimating the natives' capability, the foreigners lost the battle and Magellan was killed.
1570–1571
Spanish Occupation begins
Some islands of the archipelago were successfully occupied by the Spaniards. In 1570 they tried to land in Manila; the Muslim leader Rajah Soliman resisted their effort. In 1571, Legaspi conquered Manila and made it the capital of the Philippines. Pockets of rebellion followed, notably Diego Silang's uprising, displaying exemplary military leadership and tactics against the Spaniards. Filipino soldiers were also organized to fight for Spain and support expeditions — Filipino forces reinforced Spanish troops during the Chinese revolt in 1603.
1868
Spanish liberalism reaches the Philippines
General "Jose Prim" (see Ch. 00 note) dethroned Queen Isabela II of Spain, who had espoused liberal principles of democracy — paving the way for Filipino exposure to foreign culture and the growth of nationalism, further nurtured by propagandists Marcelo H. del Pilar, Graciano Lopez Jaena, and Jose Rizal.
7 Jul 1892
Founding of the Katipunan
Andres Bonifacio, considered the father of the Philippine Army, founded the more radical group called the "Katipunan."
23 Aug 1896
Cry of Pugad Lawin
Signaled the start of the Philippine Revolution against Spain, followed by pockets of rebellion inflicting heavy losses on the Spaniards. Two factions emerged within the growing Katipunan: the Magdalo (led by Aguinaldo) and the Magdiwang (led by Bonifacio).
22 Mar 1897
Tejeros Convention
Called to resolve the Magdalo/Magdiwang conflict. Aguinaldo won the presidency, and the occasion also gave birth to the Philippine Army.
23 Apr 1898 (see Ch. 00, Note 5)
American Influence begins — war breaks out
War broke out between the United States and Spain. The Americans convinced the Filipinos to cooperate against Spain, promising independence; Aguinaldo declared war on Spain.
12 Jun 1898
Philippine Independence declared
Declared in Kawit, Cavite.
22 Jun 1898 (see Ch. 00, Note 3)
Philippine Navy created
Created by the Revolutionary Army.
(undated)
Fall of the Malolos Republic
American occupation gained little acceptance among Filipinos due to many restrictions on Filipino forces' access to certain areas; harsh treatment by the Americans ignited conflict, and the Filipinos were defeated, leading to the fall of the Malolos Republic.
23 Mar 1901
Capture of General Aguinaldo
Captured by the Americans in Palanan, Isabela. With the Treaty of Paris, Spain had ceded the Philippines to the United States, prompting renewed Filipino resistance and significant battles exemplifying Filipino soldiers' fighting spirit and skill.
16 Apr 1902
General Malvar lays down arms
Ended the organized resistance against the American forces.
8 Aug 1901
Philippine Constabulary organized
An insular police force organized to hasten the Philippine campaign and establish peace and order.
7 Feb 1905 (see Ch. 00, Note 4)
Philippine Military Academy established
21 Dec 1935
National Defense Act enacted
Officially created the Armed Forces of the Philippines.
1 Jul 1947
Philippine Air Force established
10 Dec 1945 (see Ch. 00, Note 2 — very likely 1941)
The Japanese Occupation begins
Consequent to Japan's declaration of war with the United States, invading Japanese forces landed in Vigan and Aparri, Luzon. Unable to withstand the very strong adversary, the combined US-Filipino forces withdrew to Bataan to implement War Plan Orange.
9 Apr 1942
Fall of Bataan
6 May 1942
Fall of Corregidor
These events ended organized resistance against the Japanese invasion. The defeat of the Fil-Am forces did not end the armed struggle, however — those who refused to surrender went underground and waged guerilla warfare against the Japanese.
20 Oct 1944
Gen. Douglas MacArthur lands in Leyte
The guerilla forces' activities were very instrumental in the successful comeback of the liberating US forces.
early 1950s–1960s
International Peace Keeping Operations
The AFP participated in international peacekeeping efforts as its commitment to the United Nations: the Philippine Expeditionary Forces to Korea (PEFTOK) in the early 1950s, the Philippine Air Force Contingent in Congo, Africa in the early 1960s, and the Philippine Civic Action Group (PHILCAG) in South Vietnam in the 1960s. Philippine contingents were also sent to East Timor, Iraq, and Liberia for UN peacekeeping operations.

PART A · PHILIPPINE MILITARY HISTORY
Evolution and Role of the ROTC in the Previous Wars

Commonwealth Act Number 1, otherwise known as the National Defense Act, provided the legal basis for the conduct of ROTC instruction — but the lesson traces the need for a citizen reserve force back much further, even into the Spanish colonial period.

The 1762 British Invasion — ROTC's Earliest Roots

1
The Seven Years' War reaches the Philippines
A British flotilla of thirteen ships, headed by Admiral Samuel Cornish and General William Draper, arrived in the colony on September 22, 1762, as a consequence of the Seven Years' War in Europe between France and Great Britain. The Philippines got entangled because the monarchs of Spain and France both belonged to the Bourbon Dynasty.
2
Student volunteers organized
Spanish authorities were ill-prepared. Father Domingo Collantes, OP, Rector and chancellor of the University of Sto. Tomas, organized around 200 students from UST and Colegio de San Juan de Letran who underwent military training at Sto. Tomas Plaza in Intramuros, Manila — assisted by a sergeant in the Royal Spanish Army.
3
A mismatched but stubborn defense
These students, together with 500 Hispano-Filipino regulars (the King's Regiment) and 80 Filipinos, were sent to counter the 7,000-strong British Regiment. The skirmish lasted five days, with heavy casualties among the defenders.
4
Surrender, then the war ends
Governor-Archbishop Rojo surrendered Manila and Cavite to Lieutenant General Dawsonne Drake on October 6, 1762. The Philippines was a British colony until June 1764; with the Treaty of Paris signed on February 10, 1763, the Seven Years' War ended and the British left the archipelago for good.
5
"Muy Leal"
The Spanish king recognized the students' courage, granting the title "muy leal" (very loyal) to the young defenders and "regalia" (royal) to their institution. The "muy leal" emblem remains part of the UST ROTC seal to this day.

The Genuine Beginning of ROTC — 1912 Onward

1
1912 — UP and the Philippine Constabulary
Exactly 150 years after 1762, various Philippine colleges/universities began offering military training. Per Brigadier General Jose Syjuco (author of Military Education in the Philippines), most military historians mark 1912 as the beginning of genuine ROTC instruction, when the Philippine Constabulary (PC) started conducting military instruction at the University of the Philippines (UP) on the old Padre Faura campus. UP and Ateneo de Manila also began offering training in 1912, though graduates had no military career path unless they joined the PC or the Philippine Scouts (PS). Governor General Leonard Wood encouraged ROTC unit development similar to those in the US; Captain Silvino Gallardo became UP's first military instructor in the first semester of 1912.
2
17 Mar 1922 — Department of Military Science and Tactics (DMST)
Formally organized at UP with three objectives: (1) develop patriotic, physically sound, upright and disciplined citizens; (2) create a corps of trained officers for the reserve force; and (3) take the lead in fostering university spirit. On July 3, 1922, formal military instruction began with UP's first ROTC unit; basic infantry course became compulsory and a graduation prerequisite. UP's field artillery unit was organized October 26, 1929 (75mm field guns); a mounted battery unit (2.95-inch guns) followed in 1935.
3
Expansion & supervision
Ateneo de Manila, National University, Liceo de Manila, and San Juan de Letran formed their own ROTC units, independent from one another until 1936, when the Office of the Superintendent (of ROTC units) of the Philippine Army was activated to supervise all ROTC units nationally.

The Commonwealth Defense Plan

Under American tutelage, Commonwealth Act Number 1 provided the legal basis for mandatory citizen military training. The national defense plan, put into motion by General Douglas MacArthur and Manuel Quezon, envisioned a citizen army of two components: (1) a regular force of about 10,000 men (including the PC), and (2) a reserve force to number 400,000 by the end of a ten-year period, built by continuously training 21-year-old able-bodied men for a period of more than five months. Quezon personally selected MacArthur as military adviser (later conferring on him the rank of "Field Marshal," the highest known international military rank) to formulate the Philippine Defense System.

🗣️
Quezon's Address, National Assembly, 26 Nov 1935

"Self defense is the supreme right of mankind no more sacred to the individual than to the nation, the interests of which are immeasurably of greater significance and extent... In my opinion, the plan reflects the lessons of history, the conclusion of the acknowledge[d] masters of warfare and of statesmanship, and the sentiments and aspirations of the Filipino people. It is founded upon enduring principles that are fundamental to any plan applicable to our needs."

UP's ROTC was the first officially recognized, followed by Letran, UST, De La Salle, Adamson, Philippine Normal School, the Philippine School of Arts and Trades, San Beda, and Silliman. By 1937, the government had recognized seventeen ROTC units, mostly infantry; UP additionally had a field artillery unit, and Adamson and the Quisumbing schools had chemical warfare units. UP also trained ROTC instructors and was the source of basic ROTC training policy.

Under this system, male students took a basic two-year course with weekend training; those wanting a reserve commission could attend two more years of advanced weekend training, making them eligible for a reserve officer commission. Mandatory training wasn't instituted in all colleges, so some students transferred to schools without ROTC to avoid it. President Quezon issued Executive Order No. 207, making ROTC compulsory in all colleges/universities with 100 or more students. By 1941, around 33 colleges/universities maintained ROTC units — but all closed during the Japanese incursion.

ROTC After the War, and Its Modern Challenges

UP ROTC graduates continued rendering meritorious service after the war, both addressing domestic insurgency and manning contingency forces sent during the Korean (1950) and Vietnam (1964) wars. Prior to NSTP's implementation, ROTC was a two-year mandatory training for male college students and a graduation requisite. Enrollment declined steadily due to issues including graft and corruption, a lack of competent/dedicated/committed instructors, and higher student-instructor ratios — students increasingly appreciated the program less, shown by high drop-out/deferment rates and many opting for the program's other two components instead, significantly reducing the reserve force pool.

The program has drawn protests from students, school administrators, and parents who view it as militarization. The strongest clamor for its abolition occurred in March 2001, following the death of University of Santo Tomas ROTC Cadet Mark Chua, who was allegedly hazed by senior ROTC cadets after exposing malpractices in the program. As of the lesson's writing, many concerned citizens, school administrators, and legislators are advocating for enhancing the ROTC program by making it compulsory in state colleges and universities — an effort the AFP fully supports, based on a perceived decline in youth appreciation for patriotism and good citizenship.

Definition

💣
Improvised Explosive Device (IED)

A device placed or fabricated in an improvised manner, incorporating destructive, lethal, noxious, pyrotechnic, or incendiary chemicals, designed to destroy, incapacitate, harass, or distract. It may incorporate military stores, but is normally fabricated from non-military components.

The Five Components of an IED

COMPONENT 1
The Casing

Virtually anything can be used as a casing. Serves two purposes: (1) concealing the device from detection, and (2) enhancing the explosive power.

COMPONENT 2
The Explosive

Comes in two types, high and low explosive, in all shapes/sizes/granularity/color. Low explosive: black powder (from shotgun cartridges), potassium perchlorate (from fireworks and match heads). High explosive: TNT (harvested from conventional munitions), ammonium nitrate-based (from processed fertiliser), TATP (favoured in Europe; precursors freely available).

COMPONENT 3
The Power Source

Required by all IEDs except incendiary devices. Common items include 9V batteries, car batteries, motorcycle batteries, AA batteries — even stored mechanical energy such as a coiled spring.

COMPONENT 4
The Detonator / Initiator

A method/component needed to initiate the explosive. Detonators can be mechanical or electrical, contain a low explosive, and (per the lesson's UK-specific note) are hard to acquire, making an improvised detonator more likely. Initiators are different — used to "initiate" low explosives like black powder, often improvised and easy to make.

COMPONENT 5
The Trigger / Firing Switch

Every IED requires some action or input to function: a button, a switch, a timer. The IRA is noted as having often used a mercury tilt switch; mobile phones and UHF/VHF radios are favoured by IS.

📌
Additional notes from the lesson

Terrorist publications such as Inspire magazine are noted as regularly featuring scientifically-accurate "how-to" guides for manufacturing homemade explosives (this is stated in the lesson as a threat-awareness point, not reproduced here). IED Enhancements are additives/damage multipliers added to increase lethality: most commonly fragmentation (ball-bearings, nuts and bolts, steel rebar, bullets), or an accelerant to increase incendiary effect (gasoline, kerosene, pressurized containers such as gas cylinders). The lesson separately notes that Refrigerant 134a, in mixtures with high concentrations of air at elevated pressure/temperature, can be ignited, and that contact with flames or hot surfaces above 250°C (482°F) may cause vapour decomposition releasing toxic gases including hydrogen fluoride and carbonyl halides.

Named Types, Described in Detail

COMMAND
CW or RC
The IED receives a "command" that causes it to initiate. Two types: Command wire (CW) and Radio-controlled (RC) — RC is frequently mobile phones or radios; command wires are less popular, difficult to conceal, and have limited range.
VICTIM-OPERATED
VOIED
Initiated as a result of some action or motion of the victim: pressure plate, pressure release, motion sensor (PIR), tilt switch.
PERSON-BORNE
PBIED
Also known as a "suicide vest," worn around the waist/chest. Two types: Defensive (anti-capture device; example given: Bataclan theatre) and Offensive (inflict mass casualties, contains a fragmentation matrix; example given: Stade de France). IS is noted as utilizing female and child operatives to infiltrate secure locations, and as having experimented with internal PBIEDs.
VEHICLE-BORNE
VBIED
Huge destructive power — main charges up to 50 tonnes have been seen. Both suicide and non-suicide variants; used for perimeter breach or mass casualties.
🎯
EFP: Explosively Formed Projectile

Design originated from the Iranians; identifiable by the concave "liner," usually made of copper or steel. The lesson states it was "used to great effect during the first Gulf War," destroying a US M1 Abrams MBT — see Chapter 00, Note 6 regarding the likely conflict/era mix-up in this claim.

☣️
Chemical / Biological IED

Chemical and biological weapons add a "fear factor"; IS is noted as having experimented with these for years. The main difficulty is getting the explosive power correct — too low, and the agent won't disperse; too high, and the agent will be vaporized/destroyed.

Recitation-Ready Answer Added — Study Aid

KEY CONCEPT
VBIED vs. PBIED
Plain English: both can be suicide devices, but VBIED uses a vehicle as the delivery platform (huge charge, breach/mass-casualty use), while PBIED is worn on a person's body (vest-style, smaller charge).
Distinguishing them: VBIED's destructive power comes from its much larger possible charge size (up to 50 tonnes cited); PBIED relies on a fragmentation matrix and proximity to the target.
Example from the lesson: PBIED's Bataclan theatre (defensive/anti-capture) vs. Stade de France (offensive/mass casualty) examples.
KEY CONCEPT
EFP (Explosively Formed Projectile)
Plain English: a shaped device that turns a metal liner into a high-speed slug capable of punching through armor.
vs. a standard IED blast: a regular IED relies on general explosive force; an EFP is specifically engineered (concave copper/steel liner) to penetrate armored vehicles.
Example from the lesson: destroying a US M1 Abrams (see the conflict/era flag in Ch. 00).
