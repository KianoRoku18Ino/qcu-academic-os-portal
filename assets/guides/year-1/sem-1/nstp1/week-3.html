Source Verification Notes — read before studying

1. Vegetius epigraph date (Drills, p. 4): the lesson dates the opening quote "A.D. 378." Scholarly sources are genuinely split on exactly when Vegetius wrote De Re Militari — most place it around c. 390 AD, during the reign of Theodosius I (AD 379–395), while a wider range of 383–450 AD is also argued in the literature. 378 AD sits just before that commonly-cited window (it's actually the year of the Battle of Adrianople, a separate event). This isn't clearly wrong, but it isn't the date most classicists would give either — treat it as a contested date, not a settled one.

2. Archidamus of Sparta attribution (Drills, p. 55): the exact wording of this quote could not be confidently matched to one of Archidamus's own recorded speeches in Thucydides' History of the Peloponnesian War. At least one detailed page-by-page index of the History's speeches lists this specific passage separately from the (differently-worded, differently-dated) speeches it attributes to Archidamus. This attribution should be treated as unconfirmed rather than assumed correct.

3. Von Steuben "Blue Book," 1779 (Drills, p. 115): checked and accurate — Baron Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben, a former Prussian officer who had served under Frederick the Great, was commissioned by General George Washington and wrote Regulations for the Order and Discipline of the Troops of the United States ("the Blue Book"), published in 1779. No discrepancy found.

4. B.H. Liddell Hart, "Thoughts on War," 1944 (Drills, p. 26): checked and accurate — published by Faber and Faber, London, 1944. No discrepancy found.

5. Scope of verification: the Drills & Ceremonies deck contains many other embedded historical claims (e.g., the Middle Ages origins of military reviews, the 1892 US infantry drill regulation on band positioning). Given the volume of the material, verification effort was concentrated on the explicitly named, dated epigraph quotes and the one major named historical claim (Von Steuben) — the ones most likely to appear as identification questions — rather than every date buried in the historical-background prose.

6. Terminology overlap (Drills, p. 10 & p. 17): the lesson gives the same Filipino gloss, "(Linya)," for two different English drill terms — ALIGNMENT and LINE — which are defined differently in English. This may just be how the professor's source material renders both, but it's worth knowing in case a recitation question asks you to tell the two apart.

7. Core Values of Development — incomplete source (see Chapter 09): the deck's own "Scope of Presentation" slide lists six topics, but only three are actually developed with content. This gap is described in full in Chapter 09 and has not been filled in.

PART I · DRILLS · SECTION 1
General Drill Information
VEGETIUS · DE RE MILITARI — DATE AS GIVEN: A.D. 378
Opening Epigraph (Drills, p. 4)
See Source Verification Note 1 in Chapter 00 regarding the date
"Troops who march in an irregular and disorderly manner are always in great danger of being defeated."

Purpose and Scope

This portion of the manual provides guidance for AFP-wide uniformity in the conduct of drills. It includes methods of instructing drills, teaching techniques, individual and unit drills, manual of arms for infantry weapons, and various other aspects of basic drill instruction. It is designed for use by soldiers of all military occupational skill, including cadets and recruits in the initial training environment. So as to maintain consistency throughout the AFP, the procedures prescribed are to be adhered to as closely as possible.

General

The AFP prescribes its own drill regulations with the end goal of cultivating full alertness and presence of mind in each military personnel, not only during drills but at all times — attributes essential to AFP members. All movements in the manual are precision movements designed to foster proper coordination among military personnel, with teamwork as the final result. In the military service, teamwork is attained through drill and ceremonies.

Drills consist of certain movements by which the squad, platoon, company, or battalion is moved in an orderly manner from one formation to another, executed with smartness, order, and precision — each individual doing his part exactly as he is supposed to perform.

The primary purpose of drill is to teach a precise and orderly way of doing things. As drills increase skill and coordination, they accustom a soldier to respond to commands, and also promote teamwork. A soldier must know and understand a drill, or its value is lost; a drill is conducted with precision, and perfection is the only accepted standard. Drill periods are frequent and of short duration.

Everyone gets a certain amount of pleasure from doing anything well in joint effort with others — a military ceremony provides the same reaction. After every successful ceremony, every soldier-participant can proudly claim: "I was there."

PART I · DRILLS · SECTION 2
Commands and the Command Line
B.H. LIDDELL HART · THOUGHTS ON WAR, 1944 — VERIFIED
Opening Epigraph (Drills, p. 26)
London: Faber and Faber, 1944
"The spirit of discipline, as distinct from its outward and visible guises, is the result of association with martial traditions and their living embodiment."

2.1 General

A drill command is an oral order of a commander or leader; the precision of a movement is affected by how the command is given. When at halt, the commander faces the troops when giving commands. On commands that set the unit in motion, the commander moves simultaneously with the unit. When marching, the commander turns his head toward the troops to give commands. Exceptions occur during ceremonies.

When elements drill as part of a larger unit, the rules of supplementary commands apply. To revoke a preparatory command, the commander gives MANUMBALIK (AS YOU WERE) — this must be given prior to the command of execution; the commander cannot cancel the command of execution itself. If an improper command is not revoked, personnel execute the movement in the best manner possible.

2.2 Two-Part Commands

Most drill commands have two parts: the preparatory command and the command of execution. Neither is a command by itself, but both parts are termed "commands" to simplify instruction. (HANDA (READY), SIPAT (AIM), PUTOK (FIRE) is considered a two-part command even though it contains two pre-commands.)

1
Preparatory command
States the movement to be carried out and mentally prepares the soldier for its execution. Example: in PASULONG, KAD, the preparatory command is PASULONG.
2
Command of execution
Tells when the movement is to be carried out. In PASULONG, KAD, the command of execution is KAD.

To change direction while marching, both parts of the command begin and end in the direction of the turn — e.g., KANANG PANIG, KAD (RIGHT FLANK) is given as the right foot strikes the surface; the left-flank equivalent is given as the left foot strikes. The interval between the preparatory command and the command of execution is normally one step or count.

2.3 English Version to Filipino Version

AFP commands are derived from English and translated to Filipino. This localization attempt has led to varied interpretations of preciseness — words like RAP, NA, KAD, TA, and others are often misused or interchanged, and commanders sometimes use what they personally think is appropriate. To provide uniformity, simplicity, and preciseness suited to voice control, distinctiveness, inflection, and cadence, the following guidelines are set:

2.4 Combined Commands

In some commands, the preparatory command and the command of execution are combined — e.g., HUMANAY (FALL IN), PALUWAG (AT EASE), LUMANSAG (DISMISS). These are given without inflection, at a uniform high pitch and loudness comparable to a normal command of execution.

2.5 Supplementary Commands

Supplementary commands are oral orders given by a subordinate leader to reinforce and complement a commander's orders, ensuring proper understanding and execution of a movement. They extend to the lowest subordinate leader who commands a separate element within the same formation.

A supplementary command may be a preparatory command, a portion of a pre-command, or a full two-part command. It's normally given between the pre-command and the command of execution, used when an element must execute a movement different from (or at a different time than) other elements in the same formation. Example given: in a platoon in column, the leader commands DALAWAHANG TUDLING SUNUDSURAN MULA SA KALIWA (pause), KAD — the 1st/2nd squad leaders command PASULONG while the 3rd/4th command MANATILI (STAND FAST); at the appropriate time the nearest squad leader commands LIKO HATING KALIWA, KAD for the remaining squads, who then execute LIKO HATING KANAN to fall in behind.

A subordinate leader gives supplementary commands over his right shoulder, except when the command concerns an element on his left, or the sub-element executes LIKO SA KALIWA, LIKO HATING KALIWA, or KALIWANG PANIG (then given over the left shoulder — also used when changing a formation's configuration, e.g. forming a file or column of four). Note: when in formation at present arms and the preparatory command for order of arms is given, subordinate leaders terminate their salute before giving supplementary commands.

Supplementary commands are not given for the combined commands HUMANAY, PALUWAG, TIWALAG, or during mass drills when the element is part of a mass formation — but they ARE given when forming a mass, or forming a column from a mass. Except in mass formation, platoon leaders give supplementary commands following the commander's commands; on the preparatory command BALANGAY, platoon leaders come to attention and command PULUTONG. The company commander allows time for all supplementary commands before giving the command of execution.

When no direction is given, forward is understood. When no rate of march is given, SIGLANG HAKBANG (QUICK TIME) is understood — this normally applies when direction/rate is included in the pre-command or given as a supplementary command.

2.6 Mass Commands

Mass commands develop confidence and promote enthusiasm, and are effective for developing a command voice when instructing a leadership course. To use them, the commander commands SA AKING PAG-UTOS (AT MY COMMAND), gives the preparatory command for the movement, and the command of execution becomes RAP (COMMAND) — e.g. HARAP SA KANAN, RAP (COMMAND) executes RIGHT FACE in unison. While marching, KAD (COMMAND) substitutes for the command of execution, e.g. LIKO SA KANAN, KAD (COLUMN RIGHT COMMAND); PABALIK KAD (REAR COMMAND). Manual-of-arms mass commands follow the same pattern. To stop mass commands, the commander again commands SA AKING PAG-UTOS (AT MY COMMAND), and normal drill conduct resumes.

2.7 Directives

Directives are all oral orders given by the commander that direct or cause a subordinate leader to take actions. The commander gives directives rather than commands when it's more appropriate for subordinate elements to execute a movement or task as independent elements of the same formation. Directives are given in sentence form, normally prefixed by "IBUKA ANG TALUDTOD AT ITUNGKOD ANG SANDATA" (example: "ITANGHAL ANG SANDATA"). "PAMUNUAN ANG INYONG PANGKAT O TILAP, PULUTONG, BALANGAY" is the only directive where a commander relinquishes his command and salutes are exchanged. A subordinate commander, upon receiving a directive, resorts to the normal two-part command.

The Command Voice

2.8 General

Everyone in the unit will easily understand a correctly delivered command. Correct commands have a tone, cadence, and snap that demands willing, correct, and immediate response.

2.9 Voice Control

Loudness is adjusted to unit size; the commander normally speaks from the front/center facing the unit. Carrying power is necessary, but excessive exertion is unnecessary and harmful — trying too hard tightens the neck muscles, producing strain, hoarseness, sore throat, and indistinct/jumbled sound instead of clear commands. Ease comes from good posture, confidence, proper breathing, and correct throat/mouth adjustment. The best posture for commands is the position of attention (subordinates imitate the leader's posture). The diaphragm is the most important muscle in breathing and controls the breath used in giving commands; the throat, mouth, and nose act as amplifiers giving resonance/projection.

2.10 Distinctiveness

Depends on correct use of tongue, lips, and teeth forming separate sounds into syllables. Distinct commands are effective; indistinct commands cause confusion. Practice: give commands slowly and carefully, prolonging syllables, then gradually increase delivery rate while still articulating each syllable distinctly.

2.11 Inflection

Inflection is the rise and fall in pitch and tone. The preparatory command is pronounced with a rising inflection, ideally starting near the natural speaking pitch. The command of execution is given in a sharper tone, slightly higher pitch than the last syllable of the preparatory command, with plenty of snap. Combined commands (HUMANAY, TIWALAG) are given without inflection, at uniform high pitch/loudness.

2.12 Cadence (of commands)

A uniform, rhythmic flow of words; the interval between commands must be uniform in length for any given unit, so everyone understands the preparatory command and anticipates the command of execution. For a squad/platoon marching (absent supplementary commands), the interval allows one step/count between preparatory command and execution — the same interval applies at halt. Longer commands (e.g., KANANG PANIG, KAD) must start so the preparatory command ends on the proper foot, leaving a full count before execution. When supplementary commands are needed, the commander allows one count before the subordinate's supplementary command, and an additional count after it but before the command of execution.

Individual Movements Not Under Arms
ARCHIDAMUS OF SPARTA — ATTRIBUTION UNCONFIRMED
Opening Epigraph (Drills, p. 55)

"Maintain discipline and caution above all things, and be on the alert to obey the word of command. It is both the noblest and the safest thing for a great army to be visibly animated by one spirit."

3.1 General

This section contains most individual positions and stationary movements required in drills. Positions and their correct execution in every detail should be learned before proceeding to other drill movements. Movements are initiated from the position of attention, though some rest movements may be executed from other rest positions.

Movements While at Halt

3.2 Position of Attention

1
Feet
Bring heels together smartly on the same line, as near each other as your build permits. Turn feet out equally, forming a 45-degree angle.
2
Legs, hips, chest, shoulders
Keep knees straight without stiffness; hips level; body erect; chest lifted and arched; shoulders square and even.
3
Arms and hands
Arms hang straight down without stiffness, thumbs along trouser seams; back of hands out, fingers natural, thumbs resting along the forefinger tips.
4
Head
Erect and squarely to the front, chin drawn in (axis of head/neck vertical). Look straight to the front, about 30 yards ahead.
5
Weight & conduct
Weight rests equally on heels and balls of the feet. Remain silent; do not move unless otherwise directed.

3.3 Rest Position at the Halt

REST 1
Parade Rest

Command: TIKAS, PAHINGA. On TIKAS, shift weight to the right leg (no noticeable movement). On PAHINGA (count 1), move the left foot smartly 12 inches to the left (measured heel-inside to heel-inside); legs stay straight without stiffness, weight equal on both legs. Simultaneously, hands clasp behind the back: left hand at the small of the back below the belt, right hand inside the left, thumbs interlocked, fingers extended/joined, palms to the rear, elbows in line with the body. Silence and immobility required. To resume attention: command TILAP/PULUTONG or BALANGAY, DA — on DA, bring left heel to right heel, drop arms, assume attention.

REST 2
Stand at Ease

Command: TINDIG PALUWAG. On PALUWAG, execute Parade Rest but turn head and eyes toward the person in charge of the formation. AT EASE or REST may be executed from this position.

REST 3
At Ease

Command: PALUWAG. The soldier may move but must remain standing and silent, right foot in place. PAHINGA may be executed from this position.

REST 4
Rest

Command: PAHINGA. The soldier may move or talk, keeping the right foot in place unless otherwise directed. At Ease may be executed from this position.

Fall Out: at TIWALAG, you may leave the ranks but must remain in the immediate area; you resume your place at attention at the command HUMANAY. From any rest except TIWALAG, the command to resume attention is TILAP, HUMANDA (TILAP = take parade rest; HUMANDA = take attention). TINDIG PALUWAG, PALUWAG, and PAHINGA can also be executed directly from attention.

3.4 Dismiss

At the command LUMANSAG, troops execute about face and leave the immediate vicinity, unless instructed otherwise.

3.5 Eyes Right

A courtesy to reviewing officers/dignitaries during parades and ceremonies — a one-count movement, given while halted at attention or marching at quick time. Commands: HANDA TINGIN SA KANAN (KALIWA), NA and HANDA, RAP. On NA (count 1), all members smartly turn their heads 45° to the right; shoulders stay square to the front. There is no command to terminate — you follow the reviewing officer with head/eyes until he is straight ahead, at which point the movement terminates and you hold attention until the next command.

3.6 Facing at the Halt

TWO-COUNT MOVEMENT
Facing to the Flank
Command: HARAP SA KANAN (KALIWA), RAP. On RAP: slightly raise the right heel and left toe, turn 90° left on the left heel (assisted by pressure on the ball of the right foot); left leg stays straight, right leg bends naturally. On the 2nd count: place the right foot beside the left, resuming attention. Arms stay at the sides throughout.

Facing to the Rear

3.9 General

Contains all individual marching steps, to be mastered before unit drill. All marching movements from the halt are initiated from Attention. Except for route-step march and at-ease march, all movements are executed while marching at attention (Attention + prescribed step, simultaneously). When executed from the halt, all steps except the right step begin with the left foot. 1.1 Definition

A review is an inspection of troops by a high officer or honored guest, as a way of showing the unit's strength, state of discipline, and preparedness.

1.2 Historical Background

Reviews in the AFP were patterned after the United States Army's, due to the Americans' strong influence on the Philippine government's educational system and military during their occupation right after the Spanish regime.

US military reviews were outlined in the Regulations for the Order and Discipline of the Troops of the United States — commonly known as the Blue Book — written in 1779 by Baron Friedrich von Steuben, a former Prussian officer under Frederick the Great, commissioned by General George Washington to develop the revolutionary forces into a well-organized, highly disciplined army. Verified — see Ch. 00

The origin of military reviews traces back to the Middle Ages, when rulers held military ceremonies to show strength. At the turn of the century (per the lesson's own phrasing), armies worldwide began adopting the regimental system: regiments were assigned a color or number for identification and battlefield positioning. In battle, the color (flag) party marched front-and-center as the unit's dressing point; since victories were measured partly by enemy colors captured, the color party was a priority target and suffered heavy casualties.

In the US Army, the Colors were historically placed at the center of the formation; the company on the Colors' immediate right is still called the Color Unit. Musical elements (drums, fifes, sometimes a band) marched 12–15 paces to the rear of the formation in actual battle. By 1892, US infantry drill regulations posted bands and field music on the right of the formation, or at the head when moving in column. Today, the Colors' central position represents their old forefront battle position, while the band represents music's historic signaling role in camp and battle.

1.3 Purpose

Reviews are often mistaken for parades — features are similar, but they are two distinct ceremonies, though the two are normally combined in practice. A review is used to:

  • Honor a visiting high-ranking commander, official, or dignitary and/or let them observe a unit's state of training.
  • Present decorations and awards.
  • Honor or recognize unit or individual achievements.
  • Commemorate events.

It is more fitting to title a program "A Review in Honor of ________" or "A Review in Commemoration of ________" rather than "parade and review," when what is actually conducted is only a review.

A commander normally designates an Officer of his command as Troop Commander (TC) to enable himself to review his own command with a visiting Reviewing Officer (RO); the TC prepares the troops for the review. Three recommended formations:

  • Battalion Formation with Companies in Line (Fig. 1) — minimum 2 companies, 3 platoons per company.
  • Brigade Formation with Battalions in Line (Fig. 2) — minimum 2 battalions of 3 companies each (minimum 2 companies/battalion recommended), each company always with 3 platoons.
  • Division Formation with Brigades in Line, Battalions in Mass (Fig. 3) — same composition as Fig. 2; a saluting battery is included in the formation whenever space allows.

Formation choice depends on available space and the commander's preference; commanders may alter formation or prescribed distances for local situations. Units are sized uniformly (tallest men front and right); leaders should not be moved from position for size reasons — officers and key NCOs (including squad leaders) participate in their deserved positions of dignity.

The Distinguished Guest/Reviewing Officer (DG/RO) should ideally arrive at his post (Fig. 4) after the TC's staff has reversed and the TC has faced the reviewing stand; on arrival he takes his post immediately and refrains from greeting spectators until the review ends. The DG/RO, Military Host (MH), and other distinguished (non-receiving) persons face the troops.

When honoring someone junior in rank to the MH, the junior stands to the MH's left unless the MH yields the post of honor (normally the right — the DG/RO's post). The MH directs the TC: IHANDOG ANG PAMUNUAN (PRESENT THE TROOPS) to start the review. (Doesn't apply if guests/host are seated at a grandstand — seating protocol governs instead.)

A civilian or foreign dignitary receiving the review takes the honor position to the MH's right; the MH returns salutes when appropriate and cues/briefs the dignitary on procedure. The flag position at the rear of the reviewing party corresponds to the individual's position in the front rank. Equivalent honors (salutes) are rendered to a foreign official/dignitary entitled to them. If the dignitary's honors include their national anthem, the order is: four ruffles and flourishes, the foreign anthem, an approximate 3-second drum roll, then the Philippine National Anthem.

For ceremonies including decorations, awards, or retirements, the MH should be senior in rank/position to the highest-ranking individual honored. An officer from local staff (usually SGS or OG3) is designated to escort and brief the reviewing party.

A
Formation of Troops
Troop Adjutant (TA) directs IHUDYAT ANG PAGHANDA; band sounds ATTENTION CALL. Battalion Commanders (BCs) face about, come to attention/right shoulder arms, face front. When companies are perpendicular to their markers, BCs command KALIWANG PANIG, KAD; platoon guides go to Port Arms and move to their markers in double time, halt, execute Order Arms; BCs then command TALUPAD, TO. (Troop entrance is optional — the review may start with troops already in position, using ATTENTION CALL and the TA's command MGA GABAY SUMAHANAY NA.) The TA designates a base company ("A" BALANGAY) and positions himself between the troop line and the TC; BCs align companies with the base company (TUNTON SA KANAN), then Company Commanders and Platoon Leaders execute a detailed dressing/alignment sequence squad by squad (using the command TATAG, prolonged down the line, and short reference steps left/right). Once alignment is complete, the TA reports readiness to the TC ("Ginoo, handa na po ang talupad"), and the TC commands KALUPUNAN SUMALIKOD KO, KAD.
B
Presentation of Command and Honors
When the DG/RO halts at his post, the TC faces about and directs TANGHAL SANDATA; BCs face about, command TANGHAL, TA, face front, and salute with their staff. C
Inspection
DG/RO and MH move forward, halt 3 steps in front of the TC (staff/aides/orderlies normally don't accompany, unless the DG/RO is mounted). TC salutes, reports readiness for inspection; the band plays march music throughout. The TC escorts the DG/RO (center) and MH, positioning himself so the DG/RO is nearest the troops. During trooping the line, Platoon Leaders execute eyes-right/eyes-left as the party passes, and BC/CC face about to witness the party (KALUPUNAN, SUMALIKOD KO, KAD / MGA GABAY SUMALIKOD KO, KAD). D
Honors to the Nation
TC faces about, directs TANGHAL SANDATA; BCs face about, command TANGHAL, TA, face front, salute. TC faces front, commands PUGAY KAMAY, NA for himself and staff — signaling the band to play the National Anthem. The inspecting party and military spectators salute throughout. To end: TC commands BABA KAMAY, NA, faces about, directs BABA SANDATA; BCs terminate salutes, face about, command BABA, TA.
E
Pass-in-Review
TC brings staff behind him (KALUPUNAN, SUMALIKOD KO, KAD), then directs PASA MASID; the band moves to march position. The DG/RO moves forward to acknowledge the troops' salutes as they pass. When the Colors pass the reviewing stand, the band interrupts to play four ruffles and flourishes, and spectators salute. The band plays continuously until the last elements leave the parade ground.
F
Conclusion
Once all troops and floats have cleared the ground, the TC and staff move forward facing the grandstand and halt 10 steps in front of the honoree; the TC salutes, and the honoree acknowledges (may or may not thank/congratulate the TC and staff).

PART II · CEREMONIES · SECTION II
Parade

2.1 Definition

A parade is a military ceremony wherein a body of troops is formed before its Commanding Officer or other high-ranking officer for the display of its condition, members, equipment, and proficiency. It includes exercise in the manual of arms, the report on members of the various units present or accounted for, the publication of orders, etc., and ends with a pass in review.

2.2 Distinctive Features

Preparation and organization of troops for a parade are similar to a review's. However, since the commander is also the reviewing officer, the distance between the troops and the commander is greater than for a review. Other features: the band conducts sound off; inspection is omitted; reports are rendered; orders are published; and officers and guidons are marched forward and centered on the commander. If retreat is scheduled, it is conducted in lieu of Honors to the Nation — i.e., Retreat itself becomes the Honors to the Nation.

2.3 Types of Parades and Their Sequence

a. Ceremonial Battalion Parade

1
Formation of Troops
Units move to the ready line in the most convenient manner; the commander prescribes the assembly area, routes, sequence, and arrival time, taking his post with staff prior to Adjutant's Call. Moving from the ready line to the final line follows the same procedure as a review.
2
Sound Off
Once the battalion is formed, aligned, and at parade rest on the final line, the Adjutant directs IHUDYAT and assumes parade rest facing the formation. The band plays three sound-off chords, then moves forward playing a march, executes a left turn across the front of the troops, countermarches at the left of the line back to the right, executes a right turn, countermarches again, halts at its original position, and plays three sound-off chords once more. (Trooping the line by the band may be skipped indoors, in inclement weather, or when space is limited.) If Retreat is scheduled, it is integrated here: the Adjutant directs IHUDYAT ANG PAGSILONG, returns to parade rest; the band plays Retreat (trumpet section); the Adjutant comes to Attention, directs MGA BALANGAY HUMANDA then TANGHAL SANDATA, faces about and salutes — the band plays TO THE COLOR or the NATIONAL ANTHEM. At the conclusion, the Adjutant comes to order, faces about, directs BABA SANDATA; the commander and staff mirror PARADE REST/ATTENTION/PRESENT ARMS/ORDER ARMS with the Adjutant.
3
Honors to the Nation
If Retreat was not conducted: the Adjutant (after sound off) comes to attention and directs MGA BALANGAY HUMANDA at TANGHAL SANDATA; unit commanders come to attention, face about, command BALANGAY, DA then TANGHAL, TA, face front, salute. The Adjutant then salutes and the band plays the NATIONAL ANTHEM; the Battalion Commander and staff execute PUGAY KAMAY, NA on the first note. Military spectators rise and salute (civilian equivalent).
4
Presentation
On completion of the anthem: BC/staff execute BABA KAMAY, NA; the Adjutant terminates his salute, faces about, directs BABA SANDATA; unit commanders terminate salutes, command BABA, TA, face front. The Adjutant faces about, salutes, and reports readiness ("Ginoo, handa na po ang patikas"). The BC returns the salute and commands SUMALUNAN KA — the Adjutant trots forward, passing the BC's right, taking his post as the right-flank staff member.
5
Manual of Arms
The BC commands manual-of-arms movements as desired: HUMANDA SA PAGSASANAY SA PAGHAWAK NG SANDATA, then in sequence: SA KANANG BALIKAT, TA; KALIWANG BALIKAT, TA; AGAP, TA; BABA, TA; SIYASAT, TA; BABA, TA.
6
Report
The BC directs TANGGAPIN ANG ULAT. The Adjutant trots forward, halts at the center, directs MAG-ULAT. Unit commanders, right to left, salute and report ("Ginoo, ___ balangay, narito na po lahat o napag-alaman"); the Adjutant returns each salute, then faces front, salutes, and reports the consolidated status to the BC.
7
Publishing of Orders
The BC returns the salute and directs IPAHAYAG ANG KAUTUSAN. The Adjutant faces about and reads the orders: MAKINIG SA KAUTUSAN, ANG MGA NAKATALAGA NGAYON, PUNONG PANLARANGAN (name), PUNONG TAGAPANGALAGA (name), SA UTOS NI (name), MGA PINUNO, HUMARAP AT PUMAGITNA, KAD.
8
Officers Center March
At the last note of PUMAGITNA, all officers (Company Commanders to Platoon Leaders, including Guidon Bearers) execute Center Face; on KAD they march to the center and halt at their markers. The senior Company Commander commands MGA PINUNO, RAP, KAD — officers face the BC's position and shove off. About 12 paces from the BC, the senior CC commands MGA PINUNO, TO; officers count silently, salute at count 4, hands down at count 7 (Guidon Bearers: salute at 3, return at 6). The BC acknowledges and commands MGA PINUNO, SUMALUNAN, KAD — the group about-faces and returns; near the line, the senior CC commands MGA PINUNO, TO, RAP, KAD to halt, face their respective positions, and shove off, halting to occupy their positions and face front.
9
Pass in Review
Once all officers have returned and the band has stopped, the BC directs PASA MASID. The battalion passes in review as in a Review (Ch. 04), except units render honors 6 steps left of the Troop Commander/staff and terminate honors 6 steps to the right; the TC and staff then act as the reviewing party at their field location. Spectators may rise and applaud once the parade is complete.

b. Ceremonial Brigade Parade

The brigade is ordinarily formed in line with battalions in mass formation. The parade is the same as the Ceremonial Battalion Parade, with these exceptions:

  • The Brigade Commander is substituted for the Battalion Commander, and "brigade" for "battalion," throughout.
  • The band passes in front of the line of Battalion Commanders during sound off, across the front of the brigade.
  • Battalions execute present arms, order arms, parade rest, and attention on their own commanders' commands; reports are made by Battalion Commanders instead of Company Commanders.
  • On MGA PINUNO, PUMAGITNA, KAD: Battalion Commanders, staff, and Colors close on the line of Battalion Commanders/staffs; Company Commanders face half right/left in marching and close 4 steps to the rear of the battalion color; Guidon Bearers face half right/left and close 4 steps behind the Company Commanders; other officers face the Guidon Bearers.
  • Once closed and faced front, the senior Battalion Commander commands PASULONG, KAD (the center officer of the leading rank is the guide); the group halts with the leading rank 6 steps from the Brigade Commander, who commands TANGHAL, TA and HANDA, RAP.
  • The Brigade Commander commands MGA PINUNO, SUMALUNAN (pause), KAD — Colors reverse march, all others about-face, then march forward; the senior Battalion Commander commands MGA PINUNO, TO (restoring the original line), then SUMALUNAN (pause), KAD — Battalion Commanders/staff face outward, the battalion color guard wheels and marches back to its original position, and the other officers/Guidon Bearers return to their original positions.

c. Street Parade

Troops are formed and marched in the most convenient manner; a street parade may include military vehicles. Towed or transported weapons add drama; cargo vehicles are included only to increase the size of the display. Practical formations: columns of threes and fours; two or more columns of threes/fours abreast; mass formation. Vehicles move in a single column of twos, threes, or fours abreast, as street width permits. To keep military units in the same cadence, units should not march between two bands.

d. Evening Parade

Organization — the ceremonial elements are composed of: one brigade of two battalions (two companies of two platoons each); a band of not less than 30 members with a band conductor; two color bearers (for the National and unit colors) and two color guards.

Military Honors

3.1 General

In the Continental Army, honor guards were used as protective measures and as a means of showing the improvement in discipline of the newly formed army — likely selected for size, strength, and proven combat prowess. Today, the honor guard formation is a special courtesy to visiting dignitaries. Members are selected for soldierly appearance and superior discipline. An Honor Guard consists of a band, Colors, salute battery (when available and appropriate), and a formation of troops; it renders personal honors to persons with high military or civilian rank/position upon arrival or departure from a military command. Honor guards are not a substitute for the ceremonies appropriate in a review or parade.

An Honor Guard should not be so large as to compromise the exceptional standards required of honor guards. Minimum size: company honor guard = 2 platoons; battalion honor guard = 2 companies. Uniforms are those prescribed by the receiving unit's Commander. A designated Military Host takes part in the ceremony, briefing the honoree on the sequence of events and actions to take place. Prior to the honoree's arrival, the honor guard commander positions the band, troops, and salute battery from right to left, in that order; troops are formed in line of companies or platoons with the Colors at the center. The salute battery may be located separately when conditions dictate, but its control and purpose must not be impaired.

3.3 Policies

a. Personal Honors — Military Officers

  • Only AFP general or flag-rank officers in active service are entitled to military honors, unless otherwise directed by the CSAFP (Chief of Staff, AFP).
  • A retired general/flag officer making an official visit is likewise accorded appropriate honors.
  • Newly-promoted generals are rendered full military honors only at GHQ, their respective Major Service camps, and their own headquarters.
  • Full military honors are no longer given to commanders going to another place/unit located within their own headquarters in the same camp/station — cordon honors are appropriate instead.
  • Civilian dignitaries making official visits are accorded appropriate honors during arrival and departure from a military installation/station.
  • When two or more persons entitled to honors arrive/depart at the same time, each is honored in turn if they arrived successively — but once the most senior guest has been accorded honors, no honors are accorded to newly arrived junior guests.
  • If a person entitled to honors arrives while honors are already in progress, the ceremony continues, but the senior officer receives the departure honors.
  • If the honoree has a personal flag, the flag bearer positions 3 steps behind the honoree, follows during inspection, and returns to his original position after.
  • No honors are rendered by troops in trenches, nor by troops directly engaged in war campaigns, exercises, or disaster relief operations.
  • Guns are never fired more than once per calendar year for the same person at the same place, except for a President, Head of State, or when International Protocol requires.
  • The national flag must be at full staff whenever honors are being accorded.
  • No military honors are accorded during a national holiday, except for a President/Head of State or when International Protocol requires.
  • No honors are accorded after flag retreat and before flag raising, except for the President or his designated representative.
  • The Philippine National Police and Philippine Coast Guard are not entitled to military honors.

3.4 Procedures

a. Arrival and Departure Honors (Individual) — 1. Full Military Honors

1
Reception
The honor guard battalion starts at parade rest. The Honor Guard Commander (HGC) brings the unit to attention as the honoree arrives; the military host meets him, escorts him in front of the battalion, and leads him to the dais.
2
Presenting sword and music
The HGC faces about, commands TANGHAL, TA, faces about again, and presents sword with his staff while the honoree/host salute. Ruffles and flourishes are played, followed by appropriate music (General's March or National Anthem). Any gun salute begins with the first note of the ruffles and is evenly distributed until the music's last note.
3
Inspection of the Honor Guard
After the music, the HGC carries sword with staff, faces about, commands BABA, TA, faces about, and salutes the honoree ("Handa na po ang tanod pandangal upang libutin"). The honoree acknowledges, and the HGC escorts him and the host for inspection — presenting sword 6 paces before the Colors (TANGHAL, TA) and carrying sword 3 steps after (BABA, TA) — continuing to the last elements, then reeling off to his position in front of his staff while the honoree/host return to the dais.
4
Closing honors
The HGC faces the battalion, commands TANGHAL, TA, faces about, presents sword with his staff; the honoree acknowledges and the HGC/staff carry sword. The HGC faces about, commands BABA, TA, faces about, and salutes the honoree, saying "Tapos na po ang parangal" (the ceremony is finished).

If there is more than one honoree, the most senior takes the dais (or, if all are the same rank, one occupies the dais and the rest align beside him, joining the inspection behind the main honoree). During inclement weather, arrival/departure honors are waived except for the President/Head of State, who receive cordon honors if practicable.

a. Arrival and Departure Honors (Individual) — 2. Cordon Honors / Honor Guard

Used in addition to a scheduled honor guard ceremony, during arrival/departure of the President and certain dignitaries, welcoming/farewell to distinguished visitors, and visiting military camps — a select group of soldiers honoring and providing security at the immediate site. May also be used for other government/military officials and foreign dignitaries where practicable. The honor cordon normally consists of two squads, a cordon commander, and a host; squad size equals the number of gun salutes the honoree is entitled to. The commander may designate a band, Colors, and distinguishing flags when appropriate.

ARRIVAL
Honor Cordon — Arrival
The cordon marches to a position near the exit in two columns, centered, faced to center, dressed ~3 steps apart. As the guest and greeting party approach within 3 steps of the host, the cordon commander commands TANGHAL, TA (with arms) or PUGAY KAMAY, NA (without arms) and the cordon salutes together. Once the party clears the formation, the commander commands BABA, TA / BABA KAMAY, NA and the cordon orders arms.
DEPARTURE
Honor Cordon — Departure
Same formation as arrival. At the guest/host's approach (~3 steps), the commander commands TANGHAL, TA and the cordon presents arms. When the guest has entered the conveyance, the commander commands BABA, TA and holds until departure (or, for an aircraft, until the honoree boards). If a band and Colors are present, the organizational color dips in salute when the guest is within 6 steps, returning to carry after he passes.

b. Arrival and Departure Honors (Unit)

The unit forms in mass in front of the grandstand. When the Guest of Honor/Speaker (GOH) or representative arrives, the Unit Commander (UC) brings the troops to attention; once the GOH is seated, the UC brings the troops to present arms and presents them to the GOH, who acknowledges — the band plays appropriate march music (the grandstand stands). After the music, the UC returns the troops to attention; the Chaplain leads the invocation (everyone is seated afterward).

1
Awarding
The emcee announces the start; the UC and unit colors move forward and halt while the GOH descends the grandstand (assisted by the host) to present the award. The adjutant reads the citation before it's presented. The GOH/host return to the grandstand; the UC and unit color return to their former positions.
2
Speeches
The GOH is introduced by the host before delivering a welcome address. If the troops were previously ordered to parade rest, the UC brings them to attention after the address.
3
Recessional
The UC and troops sing "Pilipinas Kong Mahal"; the band plays appropriate recessional music. Spouses/parents proceed to present leis to the officers and enlisted men, then join their relatives for dismissal.

Note (source): the send-off ceremony for a unit departing for overseas deployment/duty follows the same procedure as the welcome ceremony, except awarding and lei presentation are omitted, and the National Anthem is played (instead of a march) during the presentation of troops.

4.2 The Colors and Color

Only the National Color is entitled to a salute by individuals or units. In garrison, the Colors are normally kept at the office/headquarters of the commanding officer, escorted to and from it by the Color Guard. In the field, they are displayed from reveille to retreat in front of the commanding officer's tent/command post; during inclement weather they are cased and placed in the CO's office, headquarters, or tent.

Individuals or units render honors when passing or being passed by uncased Colors. When not part of a formation, individuals salute at a 6-step distance and hold the salute until 6 steps beyond; the individual in charge of a formation calls it to HUMANDA and TANGHAL, TA. The Colors may be carried in any formation with two or more companies, honor guards, or representative elements. Traditionally, the command Sergeant Major is responsible for safeguarding, caring for, and displaying the organizational color, and for selecting, training, and overseeing the performance of Color bearers and guards. The regulation for individual/distinguishing flags of general officers and other dignitaries is the same as for organizational colors.

During a review, parade, or honor guard ceremony, only one National Color should be present, in the honor position, carried on marching at the right of positional/organizational colors. The organizational color of the senior headquarters sponsoring the ceremony is carried to the left of the Major Service flag; colors belonging to the headquarters conducting the ceremony are positioned in line with and centered on the command. Subordinate color-bearing organizations ordinarily carry only their own organizational colors (4 steps to the rear of their staff). When the Colors form en masse, the National Color is placed front and center of the Color guards, with organizational colors immediately behind, arranged by unit seniority.

4.3 Salutes

The National Color renders no salute (dip). The organizational color salutes (dips) in all military ceremonies while the National Anthem, To the Color, or a foreign national anthem is played, and when rendering honors to the organizational commander, an individual of higher grade (including foreign dignitaries of higher grade), and other specified cases. When marching, organizational colors salute 6 steps before the person entitled to the salute and return to carry 6 steps after.

4.4 Color Guard

The Color Guard consists of two sergeants and two privates; selection is considered an honor. The Senior (Color) Sergeant carries the National Color and commands the Color Guard, giving the necessary commands for movements and honors. When battalions/brigades carry organizational colors as part of a larger command, the color is carried 4 steps to the rear of the staff — a sergeant acts as Color bearer, with two experienced privates (selected by the battalion/brigade command Sergeant Major) as Color Guard members.

The Color guards form and march in one rank at close interval, bearer in the center; they do not execute Rear March or About Face, marching at Right Shoulder Arms and executing facing movements by wheeling right or left (command: IKOT SA KANAN (KALIWA), NA — the guard nearest the turn direction pivots in place while others shorten steps and turn in an arc to stay abreast; each then marks time until TILAP, TO or PASULONG, KAD).

Passing in review, the Color guards execute Eyes Right at the prescribed saluting distance on the Color sergeant's command (HANDA TINGIN SA KANAN, NA / HANDA RAP); the organizational color salutes at NA and resumes carry at RAP — the guard on the right flank does not execute Eyes Right. During ceremonies, color guards stay at Right Shoulder Arms except when presenting arms. If a salute is warranted outside a ceremony, the Color Sergeant commands PUGAY, WATAWAT (return to carry: HANDA DALA, WATAWAT). In formation with the Color Company (not during a ceremony), the color bearers execute at ease/rest with the staff vertical, while color guards execute Right Shoulder, Order Arms, and Present Arms with the Color company; when Colors are not forward and remarks are given, guards/bearers execute Order Arms and Parade Rest on the Color Company Commander's command, and are at Parade Rest whenever the unit is At Ease. Color guard uniform matches that of the participating troops.

4.5 Receiving or Dismissing the Colors by the Color Guard

Receiving uncased Colors on display in the commander's office: the Color Sergeant commands TANGHAL, TA and HANDA, RAP. Upon completion of Order Arms, the color bearers (without command) secure the Colors; the Color Guard files outside (guard, National Color, organizational color, guard), reforms in line, guards execute Right Shoulder Arms, bearers assume carry position. Dismissing the Colors follows the same procedure, except the Colors are placed back in their stands before executing Present Arms.

4.6 Organizational

The Color Guard consists of five men: the most senior is the Color Sergeant (officer), who commands the guards. The next two senior are the color bearers (unarmed); the two most junior are color guards (armed with pistols or rifles). The senior color bearer carries the National Colors; the junior carries the organizational colors. When only the National Colors are carried, the Color Guard has one color bearer. When organizational colors are carried alongside, extra bearers are added in order of color precedence: PHIL ARMY, PHIL AIR FORCE, PHIL NAVY, PHIL MARINE CORPS.

4.7 Formation

Color guards form and march in one rank at close interval; the National Color bearer is always on the right, the organizational color bearer to his left, and a color guard on each side. The Color Guard does not execute Rear March, facing movements, flanking, color movement, or fix bayonets — when its attached unit executes such commands (except fix bayonets), the senior color bearer orders an appropriate movement to reposition the guard. The Color Guard executes PALUWAG and PAHINGA with the Color Company (battalion); there is no prescribed At Ease/Rest position for the Color Guard itself — when such commands are given to the Color Company, the Color Guard instead executes TIKAS, PAHINGA.

Objective

Scope / Agenda

Informal Guard Mounting — Sequence of Activities

a
The Sergeant of the Guard forms the guard: HUMANAY. The Relief Commander falls in 3 steps front/centered on the Sergeant of the Guard; each relief forms at normal interval to its commander's left, sentinels in order of assigned post; the Sergeant of the Guard orders inspection of arms: SIYASAT, TA; AGAP, TA; BABA, TA.
b
If an officer has been detailed as Commander of the Guard: the Sergeant of the Guard faces about, reports "Nandito na pong lahat," exchanges salutes, then marches (half-right in marching) to a position behind the Commander of the Third Relief at normal distance.
c
If no officer has been detailed: the Sergeant of the Guard steps 3 steps forward and assumes the Commander of the Guard's position.
d
When the Officer of the Day arrives, the Commander of the Guard reports "Narito na pong lahat" and salutes are exchanged.
e
Marching to Center
The Officer of the Day commands PINUNO AT MGA KAWAL PUMAGITNA, KAD. The rearmost NCO commands the other NCOs SA KANANG BALIKAT, TA. On KAD, the Commander of the Guard marches forward and halts 3 steps in front of the Officer of the Day; the NCOs march forward, execute column left without command, and are halted/faced right by the rearmost NCO once centered.
f
The Officer of the Day marches to each NCO in turn, designating roles: PINUNO NG TANOD (Commander of the Guard position, if not already an officer), SARHENTO NG TANOD (right flank NCO), then PINUNO PANGATLONG PAMALIT, PINUNO PANGALAWANG PAMALIT, and PINUNO UNANG PAMALIT for the remaining NCOs — then returns to his post by the most direct route.
g
Return to Post
The Officer of the Day commands SUMALUNAN. The Commander of the Guard and NCOs face about and march to their posts without further command; the Sergeant of the Guard (or First Relief Commander, if no officer was detailed) commands the relief commander BABA, TA and HARAP SA LIKOD, RAP.
h
Inspecting the Guard
The Officer of the Day commands HUMANDA SA PAGSISIYASAT. The Commander of the Guard (without saluting) faces about and commands PABUKANG TALUDTOD, NA to open and dress ranks, then inspection proceeds; unpresentable sentinels are ordered to fall out (supernumeraries replace them). Afterward, the Officer of the Day commands DALHIN ANG MGA TANOD SA BAHAY TANURAN; salutes are exchanged, and the Commander of the Guard marches the guard to the guardhouse (LAPIT TALUDTOD, KAD; HARAP SA KANAN, RAP; KANANG BALIKAT, TA).
i
Relieving the Old Guard
The old guard forms in line before the new guard arrives. When the new guard is 6 steps from the old guard's left flank, the Commander of the New Guard commands HANDA, TINGIN SA KANAN, NA, salutes are rendered, and once clear of the old guard's right flank, HANDA, RAP is commanded; the Commander of the Old Guard commands BABA, TA.
j
Formation at the Guardhouse
The Commander of the (new) Guard commands KANANG PANIG, KAD, positions 6 steps to the rear/centered, halts the new guard in line 6 steps right of the old guard, then BABA, TA; HARAP SA LIKOD, RAP. The left-flank man of the first relief verifies the 6-step interval and dresses the guard (Fig. 6a).
k
Presenting the New and Old Guard
The commanders of both guards face about, command TANGHAL, TA, face each other and exchange salutes, then face their guards, command BABA, TA, and face front.
l
Presenting the Guards to the Officer of the Day
The new and old Officers of the Day take positions 18 steps in front/centered on their guards. Commanders of both guards face about, command TANGHAL, TA, salute their respective officers, then command BABA, TA. The Officers of the Day then face each other and the New Officer of the Day salutes the Old.
m
Disposition of the New and Old Guards
The New Officer of the Day commands ISALUNAN ANG UNANG PAMALIT; the new commander salutes, faces about, and commands TIWALAG — the guard reports to the guardhouse for relief-posting instructions. The Old Officer of the Day commands TIWALAG ANG MGA TANOD; the old commander salutes, faces about, and commands PAHINGA, then briefs the new commander. The old guard is marched back to its company area and dismissed with SIYASAT, TA; AGAP, TA; TIWALAG (per company, if furnished by more than one). After the third relief is relieved, its Commander reports to the Commander of the Guard, who inspects and dismisses it the same way.

Formal Guard Mounting — Sequence of Activities

a
Assembly of Band
The band takes its place with its left flank 12 steps to the right of where the guard's right flank will be; the Adjutant signals it to sound the Adjutant's Call once the guard is ready.
b
Assembly and Forming the Guard
Guard details form as in Informal Guard Mounting (a), assemble into a platoon (split into two if more than 14 men per rank). The Assistant Sergeant of the Guard posts 3 steps in front, centered on the second platoon; remaining NCOs equalize the ranks on the left flank. The Sergeant of the Guard then faces the guard to the right.
c
Marching the Guard to the Parade Ground
On the Adjutant's Call, the band plays march music; the Adjutant (Sergeant Major on his left) marches forward on the first note. The Sergeant of the Guard commands KANANG BALIKAT, TA and marches the guard onto the ground in column, halting it 12 steps from the band's left flank, centered on the Sergeant Major — then BABA, TA; HARAP SA KALIWA, RAP.
d
Dressing the Guard
The Sergeant of the Guard (and Assistant, if two platoons) dresses the guard.
e–f
Sergeant of the Guard & Sergeant Major's Report
On HANDA, HARAP, the Commander(s) of the Guard post 6 steps to the rear of the rear rank's right-flank man. The Sergeant of the Guard reports guard strength to the Sergeant Major ("Lahat po'y narito" or naming absentees); salutes exchanged, Sergeant Major commands SUMALUNAN. The Sergeant Major then reports the same to the Adjutant, exchanges salutes, and posts left of the first rank of the second platoon. The Commander of the Guard then marches around the guard's right flank to a post 6 steps in front/centered.
g–h
Marching to Center & Return to Post
The Adjutant commands PINUNO (MGA PINUNO) AT PINUNONG HINDI HIRANG, NA — the same procedure as Informal Guard Mounting's "Marching to Center," except with two Commanders of the Guard the senior is "Punong Tanod" and the junior "Pangalawang Punong Tanod" (and with two Sergeants of the Guard, "Tandes ng Tanod" / "Pangalawang Tandes ng Tanod"). The Adjutant then commands SUMALUNAN and posts are taken as in Informal Guard Mounting.
i
Inspecting the Guard
Unlike Informal Guard Mounting, the Adjutant (not the Officer of the Day) inspects, commanding HUMANDA SA PAGSISIYASAT. He selects orderlies and color sentinels and orders unprepared men to fall out. The band plays during inspection.
j
Closing Ranks & Sound Off
After inspection, the Commander(s) of the Guard close ranks and return to post; the Adjutant posts 35 steps in front, centered, facing the guard; the New Officer of the Day posts 36 steps to the Adjutant's rear; the Old Officer of the Day, 3 steps to the New's right. The Adjutant commands TANOD, HANDA; TIKAS PAHINGA; IHUDYAT — the band plays the sound-off, moves forward playing, passes left of the Commanders of the Guard and Adjutant, returns right, and plays sound-off again. The Officers of the Day stand at attention throughout.
l
Adjutant's Report
The Adjutant commands TANGHAL, TA, faces about toward the New Officer of the Day, salutes, and reports: "Ang Tanod Ay Nakahanay Na Po."
m
Marching in Review
The New Officer of the Day returns the salute and directs the Adjutant PASA MASID. The Adjutant faces about, commands BABA, TA; HARAP SA KANAN (executed at RAP); the band repositions ahead of the leading platoon. The Adjutant commands KANANG BALIKAT, TA; PASULONG, KAD. Six steps from the Officer of the Day, the Adjutant commands HANDA, TINGIN SA KANAN, NA — salutes are held until 6 steps beyond, then HANDA, RAP (with two platoons, the Assistant Commander of the Guard repeats this for the second platoon). The Officer of the Day stands at Attention throughout and returns the salutes once the Adjutant and Commander of the Guard have passed. The guard then marches to the guardhouse; the old and new guards are relieved there following the same procedure as Informal Guard Mounting.

Normal Guard Mounting

General

Guard mounting ceremony has been a tradition in military organizations that formalizes the relieving of the outgoing and incoming guards for the day. Proper accounting, punctuality, and inspection of the incoming and outgoing guards are observed to ensure they are ready to take and leave their posts respectively.

Organization

The ceremony is composed of: the Outgoing Officer of the Day, the Incoming Officer of the Day, the Outgoing Commander of the Guard, the Incoming Commander of the Guard, the Assistant Commanders of the Guard, the Outgoing Sergeant of the Guards, and the Incoming Sergeant of the Guards.

Formation

Two platoons in line: the first (incoming guards) and the second (outgoing guards, 3 paces from the first's left flank). At each platoon's right flank, the Sergeants of the Guards cover each other. The Assistant Commanders of the Guards stand 3 paces in front of the center of their respective platoons; the Commanders of the Guards stand 6 paces in front of center. The Outgoing Officer of the Day stands 6 paces in front of the Commanders of the Guards, and the Incoming Officer of the Day 3 paces in front of the Outgoing.

Procedure

a
Before the ceremony, incoming guards report to their respective relieves for instructions. The Outgoing Officer of the Day then calls the guards to fall in: HUMANAY.
b
The Assistant Commanders of the Guards face about to account for their platoons, then salute and report to their Commanders ("Pinuno, narito na po lahat," or naming any unaccounted). The Commanders acknowledge and direct SUMALUNAN KA; the Assistant Commanders position behind the Sergeants of the Guards of each platoon's last squad.
d
The Outgoing Officer of the Day directs the Commanders of the Guards to prepare the platoons for inspection: HUMANDA SA PAGSISIYASAT, then PABUKANG TALUDTOD, KAD to open ranks; the commanders take position in front of the first squad's Sergeant of the Guard and await the inspecting officer.
e
The inspecting officer (Incoming or Outgoing Officer of the Day — both may inspect simultaneously to save time) proceeds to each Commander of the Guard, who salutes and reports readiness ("Ginoo tanod pulutong, [name] namumuno, handa na po sa pagsisiyasat"), then assists in inspecting the platoon.
f
After inspection, violations are reported for action; the Commander of the Guard brings the platoon to close ranks (PASARANG TALUDTOD, KAD) and takes a position 6 paces in front-center.
g
The Outgoing Officer of the Day commands ANG MGA PINUNO NG TANOD PUMAGITNA, KAD; the Assistant Commanders and Sergeants of the Guards of both platoons march to the center and mark time. The Outgoing Commander of the Guard commands MGA PINUNO, TO, and all face the Outgoing Officer of the Day.
h
The Outgoing Commander of the Guard salutes: "Ginoo humihingi ng pahintulot para sa palitan." The Outgoing Officer of the Day acknowledges and commands: MAGPALITAN.
i
The Relief Exchange (challenge and response)
The Outgoing Commander commands MGA PINUNO HARAP SA GITNA, RAP, then MGA PAPALITANG TANOD PUGAY KAMAY, NA — outgoing guards salute and report readiness to be relieved. Incoming guards challenge whether all orders/instructions from proper authority were passed on ("Iyo bang naisalin ang lahat ng utos na nanggaling sa tamang katungkulan?"); the outgoing guards answer "opo" (yes) or "hindi po" (no) — relief does not take place if the answer is no. The incoming guards acknowledge: "Ikaw na ay papalitan." The same challenge-and-response then runs in reverse — the Incoming Commander commands MGA PAPALIT NA TANOD PUGAY KAMAY, NA, and the outgoing guards challenge whether the incoming guards have received all such orders, with the same opo/hindi po rule. Once both exchanges are confirmed, the Incoming Commander commands MGA PINUNO NG TANOD PUGAY KAMAY, NA ("Ginoo tapos na po ang palitan"), the Outgoing Officer of the Day commands SUMALUNAN, and the Incoming Commander marches everyone back to their original positions (MGA PINUNO NG TANOD SUMALUNAN, KAD) via a detailed facing-and-marching sequence specified in the lesson.
j
Officer of the Day Exchange
Once the Commanders, Assistant Commanders, and Sergeants of the Guards are back in position, the Outgoing Officer of the Day faces about and salutes the Incoming Officer of the Day with the same "handed na po upang mapalitan" line and the same opo/hindi po challenge-and-response used by the guards above (in both directions), ending with the Incoming Officer of the Day acknowledged as having taken over ("IKAW NA AY PUMALIT").
k
The Outgoing Officer of the Day executes about face and falls out; the Incoming Officer of the Day takes his position, may give announcements, then directs the Commanders of the Guards to take charge of their platoons and proceed to their respective duties.

Ethics and Moral Education From the Lesson (p. 13–14)

Ethics comes from the Greek word ethos, meaning character or custom.

Morality can be used either:

  1. Descriptively — to refer to a code of conduct put forward by a society, used as a guide to behavior by that society's members.
  2. Normatively — to refer to a code of conduct that, given specified conditions, would be put forward by all rational persons for governing the behavior of all moral agents.

Morality affects our daily choices, and those decisions are guided by our conscience. The purpose of morality is to provide a framework for optimum human survival. The standard of morality, however, is absolute and immutable — long-term optimum human survival.
