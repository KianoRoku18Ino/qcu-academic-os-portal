AS SHOWN — REVIEW OF RELATED TOPICS
Constitutional basis for the Code
"Whereas, section five of Article XIII of the Constitution provides that all schools shall aim to develop moral character, personal discipline, civic conscience, and to teach the duties of citizenship; Whereas, in order to comply with the above constitutional provision, it is necessary that a Code of Citizenship and Ethics be adopted and taught in the schools; Whereas, pending the formulation of such a code by a committee representing the various elements of the community and the adoption thereof, it is essential that the schools should at once take steps in order to effectuate the mandate of the Constitution in this respect;"

The Six Core Values

LOVE OF THE COUNTRY · VALOR · HONOR · LOYALTY · DUTY · SOLIDARITY (verified against multiple independent AFP/Philippine Army sources — see Ch. 05, no discrepancy found).

1. Love of the Country

AS SHOWN
Full definition
It is the dominant nature of an Army soldier to love his country. It is for the nation that a soldier unquestionably commits to a life of service, the highest value that the Filipino soldier acquires. It is not measured by cash, rank nor belonging. The vitality drives the soldier as the protector of national power. He remains by his vow of service with a strong heart, realizing that regardless if he is eventually left standing alone or dying in the war zone, he rests content with the thought that his purpose of existence has been satisfied. It is this love for country that infests each reference of valor for deserving soldiers, valor that further elevates the spirit of the country. wording as shown — see Ch. 05

2. Valor

AS SHOWN
Full definition
Valor streams normally from one's affection for the nation. It is the capacity to conquer fear in doing one's objective. Courage is beyond bravery like going after a mission despite seemingly insurmountable opposition. It is ascertaining yet not self-destructive. With valor you make the wisest decision.

Your valor will allow you to do the following (as shown): In shaping the Filipino soldier's identity, even beyond valor, one could also see the strong sense of honor that resonated with the call to arms in the past.

  • Control your fear in physical and moral context.
  • Take responsibility for your actions, mistakes and decisions.
  • Confront problems and do what you believe is right.
  • Report successes and failure with equal candor.

3. Honor

AS SHOWN
Full definition
Honor is the Military crowning value, the hallmark of military conduct, the nature of the soldier's awareness of individual dignity and self-worth. The soldier conveys his honor in meeting the military basic from prelude and crusade to the consequence. Regardless of the field, honor is regarded in one's self as in the other. In outward appearance, honor is found in cleanliness of the individual, courteousness, decency in daily living and reasonability in the utilization of individual or country resources. It appears in the candor, straightforwardness in speech and in action.

An individual soldier with honor does the following (as shown):

  • Develop and maintain a keen sense of ethical conduct.
  • Adhere to the public code of professional Philippine Army values.
  • Identity with the ideals embodied in the Philippine Army values. wording as shown — see Ch. 05

4. Loyalty

AS SHOWN
Full definition (two slides)
Loyalty underlies the soldier's Oath of Service. It demands obedience to the lawful privileges of duly constituted authority in the quest for the nation's interest, observance of the Chain of Command and compliance with orders to the best of one's capacity as all orders must be complied. Loyalty while itself a cause, procures the harvest of military solidarity. It foster cohesion, true sympathy toward the welfare of every individual from the Army and those they work with, and is best expressed in every member's adherence to the military value system as clarified, much as he will undoubtedly take after the guideline of law and principles and standards of the nation. grammar as shown — see Ch. 05

An individual soldier of loyalty displays the following (as shown):

  • Put obligations in correct order: the Constitution, the Philippine Army, the unit and finally, self.
  • Show faithfulness to unit and comrades by finishing all tasks with them.
  • Carry out tough orders without expressing personal criticism.
  • Defend soldiers against unfair treatment.

5. Duty

AS SHOWN
Full definition
Duty is service plain and simple. Actual duty means being always ready, all senses employed and utilized whether in the garrison or in the field. Duty means satisfying your commitment. The demanding standard of duty additionally requires that those in charge positions release their obligations without bounds.

Expressing the value of duty means, at a minimum, doing the following (as shown):

  • Carrying out the requirements of the position to the best of your ability.
  • Fulfill legal, civic and moral obligations.
  • Sacrifice personal time in pursuit of excellence.

6. Solidarity

AS SHOWN
Full definition
Solidarity is fellowship at the visible level and internally being adjusted to the Philippine Army's goals, vision and programs for reform. Solidarity ties the Army to oneness with the yearnings of the rest of the country in the way of a Filipino proud with his nation.

Application (closing section, as shown)

AS SHOWN
Responsibility for instilling values
The primary responsibility regarding ingraining and strengthening the values and standards of conduct is vested in the military leadership. Officers and NCOs in command and control positions should commit these values to memory and be in a position to clarify the same when inquired.
AS SHOWN
Values in practice
The soldier is well aware of the methods for the world and constantly utilizes his knowledge of human nature when confronted with circumstances not particularly met in his training courses. Values are taught by perception and practice over time. Thus, it is normal that steady survey of one's self in the dynamism of daily life will guide the right application of the prior qualities and standards of conduct in military life, for this code to endure and be relevant for the decades ahead.

Recitation-Ready Reference ADDED

VS. DUTY
Love of Country
The motivational root — why a soldier serves at all. Duty is the active, day-to-day obligation that follows from it. Example: a reservist reporting for duty during a calamity out of love of country, expressed through the specific duty assigned.
VS. HONOR
Valor
Valor is courage in the face of fear or danger; Honor is the underlying integrity/character that valor serves. Example: a soldier advancing under fire (valor) to keep a promise to protect civilians (honor).
VS. LOYALTY
Honor
Honor is about self-respect and personal dignity; Loyalty is about faithfulness to others (unit, chain of command). Example: refusing a bribe (honor) while still following a lawful order you personally disagree with (loyalty).
VS. SOLIDARITY
Loyalty
Loyalty is the individual soldier's obedience and faithfulness; Solidarity is the collective cohesion that results when everyone is loyal together. Example: one soldier's loyalty to his unit vs. the whole unit's shared sense of oneness.

Origin and Definition

AS SHOWN
Origin of the phrase
The expression 'Rule of Law' has been derived from the French phrase 'la principle de legalite', i.e. a Government based on the principles of law. It was expounded for the first time by Sir Edward Coke, and was developed by Prof. A.V. Dicey in his book 'The law of the Constitution' published in 1885. verified — see Ch. 05
AS SHOWN
"According to Edward Coke, 'Rule of Law' means:"
A. Absence of arbitrary power on the part of the Government.
B. No man is punishable or can be made to suffer in body or good except for a distinct breach of law established in the ordinary legal manner before the ordinary courts of the land. attribution flagged — see Ch. 05
AS SHOWN
Prof. A.V. Dicey's definition
"The Law of the Constitution"
"The rule of law means the absolute supremacy or predominance of the regular law as opposed to the influence of arbitrary power and excludes the existence of arbitrariness or even of wide discretionary authority on the part of the government."
AS SHOWN
Dicey regarded rule of law as the bedrock of the British Legal System
"This doctrine is accepted in the constitutions of U.S.A. and India."

Dicey's Three Meanings

As shown: "According to Prof. Diccy [sic], rules of law contains three principles or it has three meanings as stated below:" spelling of "Diccy" as shown

MEANING 1
Supremacy of Law

"No man is punishable or can lawfully be made to suffer in body or goods except for a distinct breach of law established in the ordinary legal manner before the ordinary courts of the land."

MEANING 2
Equality Before Law

"No man is above law."

MEANING 3
Predominance of Legal Spirit

"The general principles of the constitution are the result of juridical decisions determining [the] rights of private persons in particular cases brought before the Court."

Modern Restatement (as shown)

AS SHOWN
Basic form
The Rule of Law, in its most basic form, is the principle that no one is above the law. The rule follows logically from the idea that truth, and therefore law, is based upon fundamental principles which can be discovered, but which cannot be created through an act of will.
AS SHOWN
Most important application
The most important application of the rule of law is the principle that governmental authority is legitimately exercised only in accordance with written, publicly disclosed laws adopted and enforced in accordance with established procedural steps that are referred to as due process. The principle is intended to be a safeguard against arbitrary governance, whether by a totalitarian leader or by mob rule. Thus, the rule of law is hostile both to dictatorship and to anarchy.

Rule of Law Under the Indian Constitution as shown — foreign law, see Ch. 05

AS SHOWN
A. Preamble
The preamble of our constitution clearly sets out the principle of rule of law when it lays down the objectives of social, economic and political justice, equality of status and opportunity, and fraternity and dignity of individuals in India.
AS SHOWN
B. Part III — Fundamental Rights
Part III of the Constitution lays down the fundamental rights guaranteed to every citizen of the country. These rights are justifiable U/A 32 & 226 which ensure them a protection from any legislative or executive encroachments.

Recitation-Ready Reference ADDED

CORE IDEA
Rule of Law
No one — not even the government — is above the law. Compare to "Respect the Constitution" (Ch. 01, Tenet 3), which expresses the same idea in citizen-facing language. Example: a public official being prosecuted under the same laws as an ordinary citizen.
VS. EQUALITY BEFORE LAW
Supremacy of Law
Supremacy of Law is about the absence of arbitrary power; Equality Before Law is about everyone facing the same rules, including officials. Example: Supremacy = a police officer needing a warrant like anyone else; Equality = that same officer being tried in an ordinary court, not a special one.

CHAPTER 05
Source Verification & Discrepancy Notes

Every date, name, title, and quote above was checked against independent sources. Nothing in Chapters 01-03 was corrected to match these findings — your professor's material is reproduced exactly as shown. This chapter is where every discrepancy is reported separately, as requested.

EO 217 — date & count
Confirmed accurate. Executive Order No. 217 was indeed issued by President Manuel L. Quezon on August 19, 1939, and does contain exactly 16 tenets, verified against the Official Gazette's archived text and independent historical sources.
Tenet 2 — "sacrfice"
The slide reads "sacrfice" — a typo missing the second "i." The verified Official Gazette text reads "sacrifice." A spelling error only; the meaning is unaffected.
Tenet 16 — "National" vs. "Natural" Resources
This is a meaningful wording discrepancy, not just a typo. The slide's title reads "Use and Develop Our National Resources." The verified Official Gazette text of EO 217 reads "Use and develop our natural resources" — and the tenet's own body text ("conserve them for posterity... inalienable heritage") only makes sense read as natural resources (forests, minerals, water, etc.), not "national" resources. This looks like a one-word substitution error in the professor's slide that changes the tenet's meaning.
AFP Core Values — the six values themselves
Confirmed accurate. Love of Country, Valor, Honor, Loyalty, Duty, and Solidarity are verified as the genuine, consistently cited AFP/Philippine Army core values across multiple independent ROTC and Philippine Army sources.
"Love of Country" — "infests"
The slide reads "...it is this love for country that infests each reference of valor..." This is an unusual word choice — "infests" ordinarily carries a negative, pest-like connotation, which doesn't fit the sentence's positive meaning. It reads as though it may be a typo for a word like "infuses" or "invests," but this guide is not correcting it — presented exactly as shown, flagged for your awareness.
Honor — "Identity with the ideals"
The slide's bullet reads "Identity with the ideals embodied in the Philippine Army values." Grammatically, this likely should be the verb "Identify" rather than the noun "Identity" — flagged as a probable typo, not corrected in Ch. 02.
Loyalty — "It foster cohesion"
Subject-verb agreement error as shown ("It foster" instead of "It fosters"). Flagged only; not corrected in Ch. 02.
Rule of Law — Coke/Dicey attribution
This is the most significant discrepancy found. One slide states "According to Edward Coke, 'Rule of Law' means: A. Absence of arbitrary power... B. No man is punishable..." However, a later slide in the same lesson presents this exact same two-point content as Dicey's own "first and second meanings" of Rule of Law (Supremacy of Law and Equality Before Law). Independent verification confirms: Sir Edward Coke is historically credited only with originating the general concept of rule of law (17th century, under James I); the specific, detailed two/three-part formulation shown is Dicey's own systematic articulation from "Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution" (1885). The material appears to misattribute Dicey's specific wording to Coke on one slide, then correctly credits Dicey with the same content a few slides later — an internal inconsistency worth raising with your instructor if it comes up in recitation.
"la principle de legalite"
Minor: the French is misspelled/mis-grammared as shown. Correct French would be "le principe de légalité" (masculine article "le," not "la"; "principe" not "principle"; accent on "légalité"). Very likely inherited boilerplate rather than an error specific to your professor — see next note.
A.V. Dicey's book title
The slide cites the book as "The Law of the Constitution," published 1885. Verified: Dicey's actual title is "Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution" (1885 first edition). "The Law of the Constitution" is commonly used as an informal short title (and appears that way in many other sources too), so this isn't necessarily wrong, but the full formal title is longer than what's shown.
Indian case citations
All three case citations were independently verified as accurate, real Indian Supreme Court cases with matching quotes: Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (AIR 1975 SC 2299, decided Nov. 7, 1975), State of Bihar v. Sonawati Kumari (AIR 1961 SC 221), and Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab ("Death Penalty Case"). No factual errors found in these citations themselves.
Entire Rule of Law section is Indian law, not Philippine law
Important context, not exactly an "error." Every constitutional provision (Preamble, Part III, Articles 32 & 226) and every court case in this lesson is Indian, not Philippine. This is a widely-recycled ROTC/CWTS teaching resource — the identical content (word-for-word, including the same three case citations) was independently found circulating in study materials from at least one other Philippine university, so this isn't unique to your class or an invention of your instructor. It does mean, though, that if your exam expects Philippine constitutional provisions or Philippine case law on Rule of Law, none of that appears anywhere in the material you uploaded — worth confirming with your instructor whether Philippine-specific content was covered separately.
