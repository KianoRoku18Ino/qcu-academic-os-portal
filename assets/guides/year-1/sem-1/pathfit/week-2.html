CHAPTER 00
Overview & Study Priority Map

This unit builds the vocabulary you need to describe the human body precisely. Anatomical directions are the standard terms (anterior, posterior, superior, etc.) used to describe the location of one body part relative to another — the "language" the rest of anatomy and exercise science is written in. The 13 major muscle groups are the muscles you'll actually train and reference all semester, each with a specific function and a set of exercises that work it.

🔗
Critical Path Context

Learn Chapter 01 before Chapter 02–03 — anatomical directions are the descriptive language you'll use to talk about muscle location and movement (e.g. "the pecs are anterior to the lats," "the triceps sit on the posterior side of the upper arm"). Skipping straight to muscles without the direction vocabulary makes the functional descriptions harder to retain.

CHAPTER 01
Anatomical Directions

These 8 paired terms describe where one body structure sits relative to another. They're always used in pairs of opposites — memorize them as pairs, not as 8 isolated words, since exam questions usually test whether you can tell the two apart in context.

Front / Back — Anterior vs. Posterior

FRONT · VENTRAL
Anterior
Toward the front of the body; front or forward.
Example: The sternum is anterior to the heart.
BACK · DORSAL
Posterior
Toward the back of the body; back or rearward.
Example: The heart is posterior to the sternum.

Up / Down — Superior vs. Inferior

UPPER · HEAD-END
Superior
Toward the head end or upper part of a structure.
Example: The head is superior to the chest.
LOWER · FEET-END
Inferior
Toward the feet end or lower part of a structure.
Example: The stomach is inferior to the lungs.

Side / Center — Lateral vs. Medial

AWAY FROM MIDLINE
Lateral
Away from the midline of the body; toward the side.
Example: The arms are lateral to the chest.
TOWARD MIDLINE
Medial
Toward the midline of the body; toward the center.
Example: The breastbone is medial to the arms.

Limb Reference — Distal vs. Proximal

Unlike the three pairs above (which reference the whole body), distal and proximal only make sense relative to a point of attachment or origin — almost always used for limbs.

FARTHER FROM ORIGIN
Distal
Farther from the point of attachment or origin.
Example: The fingers are distal to the wrist.
CLOSER TO ORIGIN
Proximal
Closer to the point of attachment or origin.
Example: The elbow is proximal to the wrist.
⚠️
Common Mix-Up

Lateral/medial and distal/proximal are both about "closer vs. farther," but on different axes: lateral/medial is always relative to the body's midline, while distal/proximal is relative to a limb's point of attachment. A term like "the wrist" can be both proximal (to the fingers) and distal (to the elbow) at the same time — it depends on which direction you're comparing.

CHAPTER 02
Major Muscle Groups — Upper Body

9 of the 13 major muscle groups are in the upper body. For each, know the function and at least one sample exercise — exam questions typically give you either the function or an activity and ask you to name the muscle.

UPPER · 01
Delts (Shoulders)

Allows shoulder movement in multiple directions — lifting, pushing, pulling, and rotating the arm.
Workout: Overhead Press, Lateral Raises, Front Raises, Arnold Press.

UPPER · 02
Biceps

Flexes the elbow and rotates the forearm; acts in pulling movements.
Workout: Bicep Curls, Hammer Curls, Chin-Ups.

UPPER · 03
Triceps

Extends the elbow; acts in pushing movements — the functional opposite of the biceps.
Workout: Tricep Curls, Overhead Tricep Extension, Close-Grip Push-Ups.

UPPER · 04
Pecs (Chest)

Moves the arms forward, across the body, and inward. Important for pushing movements and upper body strength.
Workout: Push-Ups, Bench Press, Chest Fly.

UPPER · 05
Abdominals

Stabilizes the trunk, helps in bending forward, and supports good posture.
Workout: Crunches, Leg Raises, Plank, Sit-Ups.

UPPER · 06
Obliques

Allows rotation and side-bending of the trunk. Supports core stability.
Workout: Russian Twists, Side Plank, Bicycle Crunch, Woodchoppers.

UPPER · 07
Traps (Trapezius)

Elevates, retracts, and rotates the shoulder blades; supports neck and upper back movement.
Workout: Shrugs, Upright Rows, Farmer's Walk, Face Pulls.

UPPER · 08
Lats (Latissimus Dorsi)

Moves the arm down and back — a pulling movement. Helps with posture and upper body strength.
Workout: Pull-Ups, Lat Pulldown, Seated Cable Row, T-Bar Row.

UPPER · 09
Erector Spinae

Supports the spine, maintains posture, and helps in bending backward and stabilizing the body.
Workout: Back Extensions, Deadlifts, Good Mornings, Superman Hold.

🧭
Directional Tie-In

Pecs sit anterior on the torso; traps, lats, and erector spinae sit posterior. Biceps are on the anterior upper arm, triceps on the posterior upper arm — the same front/back pairing from Chapter 01 explains why biceps pull and triceps push.

CHAPTER 03
Major Muscle Groups — Lower Body

The remaining 4 major muscle groups are in the lower body — smaller in number than the upper body set, but just as heavily tested since they cover the most common daily movements (walking, running, jumping).

LOWER · 10
Glutes

Extends and rotates the hip. Important for power, posture, and lower body strength.
Workout: Squats, Hip Thrusts, Lunges, Glute Bridges.

LOWER · 11
Quads (Quadriceps)

Extends the knee and helps in hip flexion. Essential for walking, running, jumping, and squatting.
Workout: Squats, Leg Press, Leg Extensions, Forward Lunges.

LOWER · 12
Hamstrings

Bends the knee and extends the hip. Important for running, jumping, and hip stability.
Workout: Romanian Deadlift, Leg Curls, Glute-Ham Raise, Nordic Hamstring Curl.

LOWER · 13
Calves (Gastrocnemius)

Points the foot downward (plantarflexion); essential for walking, running, jumping, and balance.
Workout: Standing Calf Raise, Seated Calf Raise, Jump Rope, Calf Press on Leg Press.

⚠️
Quads vs. Hamstrings — Opposite Functions

Quads (front of thigh) extend the knee; hamstrings (back of thigh) flex the knee and extend the hip. They're an anterior/posterior pair on the thigh, same logic as biceps/triceps on the arm.

CHAPTER 04
Summary & Application

Anatomical direction terms give you the vocabulary; the 13 muscle groups give you the content. Together they let you describe not just what a muscle does, but exactly where it is and how it relates to the muscles around it — the foundation for every practical and written PATHFIT1 assessment this semester.

How to Apply This

1
Describe location with direction terms first
Before naming a muscle, practice locating it: "posterior upper arm" instead of just pointing.
2
Pair opposite muscles with opposite functions
Biceps/triceps and quads/hamstrings are the two clearest push-pull, flex-extend pairs to memorize together.
3
Link function to at least one workout
Knowing "pecs push" is easier to recall when tied to a concrete movement like the bench press.
4
Group by region for review
Quiz yourself upper body vs. lower body separately before mixing all 13 together.
✅
Key Takeaways

8 anatomical direction terms come in 4 opposite pairs: anterior/posterior, superior/inferior, lateral/medial, distal/proximal · 13 major muscle groups split into 9 upper body and 4 lower body · Each muscle has one primary function and a matching set of exercises · Direction terms describe muscle location; function + workout pairing is what's tested most.
