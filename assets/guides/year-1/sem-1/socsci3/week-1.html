CHAPTER 01
Nation vs. State

These two words get used interchangeably in everyday speech, but the module draws a hard line between them — and that line is the foundation for everything else in this module.

POLITICAL ENTITY
State

A political entity established to fulfill security and ensure the common welfare of its people. A defined territory is essential to a state's existence, and a state's legal existence cannot operate without sovereignty. Example: Russia — the biggest state in the world by geographical size.

CULTURAL/EMOTIONAL UNIT
Nation

A solidified unit of people tied together by common history and cultural heritage, full of emotional, psychological, and spiritual bonds. A nation does not need an occupied territory, and does not need to possess sovereignty. Example: the Kurdish people — a nation with no official homeland, residing across Iraq, Iran, Turkey, Syria, and Armenia.

🎤
Recitation-Ready Answer

"A state is political — it needs territory and sovereignty. A nation is cultural — it's people bound by shared history and identity, and it doesn't need either territory or sovereignty to exist." Example pairing: Russia is a state (huge, defined territory, sovereign); the Kurds are a nation without a state of their own.

How Today's Map of States Came to Be

The module traces this through the collapse of empires. The Ottoman Empire controlled much of the Middle East for roughly 500 years before a long, slow decline despite efforts to modernize — and sealed its fate by siding with Germany in World War I, which ended in defeat to Britain. The Tsarist Russian Empire met a similar fate. The vacuum the Ottomans left let Britain and France redraw the Middle East's map, giving rise to kingdoms like Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Oman, Kuwait, and Jordan. The birth of Israel added to regional friction.

Today the Middle East has 18 states (13 of them part of the Arab world), while the Kurds and Palestinians still aspire to a fully legitimate state of their own.

📊
The World in Numbers

The world today has 195 states, of which 193 are UN member states. Only 2 are non-members: the Holy See (Vatican) and Palestine. Modern statehood was often born from bloody struggle — the Philippines' flag was raised in Kawit, Cavite in 1898, partly influenced by the Spanish-American War (rooted in Cuba); India's independence came through Mahatma Gandhi's non-violent movement against the British Raj.

CHAPTER 02
Essential Elements of a State

Per the Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States (1933), a state as a person of international law must have: (a) a permanent population, (b) a defined territory, (c) government, and (d) the capacity to enter into relations with other states. The module breaks this into four essential elements.

Population
ELEMENT 1
Not about size — it's about having people
Over 7 billion people, half in Asia. China and India are the most populous. Population size is not a criterion of statehood — Vatican and Nauru are states with very small populations. Historical note: Hitler's Germany and Mussolini's Italy incentivized having more babies; China once used the One-Child Policy for economic modernization.
Territory
ELEMENT 2
Land, water, and air space — fixed and defined
No state can exist without fixed territory, divided into administrative regions that mark its geographical limits. Giant examples: Russia, Canada, China, USA. Small-territory examples: Seychelles, Vanuatu, Tuvalu.
Government
ELEMENT 3
The institution that enforces the rules
People living together need organization and accepted rules — government is the institution mandated to enforce those rules and ensure obedience. Every state's government system has its own advantages/disadvantages. Example: Singapore — an authoritarian state ruled by a single dominant party for decades.
Sovereignty
ELEMENT 4
The right to make and enforce law within your own borders
Exercised when a state makes/enforces laws and collects taxes over its territory. Traditionally means no state may interfere in another's internal matters — but globalization has reshaped this view. Examples where sovereignty was challenged: the treatment of Rohingya Muslims in Myanmar, and international criticism of Brazil's handling of Amazon deforestation.
🎤
Recitation-Ready Answer

"Per the Montevideo Convention of 1933, a state needs four things: a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter relations with other states." If asked for a tricky example: Vatican City proves population size doesn't matter, and Singapore shows government doesn't have to be democratic to count.

CHAPTER 03
North-South Divide, East-West Collision

The module frames global division along two overlapping fault lines: one economic (North-South) and one cultural (East-West).

North-South: The Economic Divide

Much of the industrialized world sits in the Northern Hemisphere; developing economies are more often in the Southern Hemisphere. "The South" is often used interchangeably with the older term Third World. Some Southern countries have since broken into the industrialized tier:

NEWLY INDUSTRIALIZED COUNTRIES
NIC

Japan, Taiwan, South Korea, and Singapore achieved an "economic miracle" that put their socioeconomic standing on par with the industrialized world.

EMERGING MARKET BLOC
BRICS

Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa — globalization electrified these economies enough that they now mount a strong challenge to the status quo.

East-West: The Cultural Collision

The foundations of democratic governance and liberalism were enriched in the West, while autocracy took a strong foothold in the East. Modernization was first achieved in the West — which is why "modernization" is often (mistakenly) equated with "westernization." The module is explicit that Eastern countries pursued modernization without following the West's path.

💡
Common trap to avoid

Modernization ≠ Westernization. The module explicitly separates these — Eastern states modernized on their own terms, not by copying Western political models.

CHAPTER 04
Nation-States in the Age of Globalization

The module frames globalization's relationships among nation-states through three lenses: cultural, political, and economic — with economic self-interest as the earliest and most persistent driver.

A Very Condensed History of Globalization's Drivers

1
Bartering
Since the dawn of civilization, early peoples traded to access food, weapons, and spices.
2
Ancient empires
Alexander the Great's empire fused Greco-Macedonian culture with Asia. The Silk Road linked the Roman Empire (west) and the Chinese Empire (east) in flourishing trade.
3
Colonization
European empires (Spanish, Portuguese, Dutch, British) combined evangelization with capitalist expansion, extracting wealth from colonized "weaker" civilizations — from Mesoamerican gold to the Spice Islands (Moluccas) — to fund their own territorial growth.
4
The world wars & the rise of sovereign states
Two brutal global wars ended the age of empires. More sovereign states emerged and agreed to build a global order meant to be humane and prosperous.
5
Multinational corporations take the stage
State sovereignty as the sole agent of globalization was challenged by huge businesses whose capital, technology, and influence couldn't be ignored — Coca-Cola and McDonald's are the module's named examples, entrenching capitalism as the system nation-states needed to modernize.
6
The fall of the USSR
The collapse of the Communist regime in Moscow and the disintegration of the USSR marked capitalism's historic triumph in the ideological struggle between economic systems.
7
Technology tears down the remaining barriers
Innovation continues to erode barriers, especially in communication and information access.
📖
Al Gore's Six Drivers of Global Change

From The Future (2007 Nobel Peace Prize winner, former US Vice President): (1) a deeply interconnected global economy, (2) planet-wide electronic communication, (3) a completely new balance of political, economic, and military power, (4) rapid unsustainable growth, (5) a revolutionary new set of biological/biochemical/genetic/materials-science technologies, and (6) a radically new ecological relationship between humans and Earth's ecosystem.

CHAPTER 05
Defining Globalization

The module notes that defining globalization is genuinely hard — supporters like Thomas Friedman and Jeffrey Sachs call it a flattener and game-changer, while critics like Joseph Stiglitz have voiced sharp criticism of it. Four definitions are given as reference points.

Scholar / SourceDefinition (in their framing)
Thomas Friedman
author, The World is Flat		The interweaving of markets, technology, information, and telecommunications in a way that shrinks the world from size medium to size small — letting everyone reach around the world farther, faster, deeper, and cheaper than ever before.
Manfred StegerThe expansion and intensification of social relations and consciousness across world time and world space — a multidimensional phenomenon spanning economics, politics, culture, ideology, environment, and technology.
Anthony GiddensThe intensification of worldwide social relations linking distant localities, such that local happenings are shaped by events occurring thousands of miles away, and vice versa. Giddens also names 4 dimensions: the world capitalist economy, the nation-state system, the world military order, and industrial development.
International Monetary Fund (IMF)The increasing integration of economies around the world, mainly through the movement of goods, services, and capital across borders — sometimes also referring to the movement of people and knowledge.
🎤
Recitation-Ready Answer

If your professor asks "who said the world is flat?" — that's Thomas Friedman. If asked for the definition with 4 named dimensions, that's Giddens (capitalist economy, nation-state system, military order, industrial development). If asked for the institutional/economic definition, that's the IMF.

CHAPTER 06
Typologies of Globalization

Because of growing trade and international exchange, the module splits globalization into six typologies.

TypologyDefinitionExample (from module)
EconomicGrowing interdependence of global economies from cross-border exchange of goods/services, investment flow, and rapid tech diffusion.Trade agreements pursued by nation-states with regional or global implications.
PoliticalEnlargement of the global governance system and international organizations in influence, power, size, and complexity.The constant evolution of the United Nations.
CulturalA dominant culture penetrating a unique, localized culture, possibly fusing the two.Globalization of sports; globalization of food culture.
FinancialInterconnection of the global financial system — investment, monetary exchange, equity markets.A local stock market collapse triggering a global meltdown, e.g. the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis.
TechnologicalMade possible by innovation and popularization of tech devices and digital social platforms.Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, etc.
EcologicalConcerted efforts by states and non-state actors to address global environmental crises.The Paris Climate Agreement of 2015.
🎤
Recitation-Ready Answer

"There are six typologies: economic, political, cultural, financial, technological, and ecological — each describing a different channel through which countries become interconnected." Fastest way to remember an example per type: trade deals (economic), the UN (political), K-pop/fast food (cultural), the 1997 Asian crisis (financial), social media (technological), the Paris Agreement (ecological).

CHAPTER 07
The Six Metaphors of Globalization

This is the module's signature framework (drawn from Manfred Steger's work) — six metaphors that each capture a different "feel" of what globalization does to the world. Every argument listed under each metaphor is preserved from the module below.

METAPHOR 1
Flow
The lifting of barriers and easing of rules governing interstate commerce, human movement, and access to communication/information.
METAPHOR 2
Friction
The conflict, disparity, and crisis generated by the open-market system.
METAPHOR 3
Acceleration
The exponential growth of technological capacity, pushing globalization to a new height — humans co-existing with intelligent machines.
METAPHOR 4
Disruption
Startup innovations outperforming branded products in markets they once dominated.
METAPHOR 5
Dislocation
Individuals, businesses, and economies that can't keep pace with global change get displaced.
METAPHOR 6
Connectivity
Infrastructure that boosts global competitiveness amid the expanding international market system.

1. Flow — full argument list

  • The worth of global trade has grown several-fold compared to half a century ago.
  • Many people were lifted from poverty in developing economies like China and India through the open-market system.
  • Advanced industrialized economies welcomed immigrants and migrant workers, which hugely contributed to their GDP growth.
  • Regionally significant, globally impactful trade agreements were pursued — the EU in Europe, ASEAN in Southeast Asia, NAFTA in North America, APEC in the Asia-Pacific.
  • More democratized, cheaper access to communication and information — specifically credited to the birth of the Internet.

2. Friction — full argument list

  • Job markets in advanced economies weakened as capital moved to developing economies with cheaper labor, hurting First World middle-class income.
  • The trade war between the US and China.
  • Terrorist groups like Al-Qaeda and ISIS exploited the borderless world using available technology to sow public fear.
  • The scandalous income gap between the rich and the middle class (the "1% vs. the 99%").
  • Non-white people receiving racial slurs and harassment from white people in America.
  • Cyberwar as the "fifth domain of war."

3. Acceleration — full argument list

  • The increasing evolution of technology, such as Artificial Intelligence (AI).
  • The creation of new jobs requiring new sets of competencies and skills.
  • The open question: can intelligent machines outperform human beings?
  • New technologies will require less dependence on petroleum-based energy.
  • Data security is the main risk.
  • Global warming is a clear and present danger requiring extraordinary global cooperation, especially between the USA and China.

Note: acceleration also covers the problem of global population growth and global warming — both threaten human and species extinction as their pace speeds up.

4. Disruption — examples given

Startup innovations outperforming long-dominant branded products: Uber, Netflix, Airbnb, driverless cars. The module singles out China as the biggest disruption in the global economic system — a communist political ideology paired with a market-capitalist economic operation.

5. Dislocation — full argument list

  • Jobs created during the First, Second, and Third Industrial Revolutions will be replaced by automation unless workers gain the new skills the Fourth Industrial Revolution demands.
  • Small firms will be absorbed by big businesses.
  • Local markets suffer from the influx of cheap foreign goods resulting from bilateral or multilateral trade agreements.

6. Connectivity — full argument list

Connectivity has two dimensions: Hard Infrastructure (world-class airports, bridges, harbors, roads) and Soft Infrastructure (education systems, efficient internet access).

  • Singapore has the best airport in the world (Changi Airport).
  • China's One Belt, One Road revives the ancient Silk Road.
  • Countries with the most innovative, competitive economies also tend to have the best education systems.
🎤
Recitation Drill — The Six Metaphors
Q1
"Give one example each of Flow and Friction."
Flow: the birth of the Internet made communication cheaper and more democratic. Friction: the US-China trade war shows the conflict the open market can generate.
Q2
"How is Disruption different from Dislocation?"
Disruption is about new players (like Uber or Netflix) outperforming old dominant ones. Dislocation is about who gets left behind entirely — workers, small firms, or whole markets that can't keep up.
Q3
"What are the two dimensions of Connectivity?"
Hard Infrastructure (physical — airports, roads, harbors) and Soft Infrastructure (systemic — education, internet access).
