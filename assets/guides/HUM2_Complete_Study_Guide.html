CHAPTER 00
Course Overview

Ethics is a CHED-mandated General Education course examining how people determine what's right and wrong, and why. Unlike RIZAL (one person's story) or SOCSCI 3 (global systems), Ethics is about REASONING — you'll spend less time memorizing facts and more time learning to construct and evaluate moral arguments. Exams and papers in this course reward students who can APPLY a framework to a new scenario, not just recite its definition.

🧭
How This Course Actually Gets Tested

Expect fewer "define this term" questions and more "here's a scenario — what would a Utilitarian say? What would Kant say?" questions. The frameworks in Chapters 03–06 aren't trivia to memorize in isolation — they're tools you're expected to actively APPLY to case studies, including ones you've never seen before.

CHAPTER 01
What Is Ethics?

Before evaluating any moral question, you need precise vocabulary — the difference between ethics and morals, moral and non-moral standards, and whether right and wrong are universal or relative to culture. These distinctions get tested directly and underpin every later chapter.

Ethics vs Morals — A Useful Working Distinction

TermDefinition
MoralsThe actual beliefs, values, and codes of conduct a person or culture holds about right and wrong — lived, practiced standards.
EthicsThe philosophical STUDY of morals — systematic reflection on what makes actions right or wrong, and why. Ethics asks "why" morals are justified; it doesn't just describe them.

Moral vs Non-Moral Standards

Not every rule or standard is a MORAL one. This distinction is one of the most frequently tested concepts in the entire course.

TypeDefinitionExample
Moral standardsConcern actions that seriously affect the wellbeing, rights, or dignity of others; involve genuine right/wrong; not created by authority — they're discovered/justified through reasoning"Don't steal," "Don't harm innocent people"
Non-moral standardsRules of etiquette, law, grammar, fashion, or convention — matters of preference, custom, or arbitrary agreement, not fundamental right/wrong"Don't wear white after a funeral," "Drive on the right side of the road"
📌
Exam-Critical Distinction — Law vs Morality

Something can be LEGAL but IMMORAL (historically, slavery was legal in many places), and something can be ILLEGAL but arguably MORAL (civil disobedience against an unjust law, e.g. Rizal's own reformist writings were illegal under Spanish rule). Law and morality often overlap but are NOT the same thing — a frequently tested point.

Six Characteristics of Moral Standards

CharacteristicWhat It Means
Involve serious wrongs or benefitsMoral standards concern things that can genuinely harm or help people, not trivial preferences
OverridingMoral standards take priority over other standards (self-interest, law, custom) when they conflict
Not established by authorityA law can be repealed by a legislature; a genuine moral truth can't simply be voted away
UniversalizableIf an action is right/wrong for one person in a situation, it's right/wrong for anyone in a relevantly similar situation
ImpartialMoral standards consider everyone's interests fairly, not just one person's or one group's
Associated with special emotionsViolating moral standards typically triggers guilt, shame, or indignation — unlike breaking a non-moral rule

Cultural Relativism vs Ethical Universalism

PositionCore ClaimMain Criticism
Cultural RelativismWhat is morally right or wrong depends entirely on a given culture's beliefs — there is no single universal standard that applies across all societiesMakes it impossible to morally criticize ANY practice of another culture (even genuinely harmful ones), and can't explain moral progress within a culture over time
Ethical UniversalismCertain moral standards (e.g. against unjustified killing, torture) hold across all cultures, regardless of local belief or customRisk of imposing one culture's values onto others under the guise of "universal" truth
💭
A Common Essay Prompt on This Chapter

"Is morality relative or universal?" Strong answers acknowledge BOTH real cultural variation in customs (funeral rites, greetings, dress) AND argue that some core moral standards (like prohibitions on unjustified killing) appear across virtually all human societies in some form — suggesting a middle ground rather than a purely relativist or purely universalist position.

CHAPTER 02
The Moral Agent

A "moral agent" is someone capable of being held morally responsible for their actions. Not every being, and not every human act, qualifies. This chapter covers what makes an act genuinely moral — and what conditions can remove or reduce responsibility.

Three Conditions for a Moral Act

ConditionWhy It's Required
FreedomAn act must be done voluntarily. A person coerced, physically forced, or acting under genuine duress is NOT fully morally responsible for that act — freedom is the precondition for praise or blame.
ReasonThe agent must understand what they are doing and its likely consequences. A person who genuinely cannot understand the nature of their act (e.g. due to severe cognitive impairment) has diminished moral responsibility.
Will (Intention)The agent must intend the act, or at minimum, act knowingly. Accidents where genuinely no reasonable foresight was possible are treated differently from deliberate acts.
📌
Exam-Critical Application

These three conditions explain WHY the law (and everyday moral judgment) treats a person who kills in self-defense differently from a premeditated murderer, and why we don't hold young children or people with severe mental incapacity to the same moral standard as competent adults — one or more of freedom, reason, or will is compromised or absent.

Feelings vs Reason in Moral Judgment

A recurring philosophical debate: should moral decisions be guided primarily by RATIONAL analysis, or does genuine moral judgment require EMOTIONAL response (empathy, compassion, moral intuition) as well?

PositionCore Claim
Reason-centered viewEmotions are unreliable and biased; genuine moral reasoning must follow logical principles consistently, independent of how we happen to feel in the moment (this view underlies Kant's ethics, Ch. 04)
Feeling-centered viewPure logic alone cannot generate moral motivation — empathy and moral emotions (guilt, compassion, indignation) are what actually drive ethical behavior and are a legitimate SOURCE of moral knowledge, not just noise to filter out
Integrated view (most widely accepted today)Sound moral judgment typically requires BOTH — reason to evaluate consistency and consequences, and feeling to recognize what's actually at stake for those affected

Determinism vs Free Will — Why It Matters for Ethics

If human choices are entirely determined by prior causes (genetics, upbringing, brain chemistry, circumstance) with no genuine freedom involved, can anyone truly be held morally responsible for anything? This is a foundational question ethics must address before moral judgment can even begin.

PositionCore Claim
Hard DeterminismAll events, including human choices, are fully caused by prior events — genuine free will is an illusion, which would undermine traditional moral responsibility entirely
Libertarian Free WillHumans possess a genuine capacity for free, uncaused choice, at least in some situations — this preserves the traditional basis for moral responsibility
CompatibilismFree will and determinism can coexist — "freedom" just means acting according to one's own reasons and desires without external coercion, even if those reasons/desires themselves have prior causes; the most common working assumption for practical ethics
🇵🇭
Filipino Cultural Concept — Kagandahang-Loob & Loob

Filipino moral philosophy often centers the concept of loob (inner self/will) — genuine moral acts spring from one's authentic inner disposition, not mere external compliance. Kagandahang-loob (roughly, "beauty of will/inner goodness") describes acting rightly not from fear of punishment or external reward, but from a genuinely good inner character — a Filipino philosophical parallel to Western debates about intention and moral motivation covered in this chapter.

CHAPTER 03
Utilitarianism

The first of four major ethical frameworks — and the single most exam-heavy topic in this course. Utilitarianism judges actions by their OUTCOMES: an act is right if it produces the greatest good for the greatest number.

Core Principle

The Principle of Utility (or "Greatest Happiness Principle"): an action is morally right to the extent that it maximizes overall happiness/wellbeing and minimizes overall suffering, considering EVERYONE affected — not just the person acting.

Key Philosophers

Jeremy Bentham
1748–1832 · England
Founder of Utilitarianism
Proposed the "hedonic calculus" — a method to quantitatively measure pleasure/pain by intensity, duration, certainty, and other factors, to calculate which action produces the most net happiness.
John Stuart Mill
1806–1873 · England
Refined Utilitarianism
Argued not all pleasures are equal — distinguished "higher" pleasures (intellectual, moral) from "lower" ones (purely physical), famously stating it's "better to be a human dissatisfied than a pig satisfied."

Act Utilitarianism vs Rule Utilitarianism

TypeCore IdeaWeakness
Act UtilitarianismEvaluate EACH individual action by its own specific consequences — what maximizes utility in THIS particular situationCan justify clearly unjust acts (e.g. framing an innocent person) if doing so happens to produce better aggregate consequences in a specific case
Rule UtilitarianismFollow the RULE that, if generally followed, would produce the greatest utility overall — even if breaking it might produce better results in one specific instanceCan become inconsistent with pure Act Utilitarianism's own logic — if breaking a rule truly maximizes utility, why not break it?

Worked Case Application

CLASSIC SCENARIO — THE TROLLEY PROBLEM
A runaway trolley is about to kill 5 people. You can pull a lever to divert it onto a side track, where it will kill only 1 person instead.
Utilitarian analysis: Pull the lever. Five lives saved outweighs one life lost — the action that produces the greatest good for the greatest number is clearly the one that minimizes total deaths. This is the textbook case used to introduce Utilitarian reasoning, precisely because most people's initial intuition (pull the lever) aligns with the Utilitarian calculation — making it a useful entry point before harder variations complicate the picture.

Major Criticisms of Utilitarianism

CriticismExplanation
Justifies harming innocentsIf sacrificing one innocent person genuinely maximized overall utility, strict Act Utilitarianism would require it — conflicting with strong moral intuitions about individual rights
Impossible to calculate preciselyAccurately predicting and quantifying all consequences, for everyone affected, is practically impossible in real time
Ignores justice and rightsA purely outcome-focused view can override individual rights whenever violating them produces a "better" aggregate outcome — this criticism directly motivates the Deontological framework in Chapter 04
Demands too muchTaken strictly, Utilitarianism could require constantly sacrificing personal interests whenever doing so marginally increases total global utility — an extremely demanding standard for everyday life
🔗
Forward Link — Chapter 04

Utilitarianism's biggest weakness — that it can justify sacrificing an innocent individual for the greater good — is EXACTLY what Kant's Deontological ethics (next chapter) was built to reject. Understanding this direct tension between the two frameworks is one of the most commonly tested comparative questions in the course.

CHAPTER 04
Deontology — Kantian Ethics

Where Utilitarianism judges actions by OUTCOMES, Deontology judges actions by DUTY — whether the act itself follows a correct moral rule, REGARDLESS of consequences. This is the framework most directly built to fix Utilitarianism's biggest flaw.

Key Philosopher

Immanuel Kant
1724–1804 · Prussia (Germany)
Founder of Deontological Ethics
Argued moral worth comes from acting out of DUTY according to universal rational principles — not from the consequences an act happens to produce, and not from personal inclination or emotion.

The Categorical Imperative — Kant's Core Test

Kant proposed a rational test for determining whether an action is morally permissible, stated in several equivalent formulations. Two are most commonly taught:

FormulationThe Test
Universal Law"Act only according to that maxim whereby you can, at the same time, will that it should become a universal law." In plain terms: could EVERYONE act this way, without the action becoming self-defeating or contradictory? If not, the act is impermissible.
Humanity Formula"Act so that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means." People must never be used PURELY as tools for someone else's purposes — they must be respected as rational beings with inherent worth.

Worked Case Application

CLASSIC SCENARIO — THE LYING PROMISE
You desperately need money and consider borrowing it, promising to repay, while secretly knowing you never will.
Kantian analysis: Apply the Universal Law test — "Can I will that EVERYONE make false promises whenever convenient?" No: if lying promises became universal, the entire institution of promise-making would collapse (no one would trust a promise at all), making the very act of "promising" self-defeating. Therefore, making a false promise fails the test and is morally impermissible — REGARDLESS of how much good the borrowed money might do. This is the textbook illustration of duty overriding consequences.

Utilitarianism vs Deontology — Direct Comparison

AspectUtilitarianismDeontology (Kant)
What determines rightnessConsequences/outcomesThe act itself, judged against duty/rational principle
Trolley problem answerPull the lever — minimizes total deathsContested among Kantians, but strict duty-based reasoning resists treating a person as a mere means to save others, making the answer far less clear-cut
FlexibilityHigh — no act is absolutely forbidden if outcomes justify itLow — certain acts (like lying, using people merely as tools) are wrong regardless of outcome
Main strengthIntuitive for policy/large-scale decisions affecting many peopleProtects individual rights from being overridden "for the greater good"

Major Criticisms of Deontology

CriticismExplanation
Can produce rigid, seemingly wrong outcomesKant himself controversially argued lying is ALWAYS wrong, even to a murderer asking where your friend is hiding — critics see this as prioritizing rule-consistency over obviously better outcomes
Conflicting dutiesReal situations can present two genuine duties that conflict (e.g. duty to honesty vs duty to protect an innocent person) with no clear resolution mechanism within the framework itself
Abstract and hard to applyDetermining exactly how to phrase the "maxim" being universalized in a real situation is often genuinely ambiguous
📌
Exam-Critical Fact — "Deontology" Means Duty-Based

The term comes from the Greek deon ("duty/obligation"). Remembering this root helps you correctly distinguish Deontology (rule/duty-focused, Kant) from Utilitarianism (outcome-focused, Bentham/Mill) instantly on any identification question.

CHAPTER 05
Virtue Ethics

The oldest of the three major frameworks, and fundamentally different in approach — Virtue Ethics doesn't ask "what should I DO?" primarily, but "what kind of PERSON should I BE?" Good actions flow naturally from good character.

Key Philosopher

Aristotle
384–322 BC · Ancient Greece
Founder of Virtue Ethics
Argued the goal of human life is eudaimonia (flourishing/genuine wellbeing), achieved by cultivating virtuous character traits through habituated practice, not by following rules or calculating outcomes.

Eudaimonia — The Ultimate Goal

Eudaimonia is often translated as "happiness," but this is misleading — it means something closer to human FLOURISHING or living well, achieved through a lifetime of virtuous activity, not a fleeting emotional state. For Aristotle, eudaimonia is the ultimate end that all human action aims toward.

The Doctrine of the Golden Mean

Aristotle argued each virtue is a MEAN (middle point) between two vices — one of excess, one of deficiency. Virtue is not about extremes in either direction, but about finding the appropriate balance for a given situation.

Vice of DeficiencyVirtue (The Mean)Vice of Excess
CowardiceCourageRecklessness
StinginessGenerosityWastefulness
ShamelessnessModestyShyness/Bashfulness
ApathyPatience/Good TemperIrritability
Self-deprecationProper PrideVanity
📌
Exam-Critical Clarification — The Mean Is Not Simply "The Middle"

The Golden Mean is NOT a fixed mathematical midpoint — it's relative to the situation and the individual. What counts as "courage" varies depending on context (a soldier's courage looks different from a whistleblower's courage). This is a frequently tested nuance that separates a strong answer from a superficial one.

Virtue as Habit — Not Innate, Not Just Knowledge

Aristotle insisted virtues are develop through HABITUATED PRACTICE, not simply granted at birth or acquired purely through intellectual understanding. You become courageous by repeatedly performing courageous acts until courage becomes part of your character — similar to how you become a skilled musician through repeated practice, not just by reading music theory.

Worked Case Application

CLASSIC SCENARIO — THE TROLLEY PROBLEM, VIRTUE LENS
Applying Virtue Ethics to the same trolley scenario from Chapter 03.
Virtue Ethics analysis: Unlike Utilitarianism or Deontology, Virtue Ethics doesn't offer a single calculable "correct" answer — instead it asks: what would a genuinely PRACTICALLY WISE person (someone with phronesis, practical wisdom) do in this exact situation, considering courage, justice, and compassion together? This is often criticized as less decisive than the other two frameworks, but Virtue Ethicists argue real moral situations rarely reduce to a single clean rule anyway — character and judgment matter more than a formula.

Three Frameworks — Side-by-Side Summary

FrameworkCentral QuestionKey Thinker
Utilitarianism"What action produces the best outcome for everyone affected?"Bentham, Mill
Deontology"What does duty/universal rational principle require, regardless of outcome?"Kant
Virtue Ethics"What would a genuinely virtuous person do, and how do I become that kind of person?"Aristotle
🇵🇭
Filipino Connection — Pakikipagkapwa & Virtue

Filipino virtue concepts like pakikisama (getting along/camaraderie) and utang na loob (debt of gratitude/reciprocity) function similarly to Aristotelian virtues — they're character traits cultivated through lived social practice, not abstract rules, and are judged by contextual wisdom (pakiramdam, sensitivity to a situation) rather than rigid calculation.

CHAPTER 06
Natural Law & Justice/Rights

Two more frameworks that frequently appear alongside the "big three" — Natural Law grounds morality in human nature itself, while theories of Justice and Rights focus on fair distribution and inviolable entitlements.

Natural Law Theory

Thomas Aquinas
1225–1274 · Italy
Leading Natural Law Theorist
Argued morality is grounded in human NATURE itself — humans have inherent purposes (rationality, sociability, self-preservation), and moral acts are those that align with fulfilling these natural human ends.

Natural Law holds that certain moral truths can be discovered through REASON by examining human nature — they exist independently of any human-made law or convention, similar in spirit to Ethical Universalism (Ch. 01) but grounded specifically in what humans are naturally FOR.

Key PrincipleExplanation
Basic goodsLife, knowledge, sociability, reasonableness — things naturally good for any human being to pursue
"Do good, avoid evil"The foundational precept from which more specific moral rules are derived through reasoning about human nature
Human/civil law must align with natural lawAn unjust human law (one that violates natural law) is, in a meaningful sense, not genuinely binding — directly echoes the Rizal Law civil-disobedience discussion from your RIZAL course

Theories of Justice

John Rawls
1921–2002 · USA
"A Theory of Justice" (1971)
Proposed the "veil of ignorance" thought experiment: imagine designing society's rules WITHOUT knowing your own future position in it (rich/poor, majority/minority) — you'd rationally choose rules that are fair even to society's worst-off members, since you might BE that person.

Rawls's Two Principles of Justice

PrincipleContent
1. Equal Basic LibertiesEvery person has an equal claim to the same basic rights and freedoms (speech, conscience, political participation)
2. Difference PrincipleSocial/economic inequalities are only justified if they work to the benefit of society's LEAST advantaged members

Human Rights Theory

Human rights are moral entitlements held simply by virtue of being human — not granted by government, and (in principle) not revocable by government either. This framework underlies most modern international law, including the UN framework you studied in SOCSCI 3.

CharacteristicMeaning
UniversalApply to all humans, regardless of nationality, culture, or government recognition
InalienableCannot be legitimately taken away or voluntarily given up, even by the person themselves
IndivisibleCivil/political rights (speech, voting) and economic/social rights (education, healthcare) are considered equally fundamental, not a hierarchy
🔗
Connects to SOCSCI 3

Human rights theory here is the philosophical FOUNDATION for the UN human rights framework and international law concepts you covered in SOCSCI 3's Global Governance chapter — that course explained the institutions; this chapter explains the philosophical justification behind why those institutions claim authority at all.

CHAPTER 07
Making Moral Decisions

Knowing the four frameworks isn't enough — this chapter gives you a structured PROCESS for actually working through a real moral dilemma, which is exactly the skill most exams and case-study assignments are testing.

A Structured Moral Decision-Making Process

1
Identify the moral issue
What exactly is the ethical question at stake? Separate the moral issue from any legal, practical, or emotional side-issues clouding it.
2
Gather the relevant facts
Who is affected? What are the realistic options? What do we actually know versus assume? Many "dilemmas" partly dissolve once the facts are fully clear.
3
Identify the stakeholders
List everyone whose interests, rights, or wellbeing are affected by each possible choice — including people not directly present in the situation.
4
Apply multiple ethical frameworks
Work through the situation using Utilitarian, Deontological, and Virtue lenses (Ch. 03–05) separately. Where do they agree? Where do they conflict, and why?
5
Consider alternative courses of action
Is the dilemma truly binary, or are there creative third options that better satisfy multiple frameworks at once?
6
Make a reasoned decision
Choose a course of action and be able to articulate WHY — which framework(s) most strongly justify it, and honestly acknowledge what's being sacrificed by not choosing the alternatives.
7
Reflect on the outcome
After acting (or in a case study, after analyzing), evaluate whether the reasoning holds up — would you decide the same way in a similar future situation?
📌
Exam-Critical Skill — Multi-Framework Analysis

The strongest case-study answers don't just pick ONE framework and stop — they explicitly show how Utilitarian, Deontological, and Virtue perspectives EACH analyze the same scenario, then explain which framework's reasoning is most convincing for that specific case and why. This demonstrates you understand the frameworks as genuinely different tools, not interchangeable synonyms.

Worked Full Example

FULL CASE APPLICATION
A pharmacist has a life-saving medicine a poor family desperately needs but cannot afford. Should the pharmacist give it away for free, against store policy?
Utilitarian view: If the family's life saved clearly outweighs the pharmacist's financial loss and any precedent-setting harm, giving the medicine away maximizes overall utility.

Deontological view: Depends on which duty is emphasized — a duty to preserve life could justify giving the medicine away; a duty to honor contractual/professional obligations to the store could argue against it. Kant would likely stress the universalizability question: could everyone systematically break store policy whenever they judged it sympathetically justified?

Virtue Ethics view: Asks what a compassionate, practically wise pharmacist would do — likely weighing generosity and justice together, perhaps seeking a middle path (partial discount, connecting the family to assistance programs) rather than a stark either/or choice.

This is exactly the kind of multi-angle answer that scores well on case-study exams.
CHAPTER 08
Applied Ethics

This closing chapter applies the four frameworks (Ch. 03–06) to specific real-world domains — the kind of concrete, current topics most likely to appear in your final position paper or reflection essay.

Bioethics

IssueCore Ethical Tension
End-of-life care / euthanasiaAutonomy and mercy (reducing suffering) versus the sanctity of life and risk of abuse/coercion of vulnerable patients
AbortionBodily autonomy of the pregnant person versus the moral status and rights of the fetus — a genuinely contested question across all four frameworks
Genetic engineeringPotential to prevent disease and suffering versus risks of inequality (only the wealthy accessing enhancement) and "playing god" concerns rooted in Natural Law thinking

Environmental Ethics

Does nature have moral value only because of its USE to humans (anthropocentrism), or does the environment itself hold intrinsic moral worth regardless of human benefit (ecocentrism)? This directly connects to the climate justice discussion from your SOCSCI 3 course — the Philippines' extreme climate vulnerability despite minimal historical emissions is a frequently cited applied ethics case study combining Utilitarian (aggregate harm), Rights-based (a right to a livable environment), and Justice (who bears responsibility for fixing a problem they didn't primarily cause) reasoning simultaneously.

Technology & AI Ethics

IssueCore Ethical Tension
AI decision-makingEfficiency and consistency (Utilitarian appeal) versus accountability and transparency when an algorithm, not a person, makes a consequential decision
Data privacyConvenience and innovation versus an individual's right to control their own personal information (Rights-based framing)
Automation and job displacementOverall economic efficiency (Utilitarian) versus fairness to displaced workers who bear concentrated costs for society's diffuse benefit (Justice-based framing, echoing Rawls's difference principle from Ch. 06)

Direct Connection to Your BSIT Degree

As a future IT professional, this section isn't abstract — it previews SPI 101 (Social and Professional Issues, Year 3 Summer), a full course dedicated to professional and technology ethics specifically for IT practitioners. The frameworks you're learning right now (Ch. 03–06) are the actual toolkit used to reason through real dilemmas like "should I flag a security vulnerability that might get a colleague fired?" or "is it ethical to build a system I know could be misused?"

Business Ethics

IssueCore Ethical Tension
Fair wages vs profit maximizationShareholder interests (often framed in Utilitarian terms — efficient markets benefit society overall) versus worker dignity and fair compensation (Rights/Justice framing)
WhistleblowingLoyalty to employer/colleagues versus duty to prevent harm to the public — a genuine conflict-of-duties case, similar to the Deontological tension discussed in Ch. 04
Advertising to vulnerable groupsFree market persuasion versus exploiting cognitive biases or vulnerabilities (e.g. children, financially desperate people)

Filipino Moral Values in Applied Context

Filipino ethics often blends Western frameworks with distinctly local values: Bayanihan (communal unity/mutual aid) reflects Virtue Ethics' emphasis on cultivated generosity as character; Hiya (a sense of shame/social propriety) functions as an internalized moral regulator similar to Kantian duty, but is socially rather than purely rationally derived; Utang na loob (debt of gratitude) creates ongoing relational obligations that don't map neatly onto any single Western framework, illustrating that lived Filipino ethics is genuinely a SYNTHESIS, not a simple import of Western theory.

🎓
Closing Synthesis — The Question This Course Builds Toward

Nearly every final position paper in this course asks you to take a real, contested applied ethics issue and defend a position using MULTIPLE frameworks explicitly, while acknowledging the strongest objections to your own view. The strongest papers don't declare one framework "correct" and dismiss the others — they show genuine command of all four, using each to stress-test the others' conclusions.
