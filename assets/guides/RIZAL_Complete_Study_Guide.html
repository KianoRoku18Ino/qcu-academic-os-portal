CHAPTER 00
Course Overview

This course is not just another General Education requirement — it exists because of a specific Philippine law. Unlike your CC/WS subjects, RIZAL doesn't sit on your BSIT prerequisite spine; it's mandated for every single college student in the Philippines, regardless of degree program. This chapter explains why, then maps out exactly what the rest of this guide covers.

⚖️
Why This Course Legally Exists — Republic Act 1425

Signed into law on June 12, 1956, RA 1425 (the "Rizal Law") mandates that all schools, colleges, and universities in the Philippines — public and private — include courses on the life, works, and writings of José Rizal, with particular emphasis on his novels Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo. This is covered in full in Chapter 08. Every Filipino you know who finished college took some version of this exact course.

Who Was José Rizal — The One-Paragraph Version

José Protasio Rizal Mercado y Alonso Realonda (1861–1896) was a Filipino polymath — physician, novelist, poet, sculptor, linguist (fluent in over 20 languages), and reformist — whose writings exposed Spanish colonial abuses and helped ignite Filipino national consciousness. He never advocated armed revolution himself, favoring reform through peaceful means, yet Spanish authorities executed him by firing squad at age 35, an act that instead galvanized the very revolution he had hoped to avoid. He is recognized today as the national hero of the Philippines.

📌
Exam-Critical Fact — Full Birth Name

José Protasio Rizal Mercado y Alonso Realonda. He later dropped "Mercado" for most of his public/published life and used simply "José Rizal" — partly because his family had used "Mercado" as a surname since an ancestor adopted it to avoid Spanish suspicion, and partly for practical reasons while traveling and publishing abroad.

Why Calamba Matters

The Rizal family leased their farmland from the Dominican friars who owned much of Calamba. Escalating friar-imposed rent increases and eventual eviction disputes — which the family fought legally and lost, resulting in the demolition of their home in 1891 — became one of Rizal's most personal grievances against the friar estates. This lived experience of land injustice directly informed the agrarian and clerical abuse themes running through both of his novels.

CHAPTER 02
Education Abroad & Travels

Between 1882 and 1892, Rizal crossed the globe repeatedly — earning multiple degrees, mastering languages, and writing both of his novels while based in different European cities. This decade abroad transformed him from a promising UST medical student into the intellectual leader of the Filipino reform movement.

Timeline — The European Years (1882–1892)

1882
Universidad Central de Madrid, Spain
Continued Medicine, also earned a degree in Philosophy and Letters. Immersed himself in Madrid's liberal intellectual circles and joined the Filipino expatriate community pushing for reform.
1885
Completes his Licentiate in Medicine
Graduated from Universidad Central de Madrid. Moved on to Paris to further specialize.
1885–86
Paris, France
Studied ophthalmology under the renowned Dr. Louis de Wecker — directly connected to his motivation to treat his mother's eye condition.
1886
Heidelberg, Germany
Continued ophthalmology studies under Dr. Otto Becker at the University of Heidelberg. Wrote the poem "A las Flores de Heidelberg" during this period, reflecting on his homesickness.
1886
Wilhelmsfeld — finishes Noli Me Tangere
Completed the final chapters of his first novel while staying with Pastor Karl Ullmer in this small German village. Covered fully in Chapter 03.
1887
Berlin, Germany — Noli Me Tangere is published
Published at his own expense with financial help from friend Maximo Viola. Rizal was also inducted into several German scientific societies during this period, reflecting his growing international academic reputation.
1887
Brief return to the Philippines
Returned to Calamba but faced hostility from colonial authorities over Noli's content. Left again in 1888 after being advised his safety was at risk.
1888
London, England
Researched and annotated Antonio de Morga's 1609 historical text Sucesos de las Islas Filipinas at the British Museum — used to argue that the Philippines had an advanced, organized civilization BEFORE Spanish colonization, directly countering colonial propaganda of the era.
1889–91
Paris & Brussels — writing El Filibusterismo
Active in the Propaganda Movement, contributing to La Solidaridad. Wrote his second novel primarily in Brussels, Belgium — chosen partly because the cost of living was lower than Paris.
Sep 1891
Ghent, Belgium — El Filibusterismo is published
Published with financial assistance from Valentin Ventura after Rizal nearly had to halt printing due to lack of funds. Covered fully in Chapter 04.

Why the Travel Matters, Not Just the Dates

Memorizing city names and years is necessary but not sufficient — what actually gets tested is WHY each stop mattered. London gave him the historical evidence to dismantle colonial propaganda. Heidelberg and Paris gave him medical specialization AND homesickness that fed his writing. Understanding this cause-and-effect chain, not just the checklist of cities, is what separates a strong essay answer from a rote list.

CHAPTER 03
Noli Me Tangere

This is the single most exam-heavy topic in the entire course. Every character, theme, and plot beat below is something you should be able to identify and analyze, not just recognize by name.

Noli Me Tangere
"Touch Me Not" — from John 20:17, Christ's words to Mary Magdalene after the Resurrection
PublishedBerlin, 1887
GenreSocial novel / Political satire
LanguageSpanish (original)
Chapters64

Why This Title?

Rizal explained the biblical reference himself: it refers to a "social cancer" so sensitive and painful that even touching upon it — naming it publicly — was seen as taboo in colonial Philippine society. The novel's entire purpose was to touch that untouchable wound: the corruption and abuse woven through the Spanish colonial and religious administration.

Plot Summary

Juan Crisóstomo Ibarra, a young, European-educated Filipino mestizo, returns home to San Diego after seven years studying abroad, eager to reunite with his love, Maria Clara, and to honor his late father's memory by building a school. He soon learns his father, Don Rafael, died in prison after being falsely accused and persecuted by the town's friar, Padre Dámaso — largely due to a private conflict between the two men. Ibarra attempts reform through peaceful, legal means: petitioning for the school, seeking reconciliation rather than revenge. His efforts are sabotaged at every turn by corrupt friars and officials, culminating in a fabricated accusation of rebellion during the school's cornerstone-laying ceremony. Betrayed, excommunicated, and hunted, Ibarra is aided in escape by the mysterious outlaw Elías. Maria Clara, manipulated into surrendering letters that seal Ibarra's fate, ultimately enters a convent rather than marry the man chosen for her, Linares. The novel ends ambiguously — Ibarra presumed dead, Elías dying in the forest, and the "cancer" of the title left unresolved, setting up its sequel.

Major Themes & Symbolism

The Title Itself
"Touch Me Not" symbolizes the untouchable social wound of colonial abuse — a cancer so deep that even acknowledging it publicly was dangerous.
Maria Clara
Frequently interpreted as an allegory for the Philippines itself: beautiful and beloved, yet cloistered, controlled, and ultimately unable to determine her own fate.
Sisa's Madness
Represents the psychological toll of colonial abuse on ordinary Filipino families, not just the elite.
Ibarra's School Project
Symbolizes the belief that EDUCATION, not violence, was the path to reform — a belief the novel's events ultimately test and complicate.
Elías vs. Ibarra
Represents the two competing paths available to reform-minded Filipinos: peaceful petition (Ibarra) versus armed resistance born of repeated injustice (Elías).
ThemeHow It Appears in the Novel

Historical Impact

The novel was immediately banned by Spanish colonial authorities in the Philippines upon its 1887 publication — possessing a copy was itself considered dangerous. Its popularity, spread through smuggled and clandestine copies, was directly cited as one of the causes investigated after the outbreak of the Philippine Revolution in 1896, and was a central factor cited during Rizal's own trial.

CHAPTER 04
El Filibusterismo

The sequel — darker, angrier, and far more overtly political than Noli. Where Noli exposed the wound, Fili asks what happens when that wound is left to fester unaddressed for years.

El Filibusterismo
"The Filibustering" / "The Subversive" — dedicated to the memory of Gomburza
PublishedGhent, Belgium, 1891
GenrePolitical novel
LanguageSpanish (original)
Chapters38

Plot Summary

Thirteen years after the events of Noli, Ibarra returns to the Philippines in disguise as Simoun, a wealthy, mysterious jeweler with close, trusted ties to the Spanish Governor-General. Believed dead, he has spent the intervening years accumulating wealth and influence, secretly plotting revenge and revolution — no longer believing reform is possible through peaceful means. Simoun deliberately worsens colonial corruption and abuse from within, reasoning that accelerating the system's decay will provoke the uprising he now believes is the only path forward. His plan reaches its climax at a wedding celebration, where he has hidden an explosive lamp intended to kill the colonial elite gathered there — but the plot is discovered and foiled at the last moment by his own hesitation, triggered by concern for a former loved one, Isagani. Wounded and pursued, Simoun flees to the home of Padre Florentino, where he confesses his true identity and reveals the poison he carries, dying as he questions whether violence was ever truly the right path, or whether it simply repeated the same cycle of suffering.

Character Profiles

PROTAGONIST
Simoun
Ibarra, Transformed
Ibarra's alter ego — now cynical, wealthy, and willing to use violence and manipulation to provoke revolution. Represents what happens when peaceful reform is repeatedly crushed: radicalization.
FOIL

The Young Idealist
A student and poet who still believes in reform and love over vengeance. His last-minute intervention — removing the bomb-lamp out of concern for his former love Paulita — foils Simoun's entire plot.
SUPPORTING

A retired, principled priest who hears Simoun's dying confession. His closing reflection on suffering, sacrifice, and whether the Philippines is "worth" freedom obtained through bloodshed is one of the novel's most analyzed passages.
SUPPORTING

Now a medical student, connecting directly back to Noli's tragedy. His personal arc — pursuing education despite the system's cruelty to his family — parallels and contrasts with Simoun's path of vengeance.
SUPPORTING
Paulita Gómez

Ultimately chooses a wealthier suitor over Isagani — a subplot reflecting the same social pressures and colonial class dynamics seen throughout both novels.

Represents the seductive but ultimately self-destructive nature of violent revolution — beautiful and radiant, but built to kill indiscriminately.

⚠️
Common Misconception — Rizal Was NOT Advocating Violence

A frequent student mistake is reading Fili's ending as Rizal endorsing Simoun's violent methods. The novel's actual conclusion is deeply ambivalent — Simoun dies uncertain and unfulfilled, and Padre Florentino explicitly questions whether the Philippines deserved freedom "bought with crime." Rizal himself remained a reformist, not a revolutionary, throughout his life — a fact frequently tested directly, since the Katipunan later invoked him as a symbol despite this distinction.

🔗
Why This Matters — Setting Up Chapter 05

Both novels were written DURING Rizal's active involvement in the Propaganda Movement — they were not separate from his political activism, but a direct extension of it. Chapter 05 covers the broader movement these novels were part of.

CHAPTER 05
The Propaganda Movement

Rizal did not work alone. This chapter covers the broader reform movement he was part of — the organizations, publications, and fellow reformists who collectively pushed for Philippine representation and rights under Spain, before the movement's eventual failure pushed events toward outright revolution.

What Was the Propaganda Movement?

A reform movement (roughly 1872–1892) led by Filipino ilustrados (educated elites), mostly based in Spain, who sought PEACEFUL, LEGAL reforms to Spanish colonial rule in the Philippines — NOT independence. Their core demands included: Philippine representation in the Spanish Cortes (parliament), secularization of parishes (replacing Spanish friars with Filipino secular priests), freedom of the press, and equal treatment of Filipinos under Spanish law.

📌
Exam-Critical Distinction — Reform, Not Revolution

The Propaganda Movement sought REFORM within the Spanish colonial system — better treatment, representation, and rights AS Spanish subjects. This is fundamentally different from the Katipunan (founded 1892 by Andrés Bonifacio), which sought outright INDEPENDENCE through armed revolution. Rizal was central to the former, not the latter — though the Katipunan later claimed him as an inspirational figure regardless.

Key Organizations & Publications

Rizal's Political Essays

Beyond his novels, Rizal published influential essays through La Solidaridad that argued directly for reform:

EssayYearCore Argument

Why the Movement Ultimately Failed

  • Spain never granted the core demands — no Cortes representation, no meaningful secularization, censorship continued.
  • Internal rivalry — tension between Rizal and del Pilar over leadership and direction weakened unified messaging.
  • Funding problems — La Solidaridad constantly struggled financially, limiting its reach and consistency.
  • Colonial backlash — Rizal's return and the swift dissolution of La Liga Filipina in 1892 signaled that even purely civic, non-violent organizing was not going to be tolerated.
🔗
Why This Matters — The Direct Path to Chapter 06

La Liga Filipina's dissolution and Rizal's arrest happened within days of each other in July 1892. This is not a coincidence — colonial authorities viewed ANY organized Filipino civic activity as a threat, regardless of its explicitly peaceful stated aims. This arrest led directly to his exile in Dapitan, covered next.

CHAPTER 06
Exile in Dapitan

Four years (1892–1896) in a remote Mindanao town became, paradoxically, one of the most productive and content periods of Rizal's life — far from the political dangers of Manila and Europe, yet still deeply engaged in improving the community around him.

Timeline — Dapitan Years

Jul 1892
Arrest & Exile Ordered
Days after founding La Liga Filipina, Rizal was arrested by Spanish authorities on charges relating to the discovery of anti-friar pamphlets and his general reformist activities. Exiled without formal trial to Dapitan, a remote town in what is now Zamboanga del Norte, Mindanao.
1893
Settles into exile life
Lived under the custody of the local Spanish commandant. Rather than remain idle, began practicing medicine, treating patients from across the region — including, notably, performing eye surgery on his own mother when she visited.
1893–95
Community development projects
Built a water supply system for the town, established a school teaching local boys reading, writing, arithmetic, and practical trades. Also engaged in farming, fishing, and various engineering projects, applying his broad self-taught expertise practically.
1895
Relationship with Josephine Bracken
Met and formed a relationship with Josephine Bracken, an Irish woman who had brought her adoptive father to Rizal for eye treatment. They were later considered common-law partners; a formal Catholic marriage was reportedly performed only shortly before his execution.
Jul 1896
Volunteers for Cuba, departure begins the final chapter
Rizal requested and received permission to serve as a volunteer army doctor in Cuba (then also under Spanish rule, dealing with yellow fever). He departed Dapitan — but the outbreak of the Philippine Revolution during his voyage would change everything, covered in Chapter 07.

Why Dapitan Matters Beyond the Timeline

ContributionSignificance
Water supply systemDemonstrated Rizal's practical engineering skill, not just literary/political talent — a self-taught, functioning public works project.
School for local boysDirectly lived out his belief (from Noli Me Tangere's central Ibarra subplot) that education was the true path to societal improvement.
Medical practiceTreated patients regardless of background, cementing his reputation as a healer as much as a writer — patients reportedly traveled from other regions specifically to see him.
Scientific collectionsContinued correspondence with European scientific societies, sending specimens of local flora and fauna collected during his exile.
💭
A Common Essay Prompt on This Chapter

Professors frequently ask students to reflect on the irony of Dapitan: a man punished and exiled for his political writing spent his exile doing the MOST tangible, immediate good for ordinary Filipinos — clean water, education, healthcare — largely disconnected from the political writing that got him exiled in the first place. Consider this contrast when preparing reflection essays on this chapter.

CHAPTER 07
Trial & Execution

This chapter covers the final months of Rizal's life — his arrest amid a revolution he did not start, an unjust trial, and the execution that transformed him from a reformist writer into the movement's most powerful martyr.

Timeline — The Final Months (1896)

Aug 1896
The Philippine Revolution begins
While Rizal was en route to Cuba via Spain, the Katipunan — the revolutionary society founded by Andrés Bonifacio — launched the armed uprising against Spanish rule, beginning with the "Cry of Pugad Lawin/Balintawak." Rizal had no involvement in planning this and had explicitly not endorsed armed revolution.
Oct 1896
Arrested at sea
Rizal was arrested aboard ship near Barcelona and sent back to Manila to face trial, accused of being the inspiration and mastermind behind the revolution — despite his actual documented opposition to the Katipunan's methods.
Nov–Dec 1896
Imprisonment at Fort Santiago
Held while military authorities prepared the case against him. During this imprisonment, Rizal wrote his final testament and prepared his defense, though the trial's outcome had effectively been predetermined.
Dec 26 1896
Court-Martial & Conviction
Tried before a military court on charges of rebellion, sedition, and forming illegal associations. The trial is widely regarded by historians as a sham — Rizal was denied adequate legal defense, and evidence used against him relied heavily on his NOVELS being treated as literal blueprints for revolution rather than works of fiction and political commentary. Sentenced to death by firing squad.
Dec 29 1896
Final night — writes Mi Último Adiós
In his final hours at Fort Santiago, Rizal composed his farewell poem, later titled "Mi Último Adiós" ("My Last Farewell"). He reportedly hid the poem inside an alcohol lamp, which was discovered by his family only after his execution. Covered in detail below.
Dec 30 1896
Execution at Bagumbayan (Luneta)
Executed by firing squad at 7:03 AM, in what is now Rizal Park, Manila. Witnesses reported that as he fell, he twisted his body so as to face the sky/upward, rather than fall face-down — a detail frequently cited symbolically in retellings of his death. He was 35 years old.
✒️
Mi Último Adiós — Context, Not Reproduction

The poem is composed of 14 five-line stanzas in Spanish, written the night before his execution as a farewell to his homeland, family, and the cause of Philippine freedom. It reflects on love of country, acceptance of death, and hope for the nation's future — its closing lines express a wish for eternal rest once the Philippines is finally free. This guide describes the poem's structure and themes rather than reproducing its verses; your professor will assign a specific translated edition for direct textual analysis. Analyzing the poem's exact imagery, structure, and translated wording is a near-certain exam or reflection paper topic.

Was the Trial Fair? — The Standard Analytical Question

ElementHistorical Assessment
Legal representationAssigned a military defense counsel with limited ability to mount a genuine defense; not a lawyer of Rizal's own choosing.
Evidence usedHis NOVELS (fiction) were presented as direct evidence of seditious intent, alongside circumstantial claims linking him to the Katipunan despite his actual documented opposition to their armed methods.
Predetermined outcomeMost historians agree colonial authorities had already decided to make an example of Rizal, given his symbolic importance to Filipino reformist and nationalist sentiment, regardless of his actual direct involvement in the revolution.
Historical verdict todayWidely regarded by historians as a politically motivated conviction rather than a fair application of justice — Rizal's actual writings and testimony consistently show he favored reform over armed revolt.

The Central Irony

Rizal spent his entire adult life arguing AGAINST armed revolution, believing reform through peaceful, legal means was the correct path — a position that put him at odds with more radical figures. Yet he was executed specifically FOR inspiring a revolution he did not organize, did not endorse, and had in fact warned against. His execution then became the single greatest catalyst for the very revolution he opposed — Filipino revolutionaries invoked his martyrdom as justification and motivation for continued armed struggle against Spain, which ultimately succeeded in 1898.

📌
Exam-Critical Fact — He Did NOT Endorse the Katipunan

This is one of the most commonly tested distinctions in the entire course. Rizal was offered the chance to join or endorse the Katipunan's armed uprising and consistently declined, believing the movement was premature and would fail without adequate arms and organization. His conviction for "inspiring" the revolution was based on his broader reformist writings and symbolic influence — NOT on any actual direct participation or endorsement of the armed uprising itself.

CHAPTER 08
The Rizal Law & Lasting Legacy

This final chapter returns to the question raised in the Overview: why does a mandatory college course about one man's life and two novels exist at all? The answer involves a genuinely contested legislative battle — one worth understanding in full, since "discuss the significance/controversy of the Rizal Law" is a near-universal final exam or reflection paper prompt.

Republic Act 1425 — The Rizal Law

DetailInformation
Full title"An Act to Include in the Curricula of All Public and Private Schools, Colleges and Universities Courses on the Life, Works and Writings of José Rizal..."
Signed into lawJune 12, 1956 (Independence Day) by President Ramon Magsaysay
Principal sponsorSenator Claro M. Recto
Core requirementMandates the study of Rizal's life, works, and writings — with SPECIFIC emphasis on Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo — in all Philippine schools, colleges, and universities, public and private.

The Controversy — Why This Law Was Actually Contested

The Rizal Bill faced significant opposition BEFORE its passage, primarily from the Catholic Church hierarchy in the Philippines. This is one of the most commonly misunderstood aspects of the law's history — many students assume it was universally welcomed, when in fact it was a genuine legislative battle.

PositionArgument
Church & Catholic groups opposedArgued that both novels — particularly Noli Me Tangere — were anti-Catholic and anti-clergy, and that mandating Catholic students to read them violated freedom of religion by forcing exposure to content critical of the Church.
Senator Recto & supportersArgued the novels were essential to understanding Philippine national identity and history, and that exposure to ideas — even critical ones — was fundamental to genuine education, not indoctrination.
The compromise reachedThe final law allowed exemption from reading the UNEXPURGATED (unedited) original texts on grounds of religious belief, provided students still study Rizal's life and the novels' content through other means (summaries, excerpts, expurgated editions).
📌
Exam-Critical Fact — The Compromise Clause

RA 1425 does NOT force every student to read the complete unedited original novels if this conflicts with genuine religious conviction — but it DOES require them to study Rizal's life and be exposed to the novels' content and significance regardless. This compromise clause is exactly the kind of specific legal detail professors test to distinguish students who skimmed a summary from those who understood the actual legislative debate.

Why Rizal, Specifically? — The National Hero Question

Unlike many countries where a national hero is formally declared by law, the Philippines has no single official statute explicitly naming Rizal "the" national hero — his status emerged through a combination of: American colonial-era promotion (which favored Rizal's peaceful reformism over more revolutionary figures like Bonifacio, for political reasons following their own takeover of the Philippines), the widespread popular reverence that followed his martyrdom, and decades of subsequent institutional reinforcement including RA 1425 itself.

💭
A Frequently Assigned Debate Topic

Some historians and educators have argued OTHER figures — particularly Andrés Bonifacio, who actually led the armed revolution — deserve equal or greater recognition as national hero, given Rizal's explicit opposition to armed revolt. This is a legitimate, still-discussed historiographical debate, not settled trivia — expect it to appear as a reflection or position paper prompt.

Closing Synthesis — Connecting All Eight Chapters

Trace the full arc: a curious, gifted child shaped by injustice (Ch. 01) becomes an internationally educated polymath (Ch. 02) who channels that education into two novels exposing colonial abuse (Ch. 03–04), works within an organized peaceful reform movement (Ch. 05), continues serving his community even in exile (Ch. 06), and is ultimately executed for a revolution he opposed — becoming, in death, the very symbol that fueled it (Ch. 07). That entire arc is WHY a specific law now mandates every Filipino student encounter his story (Ch. 08). Understanding this as one continuous, connected narrative — not eight disconnected trivia sets — is what separates strong exam and essay performance in this course from rote memorization.
