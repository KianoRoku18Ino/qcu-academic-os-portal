CHAPTER 00
Course Overview

The Contemporary World is one of the CHED-mandated General Education courses required across ALL Philippine college programs, regardless of major. It examines globalization — the accelerating interconnection of the world's economies, governments, cultures, and peoples — and asks what that interconnection means for ordinary people, nations, and the Philippines specifically. This edition of the guide has been restructured chapter-by-chapter to follow the official Week 1–18 Outcomes-Based Teaching and Learning (OBTL) Plan issued for this course, so each chapter lines up with what's actually being covered in class that week, rather than a general independent reference order.

🌍
Why "Contemporary" Actually Matters Here

This is not a history course. When your professor asks about "the current state of globalization," they want you to reference genuinely recent developments — trade tensions, shifting alliances, new technology — not decade-old textbook examples. This guide includes several 2025–2026 developments specifically because currency is part of the actual subject matter.

WEEK 1–2
CHAPTER 01
Understanding Globalization & Its Metaphors

Before analyzing globalization's effects, you need a precise working definition, a map of its dimensions, and — per your syllabus — familiarity with the well-known "metaphors" scholars and journalists use to explain it in vivid, memorable terms. This chapter directly supports the "A Picture Says A Thousand Words" group activity, since each metaphor below is essentially a compact visual image for a bigger idea.

Working Definition

Globalization is the ongoing process by which economies, societies, cultures, and populations become increasingly interconnected and interdependent through the accelerated flow of trade, capital, people, information, and ideas across national borders. It is NOT a single event with a start date — it is an intensifying, uneven, and contested PROCESS, still actively unfolding and even reversing in some areas today.

The Five Dimensions of Globalization

💰
Economic
Global trade, finance, multinational corporations, supply chains
🏛️
Political
International organizations, treaties, shared governance, sovereignty tension
🎭
Cultural
Shared media, values, lifestyles spreading across borders
💻
Technological
Internet, mobile networks, AI — the infrastructure enabling all other dimensions
🌱
Ecological
Climate change, pollution, and resource use as borderless, shared problems
🇵🇭
Filipino Example Touching All Five Dimensions

A single Filipino nurse working in the UK sending money home via GCash touches all five at once: economic (remittance flow), political (bilateral labor agreements between PH and UK), cultural (Filipino traditions carried abroad, British culture absorbed), technological (instant digital money transfer), and ecological (the carbon footprint of international travel itself). This is the kind of multi-dimensional example professors want you to construct on essay exams.

Three Major Theoretical Perspectives on Globalization

Scholars disagree sharply on how significant, new, and beneficial globalization actually is. Three broad camps dominate the academic debate — expect exam questions asking you to identify or apply one of these.

PerspectiveCore ClaimKey Idea
HyperglobalistGlobalization is real, new, and transformative — the nation-state is becoming less relevant as global markets and institutions take overBorders are dissolving; we live in a genuinely "borderless world" economically
SkepticGlobalization is exaggerated — international trade levels today aren't fundamentally different from earlier historical peaks (e.g. pre-WWI trade), and nation-states remain the dominant actorsWhat we call "globalization" is mostly regionalization between blocs (EU, ASEAN, NAFTA/USMCA), not true global integration
TransformationalistGlobalization is real and significant, but its outcomes are unpredictable, uneven, and actively reshape (not eliminate) state powerThe most widely accepted "middle ground" view among contemporary scholars — globalization changes HOW states govern, not WHETHER they govern
📌
Exam-Critical Fact — 2026 Events Actually Support the Skeptic View

The United States formally withdrawing from over 60 international organizations in January 2026 and rejecting the UN's Sustainable Development Goals is a textbook example Skeptics would point to: even the world's most powerful economy can simply choose to disengage from global institutions when it suits national interest — evidence that state sovereignty, not borderless global governance, still drives outcomes. This is a genuinely current, citable example for essay questions on this theory.

Roland Robertson

Argued global and local forces don't simply replace each other — they blend, producing hybrid outcomes adapted to local contexts. Central to Chapter 06.

The Metaphors of Globalization

Because "globalization" is such an abstract, sprawling process, several influential writers built their entire arguments around a single vivid metaphor or image — partly to make the concept memorable, partly to push a specific interpretation of what globalization IS. These are the metaphors most commonly compiled in Philippine "Contemporary World" reviewers and modules; each one doubles as a visual you could sketch for the "A Picture Says A Thousand Words" activity.

Metaphor / WorkAuthorCore Idea
The Lexus and the Olive TreeThomas FriedmanGlobalization pits the drive for modern prosperity and technology (the Lexus car) against the pull of tradition, identity, and community (the olive tree) — nations must find balance between the two, not choose only one.
The World Is FlatThomas FriedmanDigital technology has "flattened" the global economic playing field, letting individuals, small firms, and developing economies compete directly against large, established ones.
Jihad vs. McWorldBenjamin BarberGlobalization provokes two opposite reactions at once: a homogenizing, corporate consumer culture ("McWorld") on one side, and a defensive retreat into tribal, ethnic, or religious identity ("Jihad") on the other.
The Clash of CivilizationsSamuel HuntingtonPost-Cold War global conflict is driven less by ideology or economics and more by cultural and religious fault lines between major civilizational blocs (Western, Islamic, Sinic, etc.).
The End of HistoryFrancis FukuyamaWith the Cold War's end, liberal democracy and free-market capitalism represent the final, most evolved form of political-economic organization, toward which all societies are gradually converging.
McDonaldizationGeorge RitzerThe organizing principles of the fast-food industry — efficiency, calculability, predictability, and control — increasingly shape institutions worldwide far beyond food, standardizing global life.
📌
Verify Against Your Own Module/PPT

These six are the most widely circulated set in Philippine GE reviewers for this exact topic, but the precise roster your professor emphasizes can vary slightly depending on which module or textbook chapter was assigned. When you receive the actual PPT or module for this week, cross-check the list — the underlying idea (each metaphor = one author's central argument about globalization) will still hold either way.

💭
How These Connect Back to the Three Perspectives

Notice the metaphors aren't neutral — each leans toward one of the three theoretical camps above. Friedman's two books ("Lexus," "Flat") are essentially hyperglobalist arguments dressed up as travel narratives. Barber's "Jihad vs. McWorld" and Huntington's "Clash of Civilizations" are more skeptical of a smooth, unified global future. Being able to say WHICH camp a given metaphor supports, and why, is a strong move on an identification or short-essay item.

CHAPTER 02
Structure of Globalization, the Modern Global Economy & the Fourth Industrial Revolution

This chapter covers three linked ideas from your syllabus: HOW globalization is structured as a system, the specific HISTORY of how today's global economy was built, and the technological wave — the Fourth Industrial Revolution — currently reshaping that economy in real time.

The "Structures" of Globalization

Where Chapter 01 introduced globalization's five DIMENSIONS as a way of describing what kind of activity is happening, thinking in terms of structures asks a slightly different question: what are the actual systems and institutions that carry each dimension out? Most Contemporary World modules organize the rest of the course around exactly these structures — which is also, not coincidentally, how this guide's remaining chapters are organized.

History of the Modern Global Economy

Today's global economic system wasn't inevitable — it was deliberately built, mostly after two catastrophic collapses in global cooperation.

1914–1945
The Great Reversal
Two World Wars and the Great Depression caused a dramatic RETREAT from global trade and integration — proof that globalization is not automatically permanent or one-directional. Countries turned inward (protectionism, tariffs) during this period.
1944–1947
Post-War Institution Building
The Bretton Woods Conference (1944) created the IMF and World Bank. The GATT (General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, precursor to the WTO) was signed in 1947 — deliberately designed to prevent a repeat of the 1930s trade collapse.
1989–1991
Post-Cold War Acceleration
Fall of the Berlin Wall (1989) and Soviet collapse (1991) opened former communist economies to global markets — widely seen as the start of the most intense phase of globalization to date.
1995
World Trade Organization Founded
Replaced GATT with a permanent, more powerful institution to govern global trade rules and resolve disputes between nations.
2001–2010s
The Digital & Internet Wave
China joins the WTO (2001), smartphones and broadband internet spread globally, social media connects billions instantly — capital and information now move at near-zero marginal cost across borders.
2025–2026
Rising Deglobalization Pressures
Renewed tariff disputes, the US withdrawing from dozens of international bodies, and a new 1% US tax on cross-border remittances (including OFW money transfers) signal a possible partial retreat from deep global integration — actively debated as this guide is being written.

The Big Three Post-War Institutions

IMF
Founded 1944, Bretton Woods
International Monetary Fund
Monitors global financial stability, provides emergency loans to countries facing balance-of-payments crises, usually attached to policy conditions ("structural adjustment") the borrowing country must follow.
World Bank
Founded 1944, Bretton Woods
International Bank for Reconstruction & Development
Provides long-term loans and grants for development projects (infrastructure, education, health) in developing countries, distinct from the IMF's short-term crisis focus.
WTO
Founded 1995, Geneva
World Trade Organization
Sets global trade rules, negotiates trade agreements between member states, and provides a formal system for resolving trade disputes between countries.
⚠️
Common Point of Confusion — IMF vs World Bank

Students frequently mix these up. Simple distinction: the IMF is like an emergency room — short-term crisis loans to stabilize a country's finances. The World Bank is like a long-term investor — development loans for building things (roads, schools, power grids) over years or decades.

Multinational Corporations, Supply Chains & Trade Policy

Multinational corporations (MNCs) operate production, sales, or services across multiple countries simultaneously — among the most powerful economic actors in globalization, sometimes with revenues exceeding the GDP of entire nations. A modern product is rarely made in one country: a single smartphone might have chip design in the US, semiconductor fabrication in Taiwan, rare-earth mineral mining in the DRC, and assembly in China or Vietnam — a global supply chain in action.

🇵🇭
Philippine Example — BPO Industry

The Philippine Business Process Outsourcing (BPO) industry — call centers, back-office processing, IT services — is a direct product of global supply chains extending into SERVICES, not just physical goods, employing over a million Filipinos.

ApproachCore IdeaTools Used
Free TradeMinimal government interference in cross-border trade; let comparative advantage determine what each country producesTrade agreements (FTAs), tariff reduction, open borders for goods/capital
ProtectionismShield domestic industries from foreign competition to protect local jobs and strategic sectorsTariffs (import taxes), import quotas, subsidies for domestic producers
📌
Current 2025–2026 Example — Rising Protectionism

Renewed tariff disputes between major economies through 2025–2026, alongside the new 1% US tax specifically targeting outbound remittances, are live examples of protectionist tools being actively deployed right now.

The Fourth Industrial Revolution

Coined by Klaus Schwab, founder of the World Economic Forum, the Fourth Industrial Revolution (4IR) describes the current wave of technological change built on top of the digital foundation laid by the Third Industrial Revolution — but going further by blurring the lines between the physical, digital, and biological worlds.

RevolutionEraDefining Shift
1stLate 1700sSteam power, mechanization of production
2ndLate 1800s–early 1900sElectricity, mass production, assembly lines
3rd1960s–2000sElectronics, computers, the early internet — the "Digital Revolution"
4th2010s–presentAI, robotics, the Internet of Things, biotechnology, and nanotechnology converging and merging the physical, digital, and biological worlds

Why 4IR Is Different, Not Just "More Technology"

  • Velocity — change is happening at an exponential, not linear, pace
  • Breadth and depth — entire systems of production, management, and governance are being transformed, not just individual products
  • Systems impact — it reshapes whole industries, economies, and even raises questions about what it means to be human (as biology and technology increasingly merge)
⚖️
Opportunities vs. Risks

Opportunities: new industries, major productivity gains, medical breakthroughs, improved quality of life. Risks: automation displacing routine jobs, capital owners benefiting far more than labor (widening inequality — connects to Chapter 05), privacy and surveillance concerns, and a constant need for reskilling as job requirements shift faster than before.

🇵🇭
Why This Chapter Matters Directly for a BSIT Student

The Philippine BPO industry — the same one cited above as a globalization success story — is also one of the sectors most exposed to 4IR-driven automation, as AI chatbots and voice systems take over routine customer service work. Philippine government digital transformation initiatives are also accelerating. This is the most direct link this entire course has to your actual degree program: the skills you're building in BSIT sit right at the center of this shift.

Facilitators of Globalization & Theories of Globalization

Chapter 02 showed WHAT was built. This chapter asks two different questions: WHAT specific forces made all of that possible (facilitators), and WHAT theoretical frameworks scholars use to explain and critique globalization's actual outcomes — particularly whether it benefits everyone equally.

Facilitators of Globalization

A facilitator here means any concrete force, technology, or policy that made deeper global integration physically and practically possible — the "engines" underneath the abstract process.

FacilitatorHow It Enables Globalization
Technological infrastructureThe internet, mobile networks, and satellites make instant global communication and data flow possible
Transportation advancesContainerized shipping and commercial aviation made moving goods and people across the world dramatically cheaper and faster
Multinational corporations & FDIDirect investment spreads production, capital, and jobs across borders (Chapter 02)
International financial institutions & trade agreementsThe IMF, World Bank, WTO, and FTAs provide shared rules and infrastructure that make cross-border trade and finance predictable
Global media & communication networksSpread information, culture, and ideas across borders almost instantly (Chapter 06)
Migration networks & diaspora communitiesMove labor, remittances, and cultural practices across borders, and lower the cost of future migration for others (Chapter 07)
English as a global lingua francaReduces communication friction for international business, science, and diplomacy

Theories of Globalization

Beyond the process-focused Hyperglobalist / Skeptic / Transformationalist debate from Chapter 01, a separate set of theories tries to explain WHO actually benefits from globalization, and why some countries stay poor while others get rich. Your syllabus explicitly asks you to critique the theories that "lend justification" to globalization — these are the ones most commonly targeted.

Modernization Theory
Development framework
"Traditional" → "Modern"
Claims developing societies naturally progress toward becoming industrialized, Western-style "modern" societies by adopting the same technology, institutions, and values today's developed nations already have — following the same path.
Dependency Theory
Critical/structural framework
Core exploits Periphery
Argues developing ("periphery") countries stay poor not from a lack of modernization, but because their economies are structurally dependent on, and exploited by, wealthy "core" countries — a legacy of colonialism perpetuated through trade and debt.
World-Systems Theory
Immanuel Wallerstein
Core–Semi-Periphery–Periphery
The global economy is one interconnected system divided into wealthy industrialized "core" countries, transitional "semi-periphery" countries, and resource-exporting "periphery" countries — a nation's position constrains its development options.
📌
Exam-Critical — The Theory Most Often Criticized

Modernization Theory is the theory most frequently targeted for critique in Philippine Contemporary World courses: critics argue it treats the Western development path as the ONLY legitimate one, ignores how colonialism actively created today's inequality (rather than developing countries simply "not catching up" yet), and can function as an ideological justification for continued Western economic influence dressed up as "helping" developing nations modernize. Dependency Theory and World-Systems Theory were both developed largely as direct responses to this critique.

Economic Globalization vs. Economic Nationalism — Where Does the Philippines Stand?

Your syllabus explicitly asks you to defend a stance on whether the Philippines should embrace economic globalization or economic nationalism — a strong candidate for a position paper or recitation prompt.

Case for Economic GlobalizationCase for Economic Nationalism
BPO jobs and FDI depend on the Philippines staying open to global businessReduces vulnerability to sudden foreign policy shocks, like the 2026 US remittance tax
OFW remittances ($35.6B in 2025) require continued global labor mobilityProtects domestic agriculture and strategic industries from being undercut by cheaper imports

Strongest Answer Pattern

The strongest position papers on this prompt don't pick a pure extreme — they argue for SELECTIVE engagement: staying open where the Philippines has a genuine advantage (BPO, labor migration) while protecting specific vulnerable sectors (agriculture, strategic industries) — essentially a managed, not maximal, version of economic globalization.

WEEK 7–8
CHAPTER 04
The Global Interstate System & Global Governance

If there's no single "world government," how does the world organize itself at all, and how does the international community coordinate on shared problems? This chapter covers both the underlying STATE SYSTEM the whole world operates within, and the GOVERNANCE institutions built on top of it to manage cooperation without erasing state sovereignty.

The Global Interstate System

The interstate system is the foundational structure of modern international relations: a world divided into sovereign, territorially bounded nation-states, each recognized as the supreme legal authority within its own borders. This system traces back to the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which ended a series of European religious wars by establishing that states would not interfere in each other's internal and religious affairs — the origin of the modern concept of sovereignty.

  • Sovereignty — a state holds supreme authority over its own territory and internal affairs
  • Territoriality — states have defined, recognized borders
  • Anarchy (in the IR sense) — there is no central world government ABOVE states, unlike the ordered hierarchy within a single country
  • Self-help — because there's no central authority to appeal to, states ultimately rely on their own power or alliances for security

Two Basic Lenses for Reading State Behavior

Realism
States act on self-interest and power
Conflict and competition are inherent to an anarchic system; security is every state's primary concern; cooperation is possible but always fragile and secondary to self-interest.
Liberalism / Idealism
Cooperation is achievable and beneficial
International institutions, trade, and shared norms create real interdependence that reduces the likelihood of conflict — the theoretical foundation most global governance institutions are built on.

Related concept: balance of power — states and alliances counterbalance each other's strength specifically to prevent any single state from dominating the whole system.

What Is Global Governance?

Global governance refers to the collective ways states, international organizations, and other actors cooperate to manage shared global problems — WITHOUT a central world government holding binding authority over sovereign nations. It relies on treaties, voluntary cooperation, shared norms, and institutions that countries choose to join and can, in principle, choose to leave. In other words: governance is the layer of cooperation states have built ON TOP OF the anarchic interstate system described above, precisely because some problems (pandemics, climate change, trade) can't be solved by any single state alone.

⚠️
The Veto Power Problem — Frequently Tested

The Security Council veto means the UN's most powerful enforcement body can be paralyzed whenever a conflict involves the interests of any ONE of the 5 permanent members. This structural flaw is a standard essay topic: "Is the UN Security Council structure still fit for purpose in today's world?" — there is no single correct answer, but you should be able to explain BOTH the original post-WWII logic for the veto and its modern criticisms.

State Sovereignty vs Global Governance — The Central Tension

📌
Current, Highly Citable 2026 Example

In January 2026, the United States formally withdrew from more than 60 international organizations and declared open opposition to the UN's Sustainable Development Goals and the 2030 Agenda. In 2025, the US voted with the international majority on only about 5% of UN General Assembly resolutions where a vote was recorded — a dramatic outlier. This is a live, real-time demonstration of the sovereignty-vs-global-governance tension described above: a powerful state choosing its own sovereignty and interests over collective multilateral commitments.

International Law — How Does It Actually Get Enforced?

Unlike domestic law, international law has no single police force or automatic enforcement mechanism. It relies primarily on: (1) states voluntarily complying to maintain their reputation and relationships, (2) economic sanctions imposed by other states as a consequence for violations, (3) the ICJ and International Criminal Court (ICC) issuing rulings — though these bodies still depend on state cooperation to actually enforce judgments, since neither has its own police force.

WEEK 10–11
CHAPTER 05
North-South Divide, Global Inequality, Latin America & ASEAN

This chapter opens the final-term stretch by asking the central fairness question of the whole course: has globalization narrowed the gap between rich and poor countries, or widened it? It examines the world's most common regional framing for that gap, plus two regional case studies — Latin America and ASEAN — that illustrate opposite ends of the development spectrum.

The North-South Divide

The Global North and Global South are informal socio-economic groupings, not strict geography — the terms describe wealthier, more industrialized nations (roughly North America, Europe, and developed parts of East Asia and Oceania) versus poorer, developing nations (roughly most of Africa, Latin America, and Asia). The dividing line is commonly traced back to the Brandt Line, a rough boundary drawn near 30°N latitude in the 1980 Brandt Report led by former West German Chancellor Willy Brandt.

⚠️
Known Limitation of the Framework

The North-South split oversimplifies real diversity within each group — wealthy Gulf states technically sit in the geographic "South," while poorer regions exist inside "Northern" countries too. The rise of large emerging Southern economies (China, India, Brazil) has blurred the line further, which is itself a common critique/essay angle.

Two Kinds of Global Inequality

TypeDefinitionTrend
Inequality BETWEEN countriesGaps in average wealth/income comparing entire nations against each otherHas generally NARROWED in recent decades — largely due to rapid growth in China, India, and other emerging economies
Inequality WITHIN countriesGaps between rich and poor citizens inside the same nationHas WIDENED in many countries, including highly globalized economies, where gains from trade and technology often concentrate among already-wealthy groups
📌
The Key Insight Professors Look For

A country can look more "globally equal" on average (between-country gap narrowing) while its OWN citizens experience worsening inequality (within-country gap widening) at the same time. Both things are simultaneously true — this apparent contradiction is a favorite exam and discussion topic precisely because it resists a simple answer.

Regional Case Study — Latin America

Latin America is a frequently cited example of how colonial history shapes long-term economic structure — a direct parallel to the Philippines' own Spanish colonial past (Chapter 02).

  • Colonial legacy: Spanish and Portuguese colonization established extractive, raw-material-export economies that shaped regional development patterns still visible today
  • The 1980s debt crisis ("Lost Decade"): Multiple Latin American countries defaulted on foreign debt, leading to IMF-imposed structural adjustment programs — a real-world illustration of Dependency Theory (Chapter 03) in action
  • Regional integration — Mercosur: the Southern Common Market (Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, Uruguay, and associates), Latin America's rough equivalent of ASEAN or the EU
  • Current relevance: continued reliance on commodity exports and recurring political/economic volatility, alongside a growing voice within broader "Global South" solidarity movements

Regional Case Study — ASEAN

1967
Founded (Bangkok Declaration)
10
Member states, incl. the Philippines
2015
ASEAN Economic Community (AEC) established

ASEAN began with five founding members — Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand — and later grew to ten with the addition of Brunei, Vietnam, Laos, Myanmar, and Cambodia. The ASEAN Economic Community (AEC) aims for the freer movement of goods, services, investment, capital, and skilled labor within the region — ASEAN's answer to the EU's single market, though considerably less integrated (no shared currency, weaker enforcement mechanisms).

🔗
ASEAN as a Microcosm of the North-South Divide

ASEAN's own members span a wide development range internally — Singapore functions almost like a "Northern," highly developed economy, while Myanmar, Cambodia, and Laos remain much less developed. This makes ASEAN a useful smaller-scale illustration of the exact same North-South tension playing out globally, just compressed into one region.

The Philippines has been an ASEAN member since its founding, remains active in regional geopolitics (including the South China Sea / West Philippine Sea disputes), and participates heavily in intra-ASEAN labor migration.

WEEK 12–13
CHAPTER 06
Media Platforms and Globalization

Beyond money and governments, globalization moves IDEAS, VALUES, and CULTURAL PRODUCTS across borders at unprecedented speed — reshaping the political, economic, and social landscape of the "new world order" your syllabus specifically asks about, and reshaping how people everywhere eat, dress, communicate, and see themselves in the process.

Cultural Globalization — Two Competing Fears

ConceptCore Argument
Cultural HomogenizationGlobal media and consumer brands are erasing local cultural differences, producing an increasingly uniform global culture — critics call this "Americanization" or "Westernization" of global taste, values, and consumption habits.
Cultural HybridizationLocal cultures don't passively absorb global influences — they actively blend, remix, and reinterpret them, producing entirely new hybrid cultural forms rather than simple uniformity.

Glocalization

Coined by sociologist Roland Robertson (Ch. 01), glocalization describes how global products and ideas get actively ADAPTED to local contexts rather than simply imposed unchanged. This is the most widely cited concept for explaining what actually happens in cultural globalization, more so than pure homogenization.

🇵🇭
Philippine Examples of Glocalization

McDonald's serving McSpaghetti and rice meals only in the Philippines. Jollibee — a genuinely Filipino brand — expanding internationally while keeping Filipino flavor profiles for diaspora communities abroad. K-pop and Spotify algorithms surfacing OPM (Original Pilipino Music) to global audiences. Each is neither purely "global" nor purely "local" — they're a deliberate blend.

Media Platforms & Global Communication Order

Political: social media enables rapid mobilization and disinformation alike, reshaping elections and protest movements worldwide. Digital Culture & the Filipino Internet Presence

The Philippines is consistently ranked among the world's heaviest social media-using populations by time spent online. This creates a genuinely two-way cultural flow: Filipino content, memes, and trends increasingly influence global online culture, not just the reverse — a meaningful shift from earlier eras where cultural flow was overwhelmingly one-directional (developed world to developing world).

The Demographic Transition Model

The demographic transition model (DTM) describes how a country's birth rate and death rate typically change as it develops economically — and therefore how fast (or slow) its population grows at each stage.

StageBirth RateDeath RatePopulation GrowthTypical Example
1 — High StationaryHighHighLow/stablePre-industrial societies (rare today)
2 — Early ExpandingStays highFalls sharply (better healthcare, sanitation, food)Rapid growthLeast-developed countries, parts of Sub-Saharan Africa
3 — Late ExpandingBegins falling (urbanization, education, family planning access)Continues falling slowlyGrowth slowsMany developing countries, including much of the Philippines' current profile
4 — Low StationaryLowLowStable, slow growthMost developed countries (US, much of Europe)

⚠️
Don't Overstate the Philippines' Exact Stage

The Philippines is generally described as being in late Stage 2 to Stage 3 — death rates are low, but birth rates, while declining, remain moderately elevated relative to fully developed Stage 4 countries. Treat this as a general positioning, not an exact classification, and confirm the specific framing your professor uses in the actual lecture.

Urbanization & Global Cities

More than half the world's population now lives in urban areas, a trend accelerating rapidly in developing countries. Global cities — like New York, London, Tokyo, Singapore, and increasingly Manila — function as command centers for the global economy: hubs for finance, corporate headquarters, media, and international migration, disproportionately influential relative to their physical size.

CHAPTER 08
Globalization Amid the Deadly Pandemic — COVID-19

COVID-19 is the clearest real-world stress test globalization has faced this century. This chapter covers how the pandemic exposed globalization's fragility, and how different countries' responses can inform better preventive policy — directly feeding your required Reflection Paper for this unit.

How COVID-19 Exposed Globalization's Fragility

DisruptionWhat Happened
Supply chain breakdownGlobal supply chains broke down nearly simultaneously worldwide; single-source dependency on specific countries for goods like PPE and semiconductors caused severe shortages, prompting many firms to pursue "reshoring" or "friend-shoring" afterward
Border closures & travel bansNearly universal, sudden halt of international travel and migration proved that even a supposedly "borderless" world can snap back to hard national borders within days — real-time support for the Skeptic/Realist view that state sovereignty remains powerful
Vaccine nationalism vs. vaccine diplomacyWealthy countries pre-purchased and stockpiled vaccine supply ("vaccine nationalism"), while COVAX — a WHO-led global vaccine-sharing initiative — tried to ensure more equitable distribution but fell short of its targets
WHO's role & criticismCoordinated global health information and response, but faced real criticism over the speed and independence of its early actions — a live case study for Chapter 04's debate on whether global governance can actually solve global problems
Digital accelerationRemote work, e-commerce, telemedicine, and online education expanded massively — a form of DIGITAL globalization that deepened even while physical, travel-based globalization froze almost completely

Formulating Preventive Policy — Comparing Country Responses

Your syllabus specifically asks you to formulate preventive policies based on successful approaches used elsewhere. A few comparative patterns worth knowing:

  • Early, aggressive testing and contact tracing — countries like South Korea and Vietnam that acted early with mass testing and tracing generally slowed initial spread more effectively than countries that waited
  • Strong universal healthcare and public health infrastructure — countries with more robust existing health systems generally handled surges with fewer excess deaths
  • Coordinated, clearly communicated public messaging — inconsistent or delayed communication in several countries correlated with lower public compliance and slower containment
🇵🇭
The Philippine COVID-19 Experience

The Philippines implemented some of the world's longest lockdowns, which limited early spread but came with major economic contraction, disrupted education (a direct precursor to the "Online Delivery of Instruction" now used in your own courses), and a comparatively slower initial vaccine rollout pace than several neighboring ASEAN countries. This gives you specific, personal-experience material for the required reflection paper.

📝
Reflection Paper Prep — Questions to Actually Answer

Strong reflection papers on this topic engage directly with: (1) how the pandemic personally or locally affected you and your community, (2) what specifically worked or failed in the Philippine response compared to other countries covered above, and (3) what a better-prepared Philippines or world would concretely look like heading into a future pandemic. Generic "COVID was hard for everyone" reflections score lower than ones grounded in specific comparisons like the table above.

Current 2026 Status — Genuinely Off Track

As of the 2026 Sustainable Development Report, none of the 17 SDGs are on track to be achieved by the 2030 deadline. Roughly 16% of specific targets are progressing well, while another 16% are actively worsening. SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities), 14 (Life Below Water), 15 (Life on Land), and 16 (Peace, Justice, Institutions) are flagged as particularly off-track globally.

Climate Change as a Global Governance Problem

Climate change is the textbook example of a collective action problem: every country benefits from a stable climate, but any single country reducing emissions alone bears real economic costs while other countries "free-ride" on the benefit.

AgreementYearKey Feature
Kyoto Protocol1997First binding emissions targets, but only for developed countries — the US never ratified it
Paris Agreement2015Nearly universal participation; each country sets its OWN voluntary emissions targets ("Nationally Determined Contributions") rather than binding external targets

The Philippines & Climate Vulnerability

The Philippines is consistently ranked among the world's most disaster-vulnerable countries — facing an average of 20+ tropical cyclones annually, alongside earthquakes, volcanic activity, and rising sea levels. This makes the Philippines a frequently cited example of climate injustice: the country contributes a very small share of global greenhouse gas emissions historically, yet bears disproportionately severe consequences.

Global Citizenship

Global citizenship is the idea that, beyond national identity, individuals hold responsibilities toward the wider world — human rights, environmental stewardship, and solidarity with people in other countries — even without any formal global government to enforce these obligations.

The Question This Whole Course Builds Toward

Nearly every final exam or capstone essay in this course asks some version of: "Is globalization good or bad for the Philippines?" The strongest answers reject the binary.
