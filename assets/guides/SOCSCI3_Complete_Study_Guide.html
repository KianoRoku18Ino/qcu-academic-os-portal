CHAPTER 00
Course Overview

The Contemporary World is one of the CHED-mandated General Education courses required across ALL Philippine college programs, regardless of major. It examines globalization — the accelerating interconnection of the world's economies, governments, cultures, and peoples — and asks what that interconnection means for ordinary people, nations, and the Philippines specifically. Unlike RIZAL (one person's biography) or CC 101 (fixed technical facts), this course studies systems that are actively shifting RIGHT NOW — meaning some content here reflects the world as of mid-2026, not settled history.

🌍
Why "Contemporary" Actually Matters Here

This is not a history course. When your professor asks about "the current state of globalization," they want you to reference genuinely recent developments — trade tensions, shifting alliances, new technology — not decade-old textbook examples. This guide includes several 2025–2026 developments specifically because currency is part of the actual subject matter.

CHAPTER 01
Understanding Globalization

Before analyzing globalization's effects, you need a precise working definition and a map of its different dimensions — economists, political scientists, and cultural theorists don't all mean the same thing when they say "globalization."

Working Definition

Globalization is the ongoing process by which economies, societies, cultures, and populations become increasingly interconnected and interdependent through the accelerated flow of trade, capital, people, information, and ideas across national borders. It is NOT a single event with a start date — it is an intensifying, uneven, and contested PROCESS, still actively unfolding and even reversing in some areas today.

The Five Dimensions of Globalization

💰
Economic
Global trade, finance, multinational corporations, supply chains
🏛️
Political

🎭

Shared media, values, lifestyles spreading across borders
💻

🌱

🇵🇭
Filipino Example Touching All Five Dimensions

A single Filipino nurse working in the UK sending money home via GCash touches all five at once: economic (remittance flow), political (bilateral labor agreements between PH and UK), cultural (Filipino traditions carried abroad, British culture absorbed), technological (instant digital money transfer), and ecological (the carbon footprint of international travel itself). This is the kind of multi-dimensional example professors want you to construct on essay exams.

Three Major Theoretical Perspectives on Globalization

Scholars disagree sharply on how significant, new, and beneficial globalization actually is. Three broad camps dominate the academic debate — expect exam questions asking you to identify or apply one of these.

PerspectiveCore ClaimKey Idea
HyperglobalistGlobalization is real, new, and transformative — the nation-state is becoming less relevant as global markets and institutions take overBorders are dissolving; we live in a genuinely "borderless world" economically
SkepticGlobalization is exaggerated — international trade levels today aren't fundamentally different from earlier historical peaks (e.g. pre-WWI trade), and nation-states remain the dominant actorsWhat we call "globalization" is mostly regionalization between blocs (EU, ASEAN, NAFTA/USMCA), not true global integration
TransformationalistGlobalization is real and significant, but its outcomes are unpredictable, uneven, and actively reshape (not eliminate) state powerThe most widely accepted "middle ground" view among contemporary scholars — globalization changes HOW states govern, not WHETHER they govern
📌
Exam-Critical Fact — 2026 Events Actually Support the Skeptic View

The United States formally withdrawing from over 60 international organizations in January 2026 and rejecting the UN's Sustainable Development Goals is a textbook example Skeptics would point to: even the world's most powerful economy can simply choose to disengage from global institutions when it suits national interest — evidence that state sovereignty, not borderless global governance, still drives outcomes. This is a genuinely current, citable example for essay questions on this theory.

Key Theorists to Know

Thomas Friedman

Popularized the hyperglobalist idea that technology has "flattened" competitive playing fields globally, allowing individuals and small firms anywhere to compete internationally.

CHAPTER 02
History & Waves of Globalization

Globalization is not a 21st-century invention — trade networks, migration, and cultural exchange span thousands of years. Historians typically describe globalization as occurring in distinct "waves," each accelerated by a specific technological or political shift.

Timeline — The Major Waves of Globalization

~130 BC
The Silk Road Opens
Trade routes connecting China, Central Asia, the Middle East, and Europe. Carried silk, spices, and technology — but also ideas, religions, and disease, across thousands of kilometers, over centuries.
1400s–1600s
Wave 1 — The Age of Exploration
European maritime powers (Spain, Portugal, later Netherlands, England) connected the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Europe into a single trading system for the first time. This is also the era that led directly to Spanish colonization of the Philippines (1521 onward) — globalization and colonialism were deeply intertwined from this point forward.
1800s–1914
Wave 2 — Industrial-Era Globalization
Steamships, railroads, and the telegraph dramatically cut travel and communication time. Global trade volumes reached levels (relative to GDP) that some economists argue weren't matched again until the late 20th century — a key data point Skeptic theorists cite.
1914–1945
The Great Reversal
Two World Wars and the Great Depression caused a dramatic RETREAT from global trade and integration — proof that globalization is not automatically permanent or one-directional. Countries turned inward (protectionism, tariffs) during this period.
1944–1947
Post-War Institution Building
The Bretton Woods Conference (1944) created the IMF and World Bank. The GATT (General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, precursor to the WTO) was signed in 1947 — deliberately designed to prevent a repeat of the 1930s trade collapse. Covered fully in Chapter 03.
1989–1991
Wave 3 — Post-Cold War Acceleration
Fall of the Berlin Wall (1989) and Soviet collapse (1991) opened former communist economies to global markets. Widely seen as the beginning of the most intense phase of globalization to date.
1995
World Trade Organization founded
Replaced GATT with a permanent, more powerful institution to govern global trade rules and resolve disputes between nations. Covered in Chapter 03.
2001–2010s
Wave 4 — The Digital & Internet Wave
China joins WTO (2001), smartphones and broadband internet spread globally, social media connects billions instantly. Information and culture now move at near-zero marginal cost across borders.
2020–2023
COVID-19 Disruption
Global supply chains broke down simultaneously worldwide, border closures halted international travel and migration, exposing how fragile deep global interdependence actually is. Many countries began actively "reshoring" critical manufacturing afterward.
2025–2026
Wave 5? — Rising Deglobalization Pressures
Renewed tariff disputes, the US withdrawing from dozens of international bodies and rejecting the SDGs (Jan 2026), and new taxes on cross-border remittances (including a 1% US tax on OFW money transfers) signal a possible partial retreat from deep global integration — actively debated by scholars as this guide is being written.
💭
Common Essay Prompt on This Chapter

"Is globalization inevitable, or can it reverse?" The 1914–1945 retreat AND the 2020s deglobalization pressures are your strongest historical evidence that globalization is NOT a one-way, unstoppable process — it has reversed before and shows signs of partial reversal again now. This directly supports Skeptic and Transformationalist positions over the Hyperglobalist view from Chapter 01.

CHAPTER 03
The Global Economy

This chapter covers the institutions and mechanisms that structure how money, goods, and services move across borders — the "hardware" of economic globalization.

The Big Three Post-War Institutions

IMF
Founded 1944, Bretton Woods
International Monetary Fund
Monitors global financial stability, provides emergency loans to countries facing balance-of-payments crises, usually attached to policy conditions ("structural adjustment") the borrowing country must follow.
World Bank
Founded 1944, Bretton Woods
International Bank for Reconstruction & Development
Provides long-term loans and grants for development projects (infrastructure, education, health) in developing countries, distinct from the IMF's short-term crisis focus.
WTO
Founded 1995, Geneva
World Trade Organization
Sets global trade rules, negotiates trade agreements between member states, and provides a formal system for resolving trade disputes between countries.
⚠️
Common Point of Confusion — IMF vs World Bank

Students frequently mix these up. Simple distinction: the IMF is like an emergency room — short-term crisis loans to stabilize a country's finances. The World Bank is like a long-term investor — development loans for building things (roads, schools, power grids) over years or decades.

Multinational Corporations (MNCs)

Companies that operate production, sales, or services across multiple countries simultaneously — among the most powerful economic actors in globalization, sometimes with revenues exceeding the GDP of entire nations.

Argument FOR MNCsArgument AGAINST MNCs
Create jobs and transfer technology/skills to host countriesCan exploit weaker labor and environmental regulations in developing countries
Increase host-country tax revenue and infrastructure investmentProfits often flow back to the home country rather than staying local
Introduce competition, potentially lowering consumer pricesCan undercut and destroy local small businesses unable to compete
Connect local economies to global supply chains and marketsCan create dependency, leaving host economies vulnerable to the MNC's decisions

Global Supply Chains

A modern product is rarely made in one country. A single smartphone might have chip design in the US, semiconductor fabrication in Taiwan, rare-earth mineral mining in the DRC, assembly in China or Vietnam, and final sale worldwide — each step adding value in a different country before the final product reaches a consumer.

🇵🇭
Philippine Example — BPO Industry

The Philippine Business Process Outsourcing (BPO) industry — call centers, back-office processing, IT services — is a direct product of global supply chains extending into SERVICES, not just physical goods. A US company can have its customer service handled entirely from a Manila call center, made possible by internet infrastructure, English proficiency, and labor cost differences — pure economic globalization in action, employing over a million Filipinos.

Free Trade vs Protectionism

ApproachCore IdeaTools Used
Free TradeMinimal government interference in cross-border trade; let comparative advantage determine what each country producesTrade agreements (FTAs), tariff reduction, open borders for goods/capital
ProtectionismShield domestic industries from foreign competition to protect local jobs and strategic sectorsTariffs (import taxes), import quotas, subsidies for domestic producers
📌
Current 2025–2026 Example — Rising Protectionism

Renewed tariff disputes between major economies through 2025–2026, alongside the new 1% US tax specifically targeting outbound remittances (directly affecting OFW families back home), are live examples of protectionist tools being actively deployed right now — useful, citable evidence for any essay asking you to identify CURRENT protectionist trends rather than historical ones.

Regional Trade Blocs

BlocRegionPurpose

EUEurope (27 members)Deepest regional integration globally — shared currency (Euro) for most members, common market, free movement of people

🔗
Connects to Chapter 01 — The Skeptic Argument

Regional blocs like ASEAN and the EU are exactly what Skeptic theorists point to as evidence that "globalization" is often really just REGIONALIZATION — deeper integration within a bloc of neighboring countries, rather than true borderless global integration.

CHAPTER 04
Global Governance

If there's no single "world government," how does the international community coordinate on shared problems at all? This chapter covers the institutions, and the fundamental tension, at the heart of global governance: no country wants to give up its sovereignty, yet many problems (pandemics, climate change, trade) cannot be solved by any single country alone.

What Is Global Governance?

Global governance refers to the collective ways states, international organizations, and other actors cooperate to manage shared global problems — WITHOUT a central world government holding binding authority over sovereign nations. It relies on treaties, voluntary cooperation, shared norms, and institutions that countries choose to join and can, in principle, choose to leave.

The United Nations System

193

Main UN Bodies

BodyFunction
General AssemblyAll 193 member states, one vote each — the main deliberative body, votes on resolutions (mostly non-binding recommendations)
Security Council15 members (5 permanent: US, UK, France, Russia, China + 10 rotating). Only body that can authorize binding, enforceable action, including military intervention. The 5 permanent members hold VETO power — any one can block a resolution regardless of how the other 14 vote.
SecretariatAdministrative arm, headed by the Secretary-General, carries out the day-to-day work and programs of the UN
International Court of Justice (ICJ)Settles legal disputes BETWEEN states (not individuals), based in The Hague, Netherlands

⚠️
The Veto Power Problem — Frequently Tested

The Security Council veto means the UN's most powerful enforcement body can be paralyzed whenever a conflict involves the interests of any ONE of the 5 permanent members. This structural flaw is a standard essay topic: "Is the UN Security Council structure still fit for purpose in today's world?" — there is no single correct answer, but you should be able to explain BOTH the original post-WWII logic for the veto and its modern criticisms.

State Sovereignty vs Global Governance — The Central Tension

Sovereignty is a state's supreme authority over its own territory and internal affairs, free from outside interference — a principle enshrined in the UN Charter itself. Yet solving genuinely global problems (pandemics, climate change, financial crises, terrorism) often requires countries to accept SOME external rules or coordination. This creates constant friction: how much sovereignty should a country give up for the sake of collective global problem-solving?

📌
Current, Highly Citable 2026 Example

In January 2026, the United States formally withdrew from more than 60 international organizations and declared open opposition to the UN's Sustainable Development Goals and the 2030 Agenda. In 2025, the US voted with the international majority on only about 5% of UN General Assembly resolutions where a vote was recorded — a dramatic outlier. This is a live, real-time demonstration of the sovereignty-vs-global-governance tension: a powerful state choosing to prioritize its own sovereignty and interests over collective multilateral commitments, regardless of how the rest of the world votes.

Other Major International Organizations

International Law — How Does It Actually Get Enforced?

Unlike domestic law, international law has no single police force or automatic enforcement mechanism. It relies primarily on: (1) states voluntarily complying to maintain their reputation and relationships, (2) economic sanctions imposed by other states as a consequence for violations, (3) the ICJ and International Criminal Court (ICC) issuing rulings — though these bodies still depend on state cooperation to actually enforce judgments, since neither has its own police force.

🔗
Forward Link — Chapter 08

The sovereignty-vs-governance tension explored here becomes the foundation for Chapter 08's discussion of global citizenship: if state sovereignty limits what global institutions can actually enforce, what responsibility do INDIVIDUALS have in addressing problems states won't fully cooperate on?

The Philippines is consistently the world's 4th-largest remittance recipient, after India, Mexico, and China — and the world's largest single source of seafarers and among the top sources of nurses globally. This isn't incidental: it reflects decades of deliberate Philippine government labor export policy (dating to the 1970s) alongside genuine global demand for Filipino healthcare, maritime, and domestic workers.

📌
Current 2026 Development Worth Knowing

A new 1% US tax on outbound cash remittances took effect in 2026, specifically affecting money OFWs in America send home. Economists tracked a surge in remittances in December 2025 as OFWs rushed to transfer funds before the tax began — a real, current illustration of how a single policy change in one country can immediately ripple into millions of Filipino households, and a strong example of protectionism (Ch. 03) intersecting directly with migration.

Urbanization & Global Cities

More than half the world's population now lives in urban areas, a trend accelerating rapidly in developing countries. Global cities — like New York, London, Tokyo, Singapore, and increasingly Manila — function as command centers for the global economy: hubs for finance, corporate headquarters, media, and international migration, disproportionately influential relative to their physical size.

🇵🇭
Metro Manila as a Global City

Metro Manila (including Quezon City, where QCU sits) functions as the Philippines' primary global city node — hosting the BPO industry's headquarters, major international corporate offices, embassies, and serving as the departure/return point for the majority of the country's 2+ million OFWs.

CHAPTER 06
Global Culture & Media

Beyond money and governments, globalization moves IDEAS, VALUES, and CULTURAL PRODUCTS across borders at unprecedented speed — reshaping how people everywhere eat, dress, communicate, and see themselves.

Cultural Globalization — Two Competing Fears

ConceptCore Argument
Cultural HomogenizationGlobal media and consumer brands are erasing local cultural differences, producing an increasingly uniform global culture — critics call this "Americanization" or "Westernization" of global taste, values, and consumption habits.
Cultural HybridizationLocal cultures don't passively absorb global influences — they actively blend, remix, and reinterpret them, producing entirely new hybrid cultural forms rather than simple uniformity.

Glocalization

Coined by sociologist Roland Robertson (Ch. 01), glocalization describes how global products and ideas get actively ADAPTED to local contexts rather than simply imposed unchanged. This is the most widely cited concept for explaining what actually happens in cultural globalization, more so than pure homogenization.

🇵🇭
Philippine Examples of Glocalization

McDonald's serving McSpaghetti and rice meals only in the Philippines. Jollibee — a genuinely Filipino brand — expanding internationally while keeping Filipino flavor profiles for diaspora communities abroad. K-pop and Spotify algorithms surfacing OPM (Original Pilipino Music) to global audiences. Each is neither purely "global" nor purely "local" — they're a deliberate blend.

Media Flows & Global Communication

EraDominant MediaCharacteristic
Pre-1990sPrint, radio, broadcast TVLargely one-directional (national broadcasters to national audiences), limited cross-border flow
1990s–2000sCable TV, early internet24-hour global news networks (CNN), beginning of real-time cross-border information flow
2010s–presentSocial media, streaming platformsInstant, many-to-many global communication; ordinary individuals, not just broadcasters, now shape global cultural flows (viral trends, influencer culture)

Digital Culture & the Filipino Internet Presence

The Philippines is consistently ranked among the world's heaviest social media-using populations by time spent online. This creates a genuinely two-way cultural flow: Filipino content, memes, and trends increasingly influence global online culture, not just the reverse — a meaningful shift from earlier eras where cultural flow was overwhelmingly one-directional (developed world to developing world).

Language & Globalization

TrendDetail
English as global lingua francaDominant language of international business, science, aviation, and the internet — a direct legacy of British and American global influence historically
Language endangermentUNESCO estimates a significant share of the world's roughly 7,000 languages are at risk of disappearing this century, partly due to globalization pressure favoring dominant languages
Digital code-switchingTaglish (Tagalog-English mixing) itself is a everyday linguistic product of globalization — reflecting the Philippines' colonial history and ongoing global integration simultaneously
💭
Common Reflection Essay Prompt

"Is globalization erasing Filipino culture, or strengthening it?" Strong answers avoid a simple yes/no — they apply glocalization directly: Filipino culture is simultaneously ABSORBING global influences (K-pop, US media, global fast food) AND actively EXPORTING itself globally (OFW diaspora spreading Filipino food/language worldwide, OPM gaining international streaming traction). Both directions are happening at once.

Current 2026 Status — Genuinely Off Track

As of the 2026 Sustainable Development Report, none of the 17 SDGs are on track to be achieved by the 2030 deadline. Roughly 16% of specific targets are progressing well, while another 16% are actively worsening. SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities), 14 (Life Below Water), 15 (Life on Land), and 16 (Peace, Justice, Institutions) are flagged as particularly off-track globally. This is a genuinely current, citable statistic — far more accurate than assuming steady linear progress toward 2030.

Climate Change as a Global Governance Problem

Climate change is the textbook example of a collective action problem: every country benefits from a stable climate, but any single country reducing emissions alone bears real economic costs while other countries "free-ride" on the benefit. This structural tension explains why international climate agreements are notoriously difficult to negotiate and enforce.

AgreementYearKey Feature
Kyoto Protocol1997First binding emissions targets, but only for developed countries — the US never ratified it
Paris Agreement2015Nearly universal participation; each country sets its OWN voluntary emissions targets ("Nationally Determined Contributions") rather than binding external targets — a deliberate design compromise to secure broader participation, at the cost of weaker enforcement

The Philippines & Climate Vulnerability

The Philippines is consistently ranked among the world's most disaster-vulnerable countries — facing an average of 20+ tropical cyclones annually, alongside earthquakes, volcanic activity, and rising sea levels threatening its thousands of islands. This makes the Philippines a frequently cited example of climate injustice: the country contributes a very small share of global greenhouse gas emissions historically, yet bears disproportionately severe consequences from a problem largely caused by heavily industrialized nations.

📌
Exam-Critical Concept — Climate Justice

"Climate justice" argues that developed nations, which industrialized earlier and emitted the most historical greenhouse gases, bear greater responsibility for funding climate adaptation and mitigation in vulnerable developing nations like the Philippines — a frequently debated principle in international climate negotiations and a strong topic for position papers in this course.

CHAPTER 08
Global Inequality, Citizenship & the Philippines in Context

This closing chapter ties every previous chapter together: does globalization reduce inequality between and within countries, or worsen it? And once you understand these global systems, what does it actually mean to act as a responsible "global citizen"? This is the chapter most likely to appear as a comprehensive final essay prompt.

Two Kinds of Global Inequality

TypeDefinitionTrend
Inequality BETWEEN countriesGaps in average wealth/income comparing entire nations against each otherHas generally NARROWED in recent decades — largely due to rapid growth in China, India, and other emerging economies
Inequality WITHIN countriesGaps between rich and poor citizens inside the same nationHas WIDENED in many countries, including highly globalized economies, where gains from trade and technology often concentrate among already-wealthy groups
📌
The Key Insight Professors Look For

A country can look more "globally equal" on average (between-country gap narrowing) while its OWN citizens experience worsening inequality (within-country gap widening) at the same time. Both things are simultaneously true — this apparent contradiction is a favorite exam and discussion topic precisely because it resists a simple answer.

Conflict & Globalization

Globalization doesn't eliminate conflict — it changes its shape. Modern conflicts increasingly involve: cyberattacks crossing borders instantly, economic sanctions as a substitute for military action, competition over globally-traded resources, and non-state actors (terrorist networks, criminal organizations) that exploit the same global mobility and communication tools that enable legitimate trade and travel.

Global Citizenship

Global citizenship is the idea that, beyond national identity, individuals hold responsibilities toward the wider world — human rights, environmental stewardship, and solidarity with people in other countries — even without any formal global government to enforce these obligations.

DimensionWhat It Looks Like in Practice
AwarenessUnderstanding how local choices connect to global systems (e.g. consumer choices affecting global supply chains)
ResponsibilityRecognizing ethical obligations that extend beyond one's own national borders
ParticipationEngaging with global issues through advocacy, informed voting, or direct civic action

The Question This Whole Course Builds Toward

Nearly every final exam or capstone essay in this course asks some version of: "Is globalization good or bad for the Philippines?" The strongest answers reject the binary. Globalization has brought the Philippines real economic lifelines (remittances, BPO jobs) while also exposing it to real vulnerabilities (climate injustice, dependency on volatile foreign policy decisions like the 2026 remittance tax, brain drain). A synthesis answer — weighing BOTH the benefits and the costs with specific, current evidence from this guide — will consistently outperform a one-sided answer.
